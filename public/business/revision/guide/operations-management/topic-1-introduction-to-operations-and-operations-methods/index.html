Success criterion 1

The role of operations management

Operations management is the process of turning inputs into outputs (goods and services) efficiently and to the right quality. It covers production methods, location, quality, stock and supply chain management, capacity and productivity. Operations must work closely with marketing (what customers want), finance (budgets) and HR (skilled workers).

Success criterion 2

Operations and sustainability

DimensionMeaning for operations
Economic sustainabilityOperating profitably in the long term; efficient use of resources
Social sustainabilityFair treatment of workers and communities; safe conditions; ethical supply chains
Environmental sustainabilityReducing pollution, waste, energy and water use; renewable energy; recycling and circular design

Sustainable operations can cut costs (less energy and waste), improve brand image and meet regulations, but may require costly investment. This links to the key concept of sustainability.

Success criterion 3

Operations methods

MethodMeaningExamplesEvaluation
Job productionOne-off, unique products made to the customer’s specificationWedding dresses, bridges, bespoke furnitureHigh quality, customised, motivating work; but high unit cost, slow, skilled labour
Batch productionGroups of identical products made together; each batch passes through each stageBakeries, clothing, paintSome variety and economies of scale; but downtime between batches, storage of WIP
Mass/flow productionContinuous production of large volumes of identical products on a production lineCars, soft drinks, smartphonesVery low unit costs, automation; but high set-up cost, inflexible, repetitive work
Mass customisationMass production with flexible technology to tailor products to individual customersPersonalised trainers, configured laptopsChoice plus scale; but complex systems and higher costs than pure mass production

The appropriate method depends on the size of the market, the type of product, customer demand for personalisation, available technology and finance, and the skills of the workforce.

Key terms

Operations management
Managing the process of turning inputs into outputs efficiently.
Job production
Making one-off products to individual specifications.
Batch production
Making groups of identical products together.
Mass/flow production
Continuous production of large quantities of standardised products.
Mass customisation
Using flexible technology to produce personalised products at scale.
Sustainability
Meeting present needs without harming future generations: economically, socially and environmentally.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a furniture maker should move from job production to batch production. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define both methods.
  • Batch: lower unit cost, faster, economies of scale v loss of uniqueness, downtime, storage.
  • Impact on customers, workers (deskilling), brand; conclusion.

Model answer: Moving to batch production would let the firm produce standard designs in groups, lowering unit costs and serving more customers. However, customers who value unique, handmade furniture may be lost, skilled craftsmen may feel deskilled, and the business would need storage and to manage downtime between batches. A mixed approach, batch production for a standard range plus a premium made-to-order service, may protect the brand while improving efficiency.

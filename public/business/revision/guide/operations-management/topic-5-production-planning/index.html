Success criterion 3

Stock control charts

Reading the chartMaximum stock 8 500 units, buffer stock 2 200 units: reorder quantity = 8 500 − 2 200 = 6 300 units. If usage is 1 000 units a week and the lead time is 2 weeks, the reorder level = buffer stock + (usage × lead time) = 2 200 + 2 000 = 4 200 units.
Success criterion 4

Capacity utilisation and productivity

Formulas

Capacity utilisation rate = actual output ÷ productive capacity × 100. A hotel with 200 rooms and 150 occupied: 75%.

Defect rate = number of defective units ÷ total output × 100.

Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (or hours worked). Capital productivity = total output ÷ capital input (e.g. machine hours). Productivity rate = (output ÷ input) × 100.

High capacity utilisation spreads fixed costs and lowers unit costs, but leaves no room for extra orders or maintenance and can overwork staff. Low utilisation means idle resources and high unit costs. Ways to improve: increase sales (promotion), rent out spare capacity, or reduce capacity (rationalisation).

Success criterion 5

HLOperating leverage; cost to buy and cost to make

Operating leverage is the extent to which a firm’s costs are fixed rather than variable. High operating leverage (high fixed costs) means profits rise sharply when sales rise above break-even, but fall sharply when sales fall.

HLMake or buy?

Cost to make (CTM) = fixed costs + (average variable cost × quantity). Cost to buy (CTB) = price per unit charged by the supplier × quantity.

Example: 10 000 units; FC $20 000, AVC $3 ⇒ CTM = $50 000. Supplier price $4.50 ⇒ CTB = $45 000. Buying is cheaper by $5 000, but the firm should also consider quality, reliability, control and the effect on its own staff.

Key terms

Supply chain
The stages and organisations through which a product passes to reach the customer.
Just-in-case
Holding buffer stock in case of unexpected demand or supply problems.
Buffer stock
The minimum stock level held as a safety margin.
Lead time
The time between placing an order and receiving it.
Reorder level
The stock level at which a new order is placed.
Capacity utilisation
Actual output as a percentage of maximum possible output.
Labour productivity
Output per worker or per hour worked.
Defect rate
The percentage of output that is faulty.
Operating leverage
HL: the proportion of a firm’s costs that are fixed.
Cost to make / cost to buy
HL: comparing the cost of producing an item with the cost of buying it in.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a supermarket chain should use just-in-time stock control. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define JIT and JIC.
  • JIT: lower storage cost, fresher stock, less waste v risk of stock-outs, supplier reliability, disruption.
  • Perishable goods v non-perishables; conclusion.

Model answer: JIT suits fresh food because it reduces waste and storage costs and keeps products fresh. However, supermarkets face sudden changes in demand, such as panic buying, and depend on long supply chains that can be disrupted, as seen during the pandemic. A mix of JIT for perishable items and buffer stock (JIC) for essential long-life goods is likely to be the best approach.

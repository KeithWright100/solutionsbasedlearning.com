2. HL: Lean production and quality management

Success criterion 1

HLCradle-to-cradle and lean production

Cradle-to-cradle (C2C) design creates products whose materials can be fully recycled or safely returned to nature at the end of their life, so there is no waste (a circular rather than linear model).

Lean production aims to produce more with fewer resources by eliminating waste: overproduction, waiting time, unnecessary transport and movement, excess stock, defects and over-processing. Benefits: lower costs, faster production, better quality and less environmental impact.

Success criterion 2

HLMethods of lean production

MethodMeaningEvaluation
Kaizen (continuous improvement)Small, ongoing improvements suggested by all employeesMotivating; cumulative gains; but slow and needs a supportive culture
Just-in-time (JIT)Inputs arrive exactly when needed; little or no stock heldLower storage costs and waste; but relies on reliable suppliers
KanbanA visual signal (card or board) that triggers the next production or deliveryControls flow and avoids overproduction
AndonA signal (light or cord) that any worker can use to stop the line when there is a problemProblems fixed at source; empowers workers

Real-world example: Toyota developed the Toyota Production System, combining JIT, kanban, andon and kaizen; it became the model for lean production worldwide.

Success criterion 3

HLQuality control and quality assurance

Quality control checks products at the end of (or during) production to find defects; defects are rejected or reworked. It is the responsibility of inspectors, and is costly because waste has already been created.

Quality assurance builds quality into every stage of the process, so defects are prevented; every employee is responsible. It reduces waste and costs in the long run, but needs training and a quality culture.

Success criterion 4

HLQuality management methods and standards

  • Quality circles: small groups of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems.
  • Benchmarking: comparing processes and performance with the best in the industry (or other industries) to set targets.
  • Total quality management (TQM): a culture in which every employee is committed to quality and customer satisfaction at every stage.

Quality standards such as the international ISO 9000 series (quality management) and ISO 14000 (environmental management), or national marks, show customers that a business meets recognised standards. They improve reputation and access to some markets, but involve cost, paperwork and audits.

Key terms

Lean production
HL: producing with minimum waste of resources.
Kaizen
HL: continuous improvement through small, frequent changes.
Just-in-time
HL: receiving inputs only when they are needed, holding minimal stock.
Kanban
HL: a visual signal system that controls production flow.
Andon
HL: a signal that lets workers stop production when there is a problem.
Quality assurance
HL: preventing defects by building quality into every stage.
Total quality management
HL: an organisation-wide commitment to quality.
Benchmarking
HL: comparing performance with the best in the industry.
Cradle-to-cradle
HL: designing products so all materials can be reused or recycled.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a manufacturer should introduce total quality management (TQM). (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define TQM, QA v QC.
  • Benefits: fewer defects, lower waste, reputation, motivation.
  • Costs: training, time, culture change, resistance; conclusion.

Model answer: TQM makes every employee responsible for quality, so defects are prevented rather than found at the end, reducing waste and rework and improving customer satisfaction and reputation. However, introducing TQM needs heavy training, time and a change of culture, and staff may resist extra responsibility. If the manufacturer competes on quality and has high defect rates, the long-term benefits are likely to outweigh the costs.

3. Profitability and liquidity ratios (HL: efficiency and gearing)

Success criterion 1

Profitability ratios

RatioFormulaExample (from the accounts in Topic 2)
Gross profit margin (GPM)Gross profit ÷ sales revenue × 100300 ÷ 800 = 37.5%
Profit margin (PM)Profit before interest and tax ÷ sales revenue × 100120 ÷ 800 = 15%
Return on capital employed (ROCE)Profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed × 100 (capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity)120 ÷ (300 + 720) = 11.8%

A higher margin or ROCE is better. Compare with previous years, competitors and the interest rate (ROCE should beat what savings would earn). To improve: raise prices (if demand is price inelastic), cut cost of sales (cheaper suppliers, less waste), cut overheads, sell more high-margin products.

Success criterion 2

Liquidity ratios

RatioFormulaExampleGuideline
Current ratioCurrent assets ÷ current liabilities270 ÷ 150 = 1.8 : 1About 1.5–2 : 1 is often seen as healthy
Acid-test (quick) ratio(Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities150 ÷ 150 = 1 : 1About 1 : 1 is often seen as healthy

Liquidity is the ability to pay short-term debts. Too low: risk of not paying creditors. Too high: idle resources (too much cash or stock that could be invested). To improve: sell unused assets, use long-term finance instead of overdrafts, reduce stock, chase debtors, sale and leaseback.

Exam tip: Always show the formula, workings and units (%, : 1 or days), then comment on what the result means for the business.

Success criterion 3

Strategies and context

Ratios mean little on their own: they must be compared over time, with competitors and with industry norms. A supermarket can operate safely with a current ratio below 1 because it sells stock for cash quickly and pays suppliers later.

Success criterion 4

HLEfficiency and gearing ratios

RatioFormulaExampleInterpretation
Stock (inventory) turnoverCost of sales ÷ average stock (times per year), or average stock ÷ cost of sales × 365 (days)500 ÷ 120 = 4.2 timesHigher (faster) is usually better; depends on the product
Debtor daysDebtors ÷ total sales revenue × 36590 ÷ 800 × 365 = 41 daysLower is better: cash collected faster
Creditor daysCreditors ÷ cost of sales × 365110 ÷ 500 × 365 = 80 daysHigher helps cash flow, but too high harms supplier relations
Gearing ratioLoan capital ÷ capital employed × 100300 ÷ 1 020 = 29%Above 50% is high-geared: more risk if interest rates rise

HLTo improve: stock turnover — just-in-time, cut slow-moving lines; debtor days — shorter credit terms, discounts for early payment, debt factoring; creditor days — negotiate longer credit; gearing — issue shares, use retained profit, repay loans.

Success criterion 5

HLLimitations of ratio analysis

  • Based on historical data, which may not predict the future.
  • Accounts may be “window dressed” to look better.
  • Different accounting policies make comparisons difficult.
  • Ignores qualitative factors (staff morale, brand, market changes).
  • Inflation distorts comparisons over time.
  • A single ratio gives an incomplete picture.

Key terms

Gross profit margin
Gross profit as a percentage of sales revenue.
Profit margin
Profit before interest and tax as a percentage of sales revenue.
ROCE
Profit before interest and tax as a percentage of capital employed.
Liquidity
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts.
Current ratio
Current assets divided by current liabilities.
Acid-test ratio
Current assets minus inventory, divided by current liabilities.
Debtor days
HL: the average number of days customers take to pay.
Creditor days
HL: the average number of days the business takes to pay suppliers.
Gearing ratio
HL: long-term loans as a percentage of capital employed.
Exam-style question

Using ratio analysis, discuss whether a retailer should cut its prices to increase sales. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Effect on GPM and PM; PED; volume v margin.
  • Effect on liquidity and stock turnover (HL).
  • Conclusion using data where available.

Model answer: A price cut lowers the gross profit margin, so unless sales volume rises enough (price elastic demand), gross profit will fall. Higher volume could improve stock turnover and cash inflows, helping liquidity. However, competitors may match the cut, and cheaper prices could harm the brand. The decision depends on price elasticity and how much margin the retailer can afford to sacrifice.

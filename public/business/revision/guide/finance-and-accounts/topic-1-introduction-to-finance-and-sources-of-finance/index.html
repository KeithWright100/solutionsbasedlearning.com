1. Introduction to finance and sources of finance

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 3: Finance and accounts · Topics · Topic 1 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand the difference between capital and revenue expenditure and to evaluate internal and external sources of finance.

Syllabus Statement

3.1 Introduction to finance: capital expenditure and revenue expenditure. 3.2 Sources of finance: internal sources (personal funds, retained profit, sale of assets); external sources (share capital, loan capital, overdrafts, trade credit, crowdfunding, leasing, microfinance providers, business angels); short-term and long-term finance; the appropriateness, advantages and disadvantages of each source.

Success criterion 1

Capital and revenue expenditure

Capital expenditure is spending on fixed (non-current) assets that will be used for more than one year, such as buildings, machinery and vehicles. Revenue expenditure is spending on day-to-day running costs, such as wages, rent, utilities and raw materials. Capital expenditure appears on the balance sheet; revenue expenditure is an expense on the statement of profit or loss.

Success criterion 4

Choosing the right source

Short-term finance (under one year: overdrafts, trade credit) should fund short-term needs such as working capital. Long-term finance (share capital, long-term loans, retained profit) should fund capital expenditure. Matching the source to the use avoids cash flow problems.

  • Purpose and amount of finance needed.
  • Legal form (only companies can sell shares; only plcs to the public).
  • Cost (interest, dividends, fees).
  • Existing gearing (debt levels) and creditworthiness.
  • Owners’ attitude to control and risk.
  • The state of the economy and interest rates.

Exam tip: In a recommendation, always justify why the source suits this business: its legal form, size, purpose and current debt.

Key terms

Capital expenditure
Spending on fixed assets used for more than a year.
Revenue expenditure
Spending on day-to-day running costs.
Retained profit
Profit kept in the business after tax and dividends.
Share capital
Finance raised by selling shares in a company.
Loan capital
Money borrowed and repaid with interest over time.
Overdraft
A facility to withdraw more money than is in a bank account, up to a limit.
Trade credit
Buying goods now and paying the supplier later.
Crowdfunding
Raising finance from a large number of people, usually online.
Leasing
Renting an asset instead of buying it.
Business angel
A wealthy individual who invests in small businesses in return for a share of ownership.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a public limited company should finance a major expansion using share capital or loan capital. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Compare control, cost, risk, gearing, repayment.
  • Consider current gearing, interest rates and share price.
  • Justified recommendation.

Model answer: Issuing shares raises a large amount without interest or repayment, which suits a long-term, risky expansion, but dilutes ownership and may lower earnings per share. A loan keeps control with existing shareholders and interest is tax-deductible, but raises gearing and interest costs, which is risky if rates rise or sales fall. If the company already has high gearing, share capital is safer; if gearing is low and rates are low, a loan may be cheaper.

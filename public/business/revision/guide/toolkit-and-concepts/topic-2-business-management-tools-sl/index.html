2. Business management toolkit: SL and HL tools

Learning Intention

To understand and apply the toolkit used by all students: SWOT, STEEPLE, Ansoff, the BCG matrix, business plans, descriptive statistics and circular business models.

Syllabus Statement

Business management toolkit (SL and HL): SWOT analysis; Ansoff matrix; STEEPLE analysis; Boston Consulting Group (BCG) matrix; business plan; descriptive statistics; circular business models.

Success criterion 1

SWOT and STEEPLE

Exam tip: STEEPLE factors feed the opportunities and threats in a SWOT. A good SWOT is specific to the business in the case and leads to a recommendation.

Success criterion 2

Ansoff matrix

Success criterion 3

BCG matrix

The Boston Consulting Group matrix (see Unit 4, the seven Ps) classifies products as stars, question marks, cash cows and dogs according to market growth and relative market share. It helps balance a product portfolio and decide where to invest. Use it alongside the product life cycle.

Success criterion 4

Business plans and descriptive statistics

A business plan describes the business idea and how it will be achieved: executive summary, objectives, market research and the target market, the marketing mix, operations, human resources, financial forecasts (cash flow, break-even, profit) and risks. It is used to obtain finance and to guide and monitor the business.

Descriptive statistics summarise data: the mean, median and mode; the range; and charts such as bar charts, pie charts, line graphs and infographics. They make data easy to interpret, but averages can hide variation and outliers.

ExampleWeekly sales of 12, 15, 15, 18 and 40 units: mean = 100 ÷ 5 = 20; median = 15; mode = 15; range = 28. The outlier (40) pulls the mean above most weeks, so the median is the better “typical” value here.
Success criterion 5

Circular business models

A circular business model keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible, replacing the linear “take, make, dispose” model. Types include: circular supply (renewable or recycled inputs), resource recovery (recycling waste into new products), product life extension (repair, refurbish, resell), sharing platforms (renting or sharing underused assets) and product as a service (customers pay for use, the firm keeps ownership and responsibility).

Real-world example: Patagonia’s Worn Wear programme repairs and resells used clothing, extending product life and supporting its environmental brand.

Key terms

SWOT analysis
An audit of internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.
STEEPLE analysis
An analysis of social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal and ethical external factors.
Ansoff matrix
A tool showing four growth strategies by product and market.
BCG matrix
A tool that classifies products by market share and market growth.
Business plan
A document setting out a business’s objectives and how it will achieve them.
Descriptive statistics
Measures and charts that summarise data, such as the mean, median and mode.
Circular business model
A model that keeps resources in use through reuse, repair, sharing and recycling.
Exam-style question

Using the Ansoff matrix, discuss whether a coffee chain should grow through market development or product development. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Explain both quadrants and their risks.
  • Apply to the coffee chain: new countries or segments v new products (food, drinks, merchandise).
  • Justified recommendation.

Model answer: Market development, such as opening in a new country, uses the chain’s proven products and brand but faces unfamiliar customers, culture and competitors. Product development, such as adding a food range in existing stores, builds on its customer base and locations but needs new skills and suppliers. Both are riskier than market penetration. If home markets are saturated, market development may offer greater growth; if stores have spare capacity outside the morning rush, product development may be cheaper and less risky.

1. Introduction to marketing and marketing planning

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 4: Marketing · Topics · Topic 1 of 5

Learning Intention

To understand market orientation, market share and growth, and how firms plan their marketing through segmentation, targeting and positioning.

Syllabus Statement

4.1 Introduction to marketing: marketing and its relationship with other business functions; market orientation and product orientation; market share; the importance of market share and market leadership; market growth. 4.2 Marketing planning: the role of marketing planning; segmentation, targeting and positioning; target markets and market segments (niche and mass markets); consumer profiles; product position maps; unique selling point/proposition; differentiation.

Success criterion 1

Marketing and orientation

Marketing is the management process of identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs and wants profitably. It links closely with operations (what can be produced), finance (budgets and pricing) and HR (staff to deliver the service).

A market-oriented business researches customer needs first and develops products to meet them (most consumer goods firms). A product-oriented business focuses on making the best product it can and then looks for customers (some technology, luxury and engineering firms). Market orientation reduces the risk of failure but can be expensive and may limit radical innovation.

Success criterion 2

Market share and market growth

Formulas

Market share = firm’s sales ÷ total market sales × 100. Example: sales of $45m in a $300m market = 15%.

Market growth = (market size this year − market size last year) ÷ market size last year × 100. Example: a market growing from $300m to $330m has grown by 10%.

Market leadership (the largest share) brings economies of scale, brand recognition, bargaining power with retailers and suppliers, and the ability to set prices. A firm’s sales can rise while its market share falls if the market is growing faster.

Success criterion 3

The marketing plan

A marketing plan sets out the firm’s marketing objectives, research findings, target market, the marketing mix (the 7 Ps), the budget, and how results will be measured. It gives direction, coordinates activities and helps secure finance, but it takes time, is based on forecasts and must be revised as the market changes.

Success criterion 4

Segmentation and targeting

Market segmentation divides a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics: demographic (age, gender, income, occupation, religion, family size), geographic (region, climate, urban or rural) and psychographic (lifestyle, values, personality, attitudes). Targeting chooses which segments to serve; a consumer profile describes the typical customer in a segment.

Success criterion 5

Positioning, USP and differentiation

A unique selling point (USP) is a feature that makes a product different from and better than rivals’ in customers’ eyes (e.g. Dyson’s bagless cyclone technology). Differentiation is how a business makes its products stand out through quality, design, branding, service or features. It reduces price competition and builds loyalty.

Key terms

Marketing
Identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs profitably.
Market orientation
Developing products based on customer needs identified through research.
Product orientation
Focusing on making the product, then finding customers for it.
Market share
A firm’s sales as a percentage of total market sales.
Market segmentation
Dividing a market into groups of similar customers.
Niche market
A small, specialised segment of a market.
Mass market
A large market with customers who have similar needs.
Consumer profile
A description of the characteristics of the typical customer.
USP
A feature that makes a product stand out from competitors.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a small clothing company should target a niche market rather than a mass market. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define niche and mass markets; segmentation.
  • Niche: less competition, premium prices, loyalty v small size, risk. Mass: scale v competition with big brands.
  • Conclusion for a small firm with limited resources.

Model answer: A small company cannot match the low costs of large fashion brands in the mass market, so a niche, such as sustainable sportswear, lets it charge higher prices and build loyal customers with less competition. However, niche markets are small, so growth is limited, and a large competitor could enter if the niche becomes profitable. For a firm with limited resources, a niche strategy is likely to be the better starting point.

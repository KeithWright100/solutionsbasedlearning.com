1. What is a business? Sectors, entrepreneurship and types of business entity

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 1: Introduction to business management · Topics · Topic 1 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand what businesses do, how they are classified, the role of entrepreneurs, and the features of different types of business entity.

Syllabus Statement

1.1 What is a business? The nature of business; primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors; entrepreneurship; challenges and opportunities for starting a business. 1.2 Types of business entities: private and public sectors; sole traders, partnerships, privately held and publicly held companies; for-profit social enterprises (private and public sector companies, cooperatives); non-profit social enterprises (NGOs).

Success criterion 1

The nature of business

A business is a decision-making organisation that uses inputs (land, labour, capital and enterprise) in a process to produce outputs: goods (tangible) and services (intangible) that meet people’s needs and wants. Businesses add value by turning inputs into outputs that customers value more highly than the cost of the inputs.

Every business has four interdependent functional areas: human resources (people), finance and accounts (money), marketing (customers) and operations management (production). Decisions in one area affect the others; for example, a new marketing campaign needs finance and enough production capacity.

Exam tip: Customers buy a product; consumers use it. They may be different people, such as a parent (customer) buying a toy for a child (consumer).

Success criterion 2

Sectors of economic activity

Most high-income economies have a large tertiary sector (often over 70% of employment), middle-income economies a large secondary sector, and low-income economies a large primary sector. Deindustrialisation is the decline of the secondary sector, often as manufacturing moves to lower-cost countries or is automated.

Success criterion 3

Entrepreneurship and starting a business

An entrepreneur organises the other factors of production and takes the risk of starting a business, in return for profit. Intrapreneurship is entrepreneurial behaviour by employees inside an existing organisation.

Opportunities (reasons to start)Challenges (problems faced)
Being your own boss and flexibilityLack of finance and cash flow problems
Profit and personal wealthLack of experience and skills
Turning a hobby or idea into a careerCompetition from established firms
Identifying a gap in the marketBuilding a customer base and brand
Job security (cannot be made redundant)Legal requirements, red tape and high costs
Challenge and satisfactionUnlimited liability and risk of failure

A business plan sets out the business idea, objectives, market research, marketing plan, finance and operations; it is used to obtain finance and to guide decisions.

Success criterion 4

Types of business entity

TypeKey featuresAdvantagesDisadvantages
Sole traderOne owner; unincorporated; unlimited liabilityEasy to set up; owner keeps all profit; full control; privacyUnlimited liability; limited finance; long hours; no continuity
Partnership2–20 owners (usually); unlimited liability (except limited partners)More capital and skills; shared workloadDisagreements; unlimited liability; profits shared
Privately held company (Ltd)Shares sold privately, not on a stock exchange; limited liabilityLimited liability; continuity; owners keep controlCannot sell shares to the public; legal costs; must publish some accounts
Publicly held company (plc)Shares traded on a stock exchange; limited liabilityCan raise large amounts of capital; status; continuityRisk of takeover; divorce of ownership and control; short-term pressure from shareholders; disclosure

Limited liability means owners can lose only the money they invested; the company is a separate legal entity (incorporated). Unlimited liability means owners’ personal assets can be used to pay business debts.

The private sector is owned by private individuals; the public sector is owned and controlled by the government (state-owned enterprises such as many national postal services). Privatisation moves organisations from the public to the private sector.

Success criterion 5

Social enterprises

A social enterprise is a revenue-generating business whose main purpose is social or environmental. It may still make a surplus (profit), but this is reinvested in its mission.

  • For-profit social enterprises: private sector companies with a social mission (for example a café employing ex-offenders), public sector companies set up for social purposes, and cooperatives — owned and run by their members (workers, customers or producers) for their mutual benefit, with democratic control.
  • Non-profit social enterprises: non-governmental organisations (NGOs) such as charities and pressure groups, which rely on donations, grants and fundraising and use any surplus for their cause (e.g. Oxfam, Médecins Sans Frontières).

Exam tip: Paper 3 (HL) is always about a social enterprise, so make sure you can explain how its aims and sources of finance differ from a commercial business.

Key terms

Business
An organisation that uses resources to produce goods or services to meet people’s needs and wants.
Entrepreneur
A person who organises resources and takes the risk of running a business.
Intrapreneur
An employee who acts entrepreneurially within an organisation.
Limited liability
Owners are not personally responsible for business debts beyond what they invested.
Unlimited liability
Owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business.
Publicly held company
A company whose shares can be bought and sold by the public on a stock exchange.
Cooperative
A business owned and run by its members, who share the benefits.
Social enterprise
A revenue-generating business whose main aim is social or environmental.
NGO
A non-governmental, non-profit organisation working for a social cause.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a successful sole trader should convert the business into a privately held company. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Advantages: limited liability, easier to raise finance, continuity, status.
  • Disadvantages: loss of some control, legal costs, publication of accounts, profits shared with shareholders.
  • Conclusion: depends on growth plans, risk and need for finance.

Model answer: Converting gives limited liability, so the owner’s house and savings are protected as the business takes on more risk, and selling shares to family or investors raises capital for growth. However, the owner may lose some control and must share profits and meet legal and reporting requirements. If the owner plans to expand significantly, conversion is likely to be worthwhile; if the business is small and low-risk, remaining a sole trader keeps it simple.

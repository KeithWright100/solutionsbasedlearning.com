3. Growth and evolution; multinational companies

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 1: Introduction to business management · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand why businesses grow or stay small, the economies and diseconomies of scale, methods of internal and external growth, and the impact of MNCs on host countries.

Success criterion 1

Economies and diseconomies of scale

Internal economies of scale
Purchasing (bulk buying)Technical (more efficient machinery)Financial (cheaper borrowing)
Managerial (specialist managers)Specialisation of labourMarketing (spread advertising costs)
Risk-bearing (diversified products)

External economies of scale benefit all firms in an industry as the industry grows in an area: skilled local labour, specialist suppliers, better infrastructure (e.g. technology firms in Silicon Valley or Shenzhen).

Internal diseconomies: lack of control and coordination, poor communication, alienation and low morale of workers. External diseconomies: rising land prices, congestion and competition for staff as an industry concentrates in one place.

Why grow, and why stay small?

Reasons to growReasons to stay small

Success criterion 3

Internal and external growth

Internal (organic) growth uses the business’s own resources: new products, more stores, new markets, more staff. It is lower risk, keeps control and culture, and is cheaper, but it is slow.

External (inorganic) growth involves other organisations: mergers, takeovers, joint ventures, strategic alliances and franchising. It is fast and gives instant access to markets, skills and brands, but is expensive and risky (culture clashes, integration problems).

Success criterion 4

External growth methods

MethodMeaningEvaluation
MergerTwo firms agree to join into one new firmShared resources, synergy; but culture clash, job losses
Acquisition / takeoverOne firm buys a controlling interest (over 50%) in another; a takeover may be hostileFast growth, removes a rival; but expensive, resistance, integration risk
Joint ventureTwo or more firms create a new, separate business, sharing costs, risks and profitsLocal knowledge, shared risk; but shared control and profit, possible conflict
Strategic allianceFirms cooperate on a project while staying independent (no new firm)Low cost, flexible; but less commitment, possible loss of secrets
FranchisingA franchisor sells the right to use its brand and system to franchisees for a fee and royaltiesRapid low-cost expansion for the franchisor, lower risk for the franchisee; but less control, royalties reduce franchisee profit

Integration can be horizontal (same stage, same industry), vertical backward (towards suppliers) or forward (towards customers), or conglomerate (unrelated industries, for diversification).

Real-world example: McDonald’s operates mainly through franchising: around 95% of its restaurants worldwide are run by franchisees.

Success criterion 5

Multinational companies (MNCs)

A multinational company operates (produces or provides services) in more than one country. MNCs grow to access new markets, lower costs, raw materials, and to avoid trade barriers.

Possible benefits for the host countryPossible costs for the host country
Jobs and income for local peopleProfits repatriated to the home country
Investment, technology and skills transferLocal firms may be outcompeted
Tax revenue for the governmentTax avoidance (transfer pricing)
Better products and choiceExploitation of low-wage workers
Infrastructure investment and exportsEnvironmental damage and resource depletion
Supports suppliers (multiplier effects)Power to influence governments; may leave suddenly

Exam tip: For an MNC question, always say which stakeholders in the host country gain and lose, and whether the effects are short-term or long-term.

Key terms

Economies of scale
Falls in average cost as a business increases its scale of operation.
Diseconomies of scale
Rises in average cost when a business grows too large.
Internal growth
Growth using the business’s own resources (organic growth).
External growth
Growth by joining with or buying other businesses.
Merger
Two businesses agreeing to combine into one.
Takeover
Buying a controlling interest in another company, sometimes against its wishes.
Joint venture
Two or more firms creating a new, separate business together.
Strategic alliance
Cooperation between independent firms on a shared project.
Franchise
The right to use another firm’s brand and business model in return for fees and royalties.
Multinational company
A business that operates in more than one country.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether external growth is better than internal growth for a fast-growing business. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define internal and external growth; examples of each method.
  • External: speed, market access, economies of scale v cost, risk, culture clash.
  • Internal: control, lower risk v slow. Conclude with the business’s context.

Model answer: External growth, such as acquiring a competitor, allows rapid expansion and immediate access to customers, skills and economies of scale. However, it is expensive and risky: many acquisitions fail because of culture clashes and integration problems. Internal growth is slower but keeps control and culture and is less risky. A fast-growing business with strong finances and a clear target may benefit from an acquisition or joint venture to enter new markets quickly, but if finance is limited, organic growth or franchising may be safer.

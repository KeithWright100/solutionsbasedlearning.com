1. Introduction to human resource management

Success criterion 1

The role of HRM

Human resource management (HRM) is the management of an organisation’s people so that it has the right number of staff, with the right skills, in the right place at the right time. HR roles include workforce (human resource) planning, recruitment and selection, training and development, appraisal, motivation and rewards, managing employee relations, and dismissal and redundancy.

Human resource planning forecasts the organisation’s future staffing needs (demand for labour) and compares them with the likely supply of labour, then plans recruitment, training or redeployment to close any gap.

  • Dismissal: ending employment because of misconduct or poor performance.
  • Redundancy: ending employment because the job no longer exists (closure, new technology, restructuring).
  • Labour turnover, retention and absenteeism are indicators of how well HR is managed.
Success criterion 2

Factors influencing human resource planning

FactorEffect on HR planning
Demographic changeAn ageing population shrinks the workforce and raises demand for healthcare staff; a young population increases the supply of labour.
Labour mobilityOccupational mobility (changing jobs or skills) and geographical mobility (moving location) affect the supply of suitable workers.
ImmigrationMigrant workers can fill skill shortages, but rules on visas can limit supply.
Flexi-time and new working patternsEmployees choose working hours within limits; hybrid and remote working change office needs.
The gig economyShort-term, freelance or app-based work (ride-sharing, delivery) gives flexibility but fewer rights and less security.
Internal factorsThe firm’s objectives, finance, growth plans, labour turnover, organisational culture and new technology.

Outsourcing (using external firms for non-core activities), offshoring (moving activities abroad) and reshoring (bringing them back) also change how many staff a business needs and where.

Success criterion 3

Changing work patterns

PatternAdvantagesDisadvantages
Flexi-time and part-time workBetter work–life balance, retention, wider talent poolHarder to coordinate and supervise
Homeworking / remote workingLower office costs, no commuting, global recruitmentIsolation, weaker culture, IT and security costs
Gig economyFirms pay only for work done; workers choose when to workLow security and benefits for workers; quality control; legal challenges
OutsourcingLower costs, access to specialists, focus on core activitiesLoss of control and quality; job losses; reliance on supplier
Success criterion 4

Resistance to change and HR strategies

People often resist change in the workplace because of fear of the unknown, job insecurity, worries about new skills (low self-confidence), loss of status or income, disruption to routines and relationships, lack of trust in managers, and poor communication about why change is needed.

  • Communication: explain the reasons for, and benefits of, the change early and honestly.
  • Consultation and participation: involve staff in planning the change.
  • Training and support to build the skills and confidence needed.
  • Incentives: rewards or guarantees (e.g. no compulsory redundancies).
  • Introducing change gradually, with trials and clear timelines.
  • Using force field analysis (HL) to identify and reduce restraining forces.

Exam tip: Change is a key concept. In a 10-mark answer, consider how the size, speed and type of change affect stakeholders differently.

Key terms

Human resource management
Managing people in an organisation: planning, recruiting, training, rewarding and developing staff.
Human resource planning
Forecasting future staffing needs and planning how to meet them.
Labour mobility
The ease with which workers can change jobs (occupational) or location (geographical).
Gig economy
A labour market of short-term, freelance or app-based work.
Flexi-time
A system that lets employees choose their working hours within set limits.
Outsourcing
Using external organisations to carry out business activities.
Redundancy
Ending employment because a job is no longer needed.
Dismissal
Ending employment because of the employee’s conduct or performance.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a large retailer should use gig economy workers instead of permanent staff for its home delivery service. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Advantages: flexibility to match demand, lower fixed costs, quick scaling.
  • Disadvantages: quality and reliability, brand image, legal and ethical concerns, lower loyalty.
  • Stakeholders: workers, customers, shareholders; conclusion.

Model answer: Using gig workers lets the retailer pay only for deliveries made, so labour costs rise and fall with demand, which suits seasonal peaks. However, gig workers may be less committed and less well trained, which could damage service quality and the brand, and there is growing legal and ethical pressure about workers’ rights. A mix of a small permanent team plus gig workers at peak times may give the best balance between cost and quality.

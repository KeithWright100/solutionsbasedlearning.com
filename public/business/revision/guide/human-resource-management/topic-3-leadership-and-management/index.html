3. Leadership and management

Success criterion 1

Management and leadership

Management is about getting things done through planning, organising, coordinating, commanding and controlling resources (Fayol). Leadership is about influencing and inspiring people towards a goal, setting direction and creating a vision.

Managers focus on systems, short-term targets and control (“doing things right”); leaders focus on people, the long term and change (“doing the right things”). Effective managers need leadership skills, and leaders need management skills.

Success criterion 2

Leadership styles

StyleFeaturesAdvantagesDisadvantages
AutocraticLeader makes decisions alone and gives orders; one-way communicationQuick decisions; clear direction; useful in a crisis or with unskilled staffDemotivates skilled staff; no use of their ideas; high labour turnover
PaternalisticLeader decides, but in what they believe is the staff’s best interests; cares for welfareLoyalty; staff feel looked afterCan feel patronising; still little participation
DemocraticStaff are consulted and take part in decisions; two-way communicationBetter ideas; motivation (esteem needs); commitment to decisionsSlow decisions; not suitable in an emergency
Laissez-faireLeader gives staff freedom to decide how to work, with little directionCreativity and motivation for skilled, self-motivated staffLack of direction and coordination; poor results with inexperienced staff
Success criterion 3

Situational leadership

Situational leadership argues there is no single best style: effective leaders adapt to the situation. Factors include the skills and experience of the team, the task (routine or creative), the time available (a crisis needs speed), the organisational culture, the leader’s personality, and the size of the organisation.

Success criterion 4

HLScientific and intuitive thinking

Scientific thinking/management bases decisions on data, evidence and logical analysis (for example ratio analysis, investment appraisal, market research). It is objective and reduces risk, but can be slow and may ignore people and things that cannot be measured.

Intuitive thinking/management relies on gut feeling, experience and creativity. It is fast and can lead to innovation, but it is subjective and risky. Most good decisions combine both: data informs the decision, and intuition helps when information is incomplete or the situation is new.

Key terms

Management
Planning, organising, coordinating and controlling resources to achieve objectives.
Leadership
Influencing and inspiring others to achieve goals.
Autocratic leadership
The leader makes all decisions without consulting staff.
Paternalistic leadership
The leader decides in what they believe are the employees’ best interests.
Democratic leadership
The leader involves staff in decision-making.
Laissez-faire leadership
The leader gives staff freedom to make their own decisions.
Situational leadership
Adapting the leadership style to the situation.
Scientific management
HL: decision-making based on data and logical analysis.
Intuitive management
HL: decision-making based on experience, instinct and creativity.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a democratic leadership style is the most appropriate style for a fast-growing start-up. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Explain democratic leadership and compare with at least one other style.
  • Context: skills of staff, speed of decisions, culture of the start-up.
  • Situational leadership; justified conclusion.

Model answer: A democratic style can motivate a small team of skilled staff and bring in ideas, which suits an innovative start-up. However, rapid growth often requires quick decisions, and as new, less experienced staff join, more direction may be needed. Situational leadership suggests the founder should combine styles: democratic for strategy and product ideas, more autocratic in a crisis or when deadlines are tight.

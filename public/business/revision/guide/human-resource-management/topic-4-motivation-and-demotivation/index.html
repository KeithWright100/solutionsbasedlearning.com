4. Motivation and demotivation

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 2: Human resource management · Topics · Topic 4 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand motivation theories and how financial and non-financial rewards and training can motivate staff (HL: further theories, labour turnover, appraisal and recruitment).

Syllabus Statement

2.4 Motivation and demotivation: Taylor, Maslow, Herzberg; financial rewards (wages, salary, commission, PRP, profit-related pay, employee share ownership, fringe payments); non-financial rewards (job enrichment, rotation, enlargement, empowerment, purpose, teamwork); training (induction, on the job, off the job). HL: McClelland, Deci and Ryan, equity and expectancy theory; labour turnover; appraisal; recruitment.

Success criterion 1

Content theories: Taylor, Maslow and Herzberg

Taylor (scientific management): workers are motivated mainly by money. Jobs should be broken into simple tasks, studied (time and motion) and paid by output (piece rates). It raised productivity in factories, but ignores social needs, can be dehumanising and does not suit skilled or creative work.

Success criterion 2

Financial rewards

RewardMeaning
Wages (time rate or piece rate)Paid per hour, or per unit produced
SalaryFixed annual amount paid monthly
CommissionA percentage of the value of sales made
Performance-related pay (PRP)Extra pay for meeting individual targets, usually after an appraisal
Profit-related payA share of the firm’s profit paid to staff
Employee share ownershipStaff receive or can buy shares, so they gain if the firm does well
Fringe payments (perks)Benefits such as a company car, healthcare, pension, discounts

Financial rewards can motivate when pay is linked to output that can be measured, but they may encourage quantity over quality, cause conflict and, according to Herzberg, only prevent dissatisfaction.

Success criterion 3

Non-financial rewards and training

  • Job enrichment: giving more challenging, responsible tasks (vertical loading).
  • Job enlargement: more tasks at the same level (horizontal loading).
  • Job rotation: moving between different tasks to reduce boredom and multi-skill staff.
  • Empowerment: giving staff authority to make decisions about their work.
  • Purpose / the opportunity to make a difference: meaningful work linked to the organisation’s mission.
  • Teamwork: working in groups, meeting social needs.

Training: induction introduces new staff to the organisation; on-the-job training happens at work (cheap and relevant, but may pass on bad habits); off-the-job training happens away from the workplace (specialist and focused, but costly and staff may leave once qualified).

Success criterion 4

HLFurther motivation theories

TheoryMain idea
McClelland’s acquired needs theoryPeople are driven by a dominant need for achievement, affiliation or power, shaped by experience. Managers should match roles to needs (e.g. challenging targets for high achievers).
Deci and Ryan’s self-determination theoryIntrinsic motivation depends on autonomy, competence and relatedness. Rewards that feel controlling can reduce motivation.
Equity theory (Adams)Workers compare their inputs and rewards with others; perceived unfairness demotivates.
Expectancy theory (Vroom)Motivation = expectancy (effort leads to performance) × instrumentality (performance leads to reward) × valence (the reward is valued).
Success criterion 5

HLLabour turnover, appraisal and recruitment

HLLabour turnoverLabour turnover = number of staff leaving in a year ÷ average number of staff × 100. Example: 18 leavers, average workforce 120: 18 ÷ 120 × 100 = 15%. High turnover raises recruitment and training costs and can signal low morale; some turnover brings fresh ideas.
  • Appraisal: formative (ongoing feedback to help development), summative (judging performance at the end of a period), 360-degree feedback (from managers, peers, subordinates and customers) and self-appraisal.
  • Recruitment: internal (promotion or transfer: cheaper, quicker, known candidates, motivating; but no new ideas and creates another vacancy) and external (advertising, agencies, social media, headhunting: wider pool and fresh ideas, but costly and riskier).

Exam-style question

Discuss whether financial rewards are the best way to motivate workers in a call centre. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Use Taylor (money motivates) v Maslow and Herzberg (higher needs, motivators).
  • Financial methods: commission, PRP; non-financial: enrichment, empowerment, teamwork.
  • Conclusion based on the nature of the work and staff.

Model answer: Call centre work is often repetitive and measurable, so commission or performance-related pay can raise output, as Taylor suggests. However, Herzberg argues pay is a hygiene factor: it prevents dissatisfaction but does not create lasting motivation, and targets may reduce service quality. Non-financial methods such as job rotation, teamwork and empowerment to solve customer problems can meet social and esteem needs. A combination is likely to work best.

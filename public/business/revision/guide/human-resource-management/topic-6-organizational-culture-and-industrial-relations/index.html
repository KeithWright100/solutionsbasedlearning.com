6. HL: Organizational culture and industrial/employee relations

IB Business Management Revision Guide · Unit 2: Human resource management · Topics · Topic 6 of 6

Success criterion 1

HLOrganisational culture

Organisational (corporate) culture is the shared values, beliefs, attitudes and norms that shape how people behave in an organisation: “the way we do things around here”. Elements include the leadership style, dress code, symbols and stories, rituals, the language used, the physical working environment and how risk and failure are treated.

Charles Handy described four types: power culture (control by a central figure), role culture (rules and procedures; bureaucratic), task culture (team-based, project-focused) and person culture (individuals are the focus, e.g. partners in a law firm).

Culture influences individuals (new staff adapt to it), and individuals, especially leaders, can change culture. A strong positive culture improves motivation, loyalty and performance; a toxic culture can lead to high labour turnover and scandals.

Success criterion 2

HLCultural clashes

A cultural clash happens when people with different values and ways of working are brought together: after a merger or takeover, when a new leader arrives, or when a business expands abroad (national cultural differences; see Hofstede in the toolkit). Consequences: conflict, misunderstandings, lower productivity, resistance to change and staff leaving.

Reducing clashes: due diligence on culture before a merger, clear communication of shared values, training, integration teams, and allowing time for change.

Success criterion 3

HLIndustrial/employee relations: sources of conflict

Employee relations are the relationships between managers and employees, often represented by trade unions (organisations that represent workers in negotiations). Conflict arises over pay and benefits, working conditions and hours, job security and redundancies, changes in working practices or technology, and management styles.

Success criterion 4

HLResolving conflict

  • Conciliation: an independent third party helps both sides talk and reach their own agreement.
  • Arbitration: an independent third party hears both sides and decides; in binding arbitration both sides must accept the decision.
  • Employee participation and industrial democracy: worker representatives on boards and works councils (common in Germany).
  • No-strike agreements: unions agree not to strike in return for guarantees, often with arbitration.
  • Single-union agreements: managers negotiate with one union, simplifying bargaining.

Real-world example: In 2023, Hollywood writers (WGA) and actors (SAG-AFTRA) went on long strikes over pay from streaming and the use of AI, halting film and TV production before agreements were reached.

Exam-style question

Discuss how a cultural clash might affect a company after it takes over a foreign competitor. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define culture and cultural clash; use Handy and/or Hofstede.
  • Effects: conflict, lower productivity, staff leaving, slower integration.
  • How it can be managed; conclusion on its significance.

Model answer: If the buyer has a hierarchical role culture and the target has an informal task culture, staff may resent new rules and controls, productivity may fall and key talent may leave. National differences in power distance and communication style can add to misunderstanding. However, with careful planning, communication and integration teams, the clash can be reduced, and some differences can even bring new ideas.

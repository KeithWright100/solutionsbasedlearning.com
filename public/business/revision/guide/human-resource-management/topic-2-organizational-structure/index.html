2. Organizational structure

Success criterion 1

Key terms

TermMeaning
DelegationPassing authority (but not ultimate responsibility) to a subordinate to carry out a task.
Span of controlThe number of subordinates directly responsible to a manager.
Levels of hierarchyThe number of layers of management in an organisation.
Chain of commandThe line of authority through which instructions pass from the top to the bottom.
BureaucracyRules, procedures and paperwork that govern how decisions are made.
CentralisationDecision-making authority is kept at the top of the organisation.
DecentralisationDecision-making authority is passed down to lower levels or local branches.
DelayeringRemoving one or more levels of hierarchy, usually middle management.

There is an inverse relationship: the wider the span of control, the fewer the levels of hierarchy (flatter structure).

Success criterion 2

Organisation charts

Organisations can also be structured by function (marketing, finance, HR, operations), by product (separate divisions for each product line, e.g. a consumer electronics division and an appliances division) or by region (Asia, Europe, Americas). Product and regional structures allow decisions to suit each market, but can duplicate functions and raise costs.

Success criterion 4

Choosing an appropriate structure

Structures must change as the business and its environment change. A fast-changing, competitive market (new technology, changing tastes) favours flatter, decentralised structures that respond quickly; a large organisation in a stable or highly regulated market (banks, public services) may need centralised, more bureaucratic control. Growth abroad often leads to regional structures; a crisis may lead to centralisation.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
CentralisationConsistent decisions and brand; tight control; economies of scale in decision-makingSlow; ignores local knowledge; can demotivate middle managers
DecentralisationFaster local decisions; motivates and develops managers; local responsivenessInconsistent decisions; loss of control; duplication of costs
Success criterion 5

HLProject-based organisation and the Shamrock organisation

A project-based organisation creates temporary teams for each project, bringing together people with the skills needed; teams disband when the project ends. It is flexible and suits innovative industries (consulting, film-making, construction).

Charles Handy’s Shamrock organisation has three “leaves”: (1) the professional core of essential, permanent, highly skilled staff; (2) the contractual fringe of outsourced specialists and contractors; and (3) the flexible labour force of part-time, temporary and seasonal workers. It lowers costs and increases flexibility, but non-core workers may have little loyalty and job security, and it can be hard to maintain quality and culture.

Key terms

Span of control

Levels of hierarchy

Chain of command
The line of authority from the top to the bottom of an organisation.

A structure where staff from different functions work in project teams, with two lines of reporting.

Exam-style question

Discuss whether a growing technology company should delayer its organisational structure. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Marked with five criteria, each worth 2 marks (10 in total):
  • A: knowledge and understanding of relevant tools, techniques and theories.
  • B: application to the case study or stimulus (use names, figures and facts from it).
  • C: reasoned arguments, balanced, with a justified conclusion.
  • D: structure (introduction, logical paragraphs, conclusion) and relevant terminology.
  • E: individuals and societies: consider the perspectives of different stakeholders.
  • Define delayering, span of control, levels of hierarchy; chart before/after.
  • Benefits: lower costs, faster communication, empowerment.
  • Costs: overworked managers, fewer promotion routes, redundancy and morale; conclusion.

Model answer: Delayering removes layers of middle management, so communication is faster and decisions can be made closer to customers, which suits a fast-changing technology market. It also cuts salary costs and can empower staff. However, wider spans of control may overload remaining managers, reduce supervision and cut promotion opportunities, and redundancies may lower morale. If staff are skilled and self-motivated, as in many tech firms, the benefits are likely to outweigh the costs.

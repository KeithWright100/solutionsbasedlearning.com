6. HL: Asymmetric information, market power and equity

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 2: Microeconomics · Topics · Topic 6 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how asymmetric information and market power cause market failure, how firms behave in different market structures, and why markets may not achieve equity.

Syllabus Statement

2.10 Market failure — asymmetric information (HL): adverse selection, moral hazard, responses. 2.11 Market failure — market power (HL): costs, revenues and profit maximisation; perfect competition, monopoly, natural monopoly, oligopoly (collusive and non-collusive, game theory) and monopolistic competition; efficiency; barriers to entry; regulation of market power. 2.12 The market’s inability to achieve equity (HL).

Success criterion 1

HLAsymmetric information

Asymmetric information exists when one party to a transaction has more information than the other, so the market may not reach the efficient outcome.

  • Adverse selection (before the transaction): the party with more information uses it. Sellers of used cars know more about their quality (the “market for lemons”); people who know they are high risk are most likely to buy health insurance, pushing up premiums for everyone.
  • Moral hazard (after the transaction): one party takes more risks because the cost is borne by someone else. Insured drivers may drive less carefully; banks that expect a government bailout may lend recklessly (as before the 2008 financial crisis).

Responses: screening (insurers use medical checks), signalling (warranties, qualifications, brands), regulation (licensing professionals, compulsory disclosure, deposit insurance with bank regulation) and government provision of information.

Success criterion 2

HLCosts, revenue and profit maximisation

Firms maximise profit where marginal cost = marginal revenue (MC = MR): producing one more unit would add more to cost than to revenue.

Economists measure economic costs as explicit costs (payments to others, such as wages and rent) plus implicit costs (the opportunity cost of resources the owner already has, such as their own time and capital). In the short run, total cost = fixed cost + variable cost, and average costs (AFC, AVC, ATC) and marginal cost are derived from these; MC rises because of diminishing marginal returns.

  • Normal profit (AR = AC): the minimum return needed to keep the entrepreneur in the industry. It is included in costs, so economic profit is zero.
  • Abnormal (supernormal) profit (AR > AC): profit above normal profit, which attracts new firms if entry is possible.
  • Loss (AR < AC). In the short run a firm keeps producing while AR covers average variable cost (AVC); it shuts down if price falls below AVC. In the long run it must cover all costs.
HLCalculations from dataTotal profit = TR − TC. AR = TR ÷ Q. MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ. AC = TC ÷ Q. MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ. Find the output where MC = MR (or where TR − TC is largest).

Monopolies are not always bad: abnormal profit can fund research and development and innovation (dynamic efficiency), and large firms may gain economies of scale that lower costs below those of competitive firms.

Success criterion 4

HLOligopoly and monopolistic competition

Oligopoly: a few large, interdependent firms dominate the market (high concentration ratio), with high barriers to entry — for example supermarkets, mobile networks, airlines.

  • Collusive oligopoly: firms agree to fix prices or output (a cartel, e.g. OPEC+), acting like a monopoly, so collusive oligopoly is drawn with the monopoly diagram (output where MC = MR, price above MC). Formal collusion is illegal in most countries. Tacit collusion includes price leadership.
  • Non-collusive oligopoly: firms compete but each must consider rivals’ reactions. They avoid price wars and use non-price competition (advertising, branding, loyalty schemes, product development).
  • Game theory (the prisoner’s dilemma) shows that firms acting in their own interest (e.g. both cutting prices) can end up worse off than if they cooperated, which creates an incentive to collude — and to cheat on agreements.
Payoff matrix (profits)
B keeps price highB cuts price
A keeps price highA $10m, B $10mA $2m, B $14m
A cuts priceA $14m, B $2mA $5m, B $5m

Whatever B does, A earns more by cutting price (14 > 10, 5 > 2), and the same is true for B, so both cut price and earn $5m — worse than the $10m each from cooperating.

Success criterion 5

HLRegulating market power and the market’s inability to achieve equity

  • Legislation (competition law) bans cartels and abuse of a dominant position, and blocks harmful mergers.
  • Regulation of natural monopolies: price caps or rate-of-return regulation.
  • Fines for anti-competitive behaviour (the EU fined Google €4.3 billion in 2018 over Android).
  • Nationalisation or public ownership; trade liberalisation to bring in foreign competition.

Equity: even an efficient free market may produce an unequal distribution of income and wealth, because people start with different amounts of factors of production (skills, capital, land) and opportunities. In the circular flow, households receive income only for the factors they own and sell, so those without factors to sell — the elderly, the sick, the unemployed — receive little income. Markets also under-provide merit goods to those who cannot pay. Government responses (taxation, transfers, merit goods) are covered in Unit 3.

Key terms

Asymmetric information
One party to a transaction has more information than the other.
Adverse selection
The party with more information uses it before a transaction, leading to market failure.
Moral hazard
Taking more risks after a transaction because someone else bears the cost.
Normal profit
The minimum profit needed to keep a firm in the industry (AR = AC).
Abnormal profit
Profit above normal profit (AR > AC).
Barriers to entry
Obstacles that prevent new firms from entering a market.
Natural monopoly
A market where one firm can supply the whole market at lower cost than two or more firms.
Oligopoly
A market dominated by a few large interdependent firms.
Cartel
A group of firms that collude to fix prices or output.
Concentration ratio
The share of the market held by the largest firms.
Monopolistic competition
A market with many firms selling differentiated products and low barriers to entry.
Exam-style question

Using an appropriate diagram, explain why a monopoly may lead to a misallocation of resources. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define monopoly and allocative efficiency (P = MC).
  • Draw monopoly diagram with AR, MR, MC; mark Qm, Pm and the competitive output; shade welfare loss.
  • Explain that output is restricted and price raised, so P > MC and social surplus is lost.

Model answer: A monopoly is the only supplier, so it faces the downward-sloping market demand curve and MR lies below AR. It maximises profit where MC = MR, producing Qm and charging Pm. At Qm, price (the value consumers place on the last unit) exceeds marginal cost, so society would gain from more output. Compared with the competitive outcome at Qc, output is lower and price higher, and there is a welfare loss: resources are underallocated to this industry.

  • May 2022 · P1 HL · Q1(b)Discuss the effects of large firms with significant market power, using real examples.[15]

    • Balance: economies of scale, R&D and innovation (dynamic efficiency), natural monopoly; v consumer harm and collusion.

  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(a)(vi)Calculate suppliers’ total profit from cost and revenue data.[2]

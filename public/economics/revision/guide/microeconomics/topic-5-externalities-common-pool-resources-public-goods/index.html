5. Market failure: externalities, common pool resources, public goods and asymmetric information

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 2: Microeconomics · Topics · Topic 5 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand why markets fail when there are externalities, common pool resources, public goods and (HL) asymmetric information, and to evaluate the policies used to correct them.

Success criterion 1

Market failure and negative externalities

Market failure occurs when the market does not allocate resources efficiently, so social surplus is not maximised. An externality is a cost or benefit to a third party that is not reflected in the market price. When there are externalities, private and social costs or benefits differ:

MSC = MPC + external costs; MSB = MPB + external benefits. The social optimum is where MSB = MSC.

Real-world example: Air pollution from coal-fired power stations damages the health of people living nearby: an external cost of production. Second-hand smoke is an external cost of consumption.

Success criterion 2

Positive externalities; merit and demerit goods

Merit goods (education, healthcare, vaccinations) are underprovided and underconsumed by the free market, partly because of positive externalities and partly because consumers underestimate their benefits (imperfect information). Demerit goods (tobacco, alcohol, sugary drinks) are overconsumed because of negative externalities and because consumers underestimate the harm.

Policies for positive externalities: government provision (free schools, vaccination programmes), subsidies, legislation and regulation (compulsory schooling, required vaccinations), education and awareness campaigns and, at HL, nudges.

Success criterion 3

Common pool resources and sustainability

Common pool resources (fish stocks, forests, grazing land, the atmosphere) are rivalrous (one person’s use reduces what is left for others) but non-excludable (it is hard to stop people using them). There is no price, so users overuse them: the tragedy of the commons. Unsustainable production (such as burning fossil fuels) creates negative externalities that threaten future well-being.

  • Legislation: fishing quotas, protected areas, bans on logging.
  • Carbon taxes and cap and trade schemes.
  • Subsidies for clean technologies and renewable energy.
  • International agreements (the Paris Agreement, the Montreal Protocol) — needed because the problem crosses borders but hard to enforce.
  • Collective self-governance (Elinor Ostrom): local communities can manage shared resources successfully through their own rules.

Success criterion 5

Public goods

ExcludableNon-excludable
RivalrousPrivate goods (food, clothes)Common pool resources (fish)
Non-rivalrousClub goods (subscription TV)Public goods (street lighting, national defence)

Public goods are non-rivalrous (one person’s use does not reduce availability to others) and non-excludable (people cannot be stopped from benefiting). Because people can benefit without paying, they have an incentive to be free riders, so private firms cannot earn revenue and the market provides too little or none. The result is a missing market. Governments therefore provide public goods directly, financed by taxation, or contract private firms to supply them.

Success criterion 6

HLMarket failure: asymmetric information

Asymmetric information exists when one party to a transaction (buyer or seller) has more information than the other. Like externalities and public goods, it is a source of market failure: the market price does not reflect the true costs and benefits, so resources are misallocated and the market may not reach the socially optimum output, or may not exist at all.

  • Adverse selection (before the transaction): the party with more information uses it. Sellers of used cars know more about their quality (the “market for lemons”); people who know they are high risk are most likely to buy health insurance, pushing up premiums for everyone.
  • Moral hazard (after the transaction): one party takes more risks because the cost is borne by someone else. Insured drivers may drive less carefully; banks that expect a government bailout may lend recklessly (as before the 2008 financial crisis).

Responses: screening (insurers use medical checks), signalling (warranties, qualifications, brands), regulation (licensing professionals, compulsory disclosure, deposit insurance with bank regulation) and government provision of information.

Exam tip: Link asymmetric information back to the other market failures on this page: in each case the free market does not achieve allocative efficiency (MSB = MSC), so there is a case for government intervention.

Key terms

Market failure
When the market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so social surplus is not maximised.
Externality
A cost or benefit to a third party not reflected in the price.
Marginal social cost (MSC)
MPC plus any external cost.
Marginal social benefit (MSB)
MPB plus any external benefit.
Merit good
A good that is underconsumed in a free market; it has external benefits or its private benefits are underestimated.
Demerit good
A good that is overconsumed in a free market and harmful to the consumer or others.
Common pool resource
A resource that is rivalrous but non-excludable.
Tragedy of the commons
The overuse of common pool resources because no one owns them or pays for their use.
Tradable permits
Permits to pollute that can be bought and sold, within a cap set by the government.
Public good
A good that is non-rivalrous and non-excludable.
Free rider problem
People benefit from a good without paying for it, so the market will not provide it.
Asymmetric information (HL)
When one party to a transaction has more information than the other.
Adverse selection (HL)
Before a transaction, the better-informed party uses hidden information, so the wrong buyers or sellers enter the market.
Moral hazard (HL)
After a transaction, one party takes more risk because someone else bears the cost.
Signalling and screening (HL)
Private responses: the informed party signals quality (warranties, qualifications); the uninformed party screens (medical checks, credit checks).
Exam-style question

Evaluate the view that a carbon tax is the most effective way to reduce the negative externalities of fossil fuel use. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define negative production externality, carbon tax; draw MSC/MPC diagram with tax shifting MPC to MSC.
  • Compare with tradable permits, regulation and subsidies for renewables.
  • Evaluate: PED, measuring external cost, regressive effects, international coordination, example (e.g. Sweden’s carbon tax, EU ETS).

Model answer: A carbon tax shifts MPC up towards MSC, moving output towards the social optimum and raising revenue that can fund renewables or compensate poorer households. Sweden has had one of the world’s highest carbon taxes since 1991 while its economy kept growing. However, the right tax is hard to calculate, demand for energy is inelastic in the short run, the tax may be regressive and firms may move abroad. Tradable permits fix the quantity of emissions with more certainty; in practice countries often combine both policies.

7 IB questions on this topic

These are real IB questions from May 2022 and May 2023, reworded here as a summary. Paper 2 and Paper 3 questions use texts and data from the paper, so ask your teacher for a copy of the paper before you answer. Write your answer, then check it against the tips.

  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q1(a)Explain why positive externalities in healthcare and education can lead to market failure.[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Positive consumption externality diagram: MSB above MPB, Qm < Qopt, welfare loss shaded.
    • Apply it to BOTH healthcare and education (external benefits to society, underconsumption).
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q1(b)Discuss whether subsidies are the best way to raise consumption of merit goods, using real examples.[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Subsidy diagram moving output towards the optimum.
    • Because of "best way", compare with direct provision, regulation (compulsory schooling, vaccination), information and nudges.
    • Evaluate cost, opportunity cost, PED, targeting and who gains.
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ2 · Q1(a)Explain two government interventions to correct the market failure from demerit goods.[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Negative consumption externality diagram; explain two policies (indirect tax, regulation, information, nudges) and show the move to the optimum.
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ2 · Q1(b)Discuss whether governments should always provide public goods directly, using real examples.[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain non-rivalry, non-excludability and free riding.
    • Evaluate alternatives: contracting private firms, public–private partnerships, charities; cost and efficiency of state provision.
  • May 2022 · P2 SL · Q1(d)Using an externalities diagram, explain why reliance on coal is a market failure.[4]
    How to answer
    • Negative production externality: MSC above MPC, Qm > Qopt, shade the welfare loss.
    • Explain over-allocation of resources to coal.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(a)(i)Using a diagram, explain why traffic congestion is a market failure.[4]
    How to answer
    • Negative consumption (or production) externality diagram; Qm > Qopt and a welfare loss.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(b)Recommend a policy to tackle traffic congestion in New Delhi.[10]
    How to answer
    • Recommend ONE policy. Explain how it works with theory, use the data, weigh up its strengths and limitations, and justify why it is a good choice for this country.
    • Options include congestion charges, fuel taxes, public transport investment, nudges or regulation.

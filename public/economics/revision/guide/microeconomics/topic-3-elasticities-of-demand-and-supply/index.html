3. Elasticities of demand and supply

Success criterion 1

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

FormulaPED = %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ price. The answer is negative (law of demand), so we usually ignore the sign. Example: price rises from $10 to $12 (+20%) and quantity falls from 500 to 450 (−10%). PED = −10 ÷ 20 = −0.5: inelastic.
Rearranging: %ΔQd = PED × %ΔP. If PED = −1.2 and price falls by 5%, quantity demanded rises by 6%.
PED value (ignoring sign)Meaning
0Perfectly inelastic: quantity does not change
0 to 1Inelastic: %ΔQd < %ΔP
1Unit elastic: %ΔQd = %ΔP
1 to ∞Elastic: %ΔQd > %ΔP
∞Perfectly elastic: any price rise causes demand to fall to zero

Determinants of PED: the number and closeness of substitutes (the most important), the degree of necessity (and addictiveness), the proportion of income spent on the good, and time (demand becomes more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives). How broadly a good is defined matters: demand for “food” is inelastic, but for one brand of cereal it is elastic.

Success criterion 2

PED and total revenue; why PED matters

DemandPrice risePrice cut
Inelastic (PED < 1)Total revenue risesTotal revenue falls
Unit elastic (PED = 1)No changeNo change
Elastic (PED > 1)Total revenue fallsTotal revenue rises
  • Firms use PED to set prices: raise prices for inelastic products, cut prices for elastic ones. It also helps with price discrimination (charging different groups different prices).
  • Governments raise more tax revenue by taxing goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco, alcohol), but such taxes do little to cut consumption and may be regressive. PED also predicts the effect of subsidies and of a currency depreciation on export revenue.

Exam tip: Total revenue = price × quantity. On the diagram, show the revenue gained and lost as rectangles.

Success criterion 3

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

FormulaYED = %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ income. Example: incomes rise by 5% and demand for restaurant meals rises by 8%. YED = 8 ÷ 5 = +1.6: a normal, income-elastic (luxury) good.
Rearranging: %ΔQd = YED × %Δincome. If YED for bus travel is −0.5 and incomes rise by 4%, demand falls by 2%.
ValueType of goodExamples
YED < 0Inferior goodBus travel, cheap own-brand food
0 < YED < 1Normal good, income inelastic (necessity)Basic food, utilities
YED > 1Normal good, income elastic (luxury)Foreign holidays, sports cars, fine dining
Success criterion 4

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

FormulaPES = %Δ quantity supplied ÷ %Δ price. Example: price rises by 10%, quantity supplied rises by 4%. PES = 0.4: inelastic.
Rearranging: %ΔQs = PES × %ΔP. If PES = 1.5 and price rises by 6%, quantity supplied rises by 9%.

Determinants of PES: time (supply is more elastic in the long run), spare capacity, the mobility of factors of production, the ability to store stock, and how fast costs rise as output increases.

Success criterion 5

HLPED along a straight-line demand curve; PED of primary commodities

Why PED changes along a straight-line demand curve

A straight-line demand curve has a constant slope (the same absolute change in quantity for each $1 change in price), but PED uses percentage changes. At high prices, a $1 change is a small percentage of the price, while the change in quantity is a large percentage of the small quantity, so demand is elastic. At low prices the opposite is true, so demand is inelastic. At the midpoint PED = 1 and total revenue is maximised. So you cannot judge PED from the slope (steepness) of a curve alone, except for the special cases of perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic demand.

Why PED for primary commodities is generally lower than for manufactured products

  • Necessities with few substitutes: food, energy and many raw materials have no close substitutes, so people keep buying when prices rise.
  • Derived demand for raw materials: firms need oil, copper or cotton to produce; in the short run they cannot switch inputs, and the raw material is often a small share of the final price.
  • Broad definition: “wheat” or “coffee” is a broad category, whereas manufactured products are sold as brands (one phone or car model) with many close substitutes, so their demand is more elastic.
  • Small share of income for basic foods in high-income countries.

Consequences: with low PED, any shift in supply (weather, disease, conflict) causes large swings in price, and farmers’ and commodity-exporting countries’ revenues are volatile. A bumper harvest can even lower farm revenue, because the price falls proportionately more than output rises. Low PED also means indirect taxes on commodities such as fuel raise a lot of revenue.

Success criterion 6

HLImportance of YED for firms and for the sectoral structure of the economy

Importance of YED for firms

  • Forecasting sales over the business cycle: in an economic boom, demand for income-elastic goods (foreign holidays, new cars, restaurant meals) grows fastest; in a recession it falls fastest, while demand for inferior goods (discount supermarkets, bus travel) rises. Firms use YED to plan output, stock levels, staffing and investment.
  • Spreading risk through the product range: a firm selling only luxuries is very exposed to recessions, so firms may offer a mix of products with different YEDs (for example a supermarket with a premium range and a value range).
  • Choosing markets: firms selling income-elastic products target fast-growing economies where incomes are rising quickly (luxury brands expanding in Asia).
  • Marketing and pricing: firms reposition products, for example marketing a value range more heavily when incomes fall.

Example: If YED for new cars is +2.5 and incomes are forecast to fall by 2% in a recession, a car maker can expect sales to fall by about 5% (2.5 × 2%), and can cut production before unsold stock builds up.

Importance of YED in explaining changes in the sectoral structure of the economy

The three sectors are primary (agriculture, fishing, forestry, mining), secondary (manufacturing and construction) and tertiary (services). Their products have different YEDs:

  • Primary products (especially food) have low YED (0 < YED < 1, and some are inferior). As incomes rise, people spend a smaller share of income on food (Engel’s law).
  • Manufactured goods have higher YED, so their share of spending grows as countries move from low to middle incomes.
  • Services (tourism, finance, education, healthcare, entertainment) have the highest YED, so their share keeps growing as incomes rise.

So, as an economy grows, demand and resources (labour and capital) shift from the primary sector to the secondary sector and then to the tertiary sector. This is why high-income economies are dominated by services. It also helps explain why countries that depend on exporting primary commodities may see their export revenues grow more slowly than world incomes, and why the prices of primary products tend to fall relative to manufactured goods over the long term: a reason to diversify.

Success criterion 7

HLWhy PES for primary commodities is lower than for manufactured products

Determinant of PESPrimary commodities (low PES)Manufactured products (higher PES)
Time / length of productionCrops follow growing seasons; coffee and rubber trees take years to mature; new mines and oil fields take years to openOutput can be raised in days or weeks by adding shifts or overtime
Unused (spare) capacityLand is usually fully in use; farms cannot quickly expandFactories often have spare capacity
Mobility of factors of productionLand is fixed and specific (land for coffee cannot quickly grow something else); specialist mining equipmentWorkers and machinery can more easily be moved between product lines
Ability to storeMany farm products are perishable, so stocks cannot be held to release when prices riseMost goods can be stored as stock and released quickly
Rate at which costs riseExtra output means using poorer land or deeper deposits, so costs rise quicklyExtra output can often be produced at similar cost

Consequences: because both PED and PES are low for primary commodities, any shift in demand or supply causes large price fluctuations and unstable incomes for farmers and for commodity-dependent countries. This makes planning and investment difficult and can cause government revenue to swing. Responses include buffer stock schemes, minimum (support) prices, commodity agreements and diversification into manufacturing and services.

Real-world example: World coffee prices roughly doubled between 2020 and 2022 after frost and drought in Brazil. New coffee trees take three to four years to produce a crop (inelastic supply), and coffee drinkers kept buying (inelastic demand).

Exam tip: For “why are commodity prices volatile?”, draw a diagram with steep (inelastic) demand and supply and show a shift: the steeper the curves, the bigger the price change.

Key terms

Price elasticity of demand (PED)
The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.
Income elasticity of demand (YED)
The responsiveness of demand to a change in income.
Price elasticity of supply (PES)
The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.
Total revenue
Price multiplied by quantity sold.
Unit elastic
Elasticity equal to one: the percentage change in quantity equals the percentage change in price.
Luxury good
A normal good with YED greater than one.
Primary product
A product of agriculture, fishing, forestry or mining.
Engel’s law
As income rises, the proportion of income spent on food falls.
Sectoral structure
The shares of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors in a country’s output and employment.
Commodity-dependent country
A country that relies on primary commodities for most of its export earnings.
Exam-style question

Explain why governments might choose to impose indirect taxes on goods with price inelastic demand. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define PED, inelastic demand and indirect tax.
  • Draw a tax diagram with steep (inelastic) demand showing large tax revenue and small fall in quantity.
  • Explain: high revenue, most of the burden falls on consumers, small fall in consumption.

Model answer: When demand is price inelastic, a tax that raises price causes a smaller percentage fall in quantity demanded. On a diagram with a steep demand curve, the supply curve shifts up by the tax and quantity falls only slightly, so tax revenue (tax per unit × new quantity) is large. Governments therefore tax fuel, tobacco and alcohol to raise revenue. Most of the tax burden falls on consumers. However, because quantity falls little, such taxes are less effective at reducing consumption.

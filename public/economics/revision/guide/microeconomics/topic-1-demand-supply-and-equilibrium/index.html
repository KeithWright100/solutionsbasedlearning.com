1. Demand, supply and competitive market equilibrium

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 2: Microeconomics · Topics · Topic 1 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how demand and supply determine price and quantity in a competitive market, and how the price mechanism allocates resources efficiently.

Syllabus Statement

2.1 Demand; 2.2 Supply; 2.3 Competitive market equilibrium: the law of demand and supply, non-price determinants, movements and shifts, equilibrium, the signalling and incentive functions of price, consumer and producer surplus, allocative efficiency (MB = MC). HL: assumptions behind the laws of demand and supply; calculating consumer and producer surplus from a diagram.

Success criterion 1

Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given time period. The law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (an inverse relationship). Market demand is the sum of all individual demands.

Non-price determinantEffect on demand
IncomeNormal goods: demand rises with income. Inferior goods (e.g. bus travel, own-brand food): demand falls as income rises.
Prices of related goodsSubstitutes (tea and coffee): a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other. Complements (printers and ink): a rise in the price of one reduces demand for the other.
Tastes and preferencesFashion, advertising, health information.
Future price expectationsIf prices are expected to rise, demand rises now.
Number of consumersPopulation size and age structure.

Exam tip: A change in the good’s own price causes a movement along the curve (a change in quantity demanded). Anything else causes a shift of the curve (a change in demand). Using the right words earns marks.

Success criterion 2

Supply

Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at each price in a given time period. The law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus, because higher prices make production more profitable and can cover rising marginal costs.

  • Costs of factors of production (wages, raw materials, energy): higher costs shift supply left.
  • Technology: better technology lowers costs and shifts supply right.
  • Prices of related goods: joint supply (beef and leather) and competitive supply (a farmer choosing wheat or maize).
  • Expectations, indirect taxes and subsidies, the number of firms, and supply shocks such as bad weather, war or natural disasters.

Real-world example: After Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, the supply of wheat and sunflower oil on world markets fell sharply, shifting supply left and pushing up world prices.

Success criterion 3

Equilibrium and the price mechanism

A market is in equilibrium where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: there is no tendency for the price to change. If the price is above equilibrium there is excess supply (a surplus), so firms cut prices; below it there is excess demand (a shortage), so prices are bid up.

Prices ration scarce goods and allocate resources through their signalling and incentive functions:

  • Rationing: when a good becomes scarcer its price rises, so only those willing and able to pay obtain it.
  • Signalling: a rising price signals to producers that consumers want more and to consumers that the good is becoming scarcer.
  • Incentive: a higher price gives producers an incentive to supply more (and consumers an incentive to buy less), so resources move towards the goods society values most.

Exam tip: In a Paper 2 data question, always show the shift, the new equilibrium and both old and new price and quantity, and explain the excess demand or supply created at the old price.

Success criterion 4

Consumer surplus, producer surplus and allocative efficiency

Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay (the area below the demand curve and above the price). Producer surplus is the difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they would accept (the area above the supply curve and below the price).

The demand curve shows marginal benefit (MB) and the supply curve marginal cost (MC). At the competitive equilibrium MB = MC, so social (community) surplus — consumer plus producer surplus — is maximised. This is allocative efficiency: resources are allocated to produce the combination of goods society values most. Any other output creates a welfare loss.

Success criterion 5

HLAssumptions and calculating surplus

Why demand slopes down: the law of diminishing marginal utility (each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so consumers will only buy more at a lower price), the income effect (a lower price raises real income) and the substitution effect (the good becomes cheaper relative to substitutes).

Why supply slopes up: the law of diminishing marginal returns — in the short run, as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, extra output eventually falls — so marginal costs increase and firms need a higher price to supply more.

HLCalculating surplus from a diagram

Consumer and producer surplus are triangles, so use ½ × base × height.

Example: the demand curve meets the price axis at $20, the supply curve meets it at $2, and equilibrium is at P = $8, Q = 60 units. Consumer surplus = ½ × 60 × (20 − 8) = $360. Producer surplus = ½ × 60 × (8 − 2) = $180. Social surplus = $540.

Exam tip: The current syllabus does not include linear demand and supply functions (equations such as Qd = a − bP); surplus is calculated from a diagram.

Key terms

Demand
The quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given time period.
Supply
The quantity of a good producers are willing and able to sell at each price in a given time period.
Normal good
A good for which demand rises when income rises.
Inferior good
A good for which demand falls when income rises.
Substitutes
Goods that can be used in place of each other.
Complements
Goods that are used together.
Equilibrium
The price and quantity at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
Consumer surplus
The difference between the price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay.
Producer surplus
The difference between the price producers receive and the lowest price they would accept.
Allocative efficiency
Output where MB = MC, so social surplus is maximised.
Diminishing marginal utility

Exam-style question

Using a demand and supply diagram, explain how the price mechanism would respond to a large fall in the supply of coffee beans after a drought in Brazil. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define supply, equilibrium and the price mechanism.
  • Draw a leftward shift of supply, showing the shortage at the original price and the new equilibrium.
  • Explain the signalling and incentive functions: higher price rations the coffee and encourages supply from elsewhere.

Model answer: A drought shifts the supply of coffee beans left from S1 to S2. At the original price there is now a shortage, so buyers bid the price up. The higher price signals scarcity: consumers reduce quantity demanded (moving up D) and switch to substitutes such as tea, while producers in other countries have an incentive to supply more. A new equilibrium is reached at a higher price and lower quantity, so the price mechanism has rationed the reduced supply without any government action.

4. The role of government in microeconomics

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 2: Microeconomics · Topics · Topic 4 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand why governments intervene in markets and the effects of price controls, indirect taxes and subsidies on markets and stakeholders.

Syllabus Statement

2.7 Role of government in microeconomics: reasons for intervention; price controls (price ceilings and floors) and their consequences; indirect taxes (specific and ad valorem) and subsidies, their impact on markets and stakeholders; direct provision; command and control regulation; consumer nudges (HL). HL: calculating the effects on markets and stakeholders.

Success criterion 1

Why governments intervene

  • To earn revenue (indirect taxes on goods).
  • To support firms (subsidies to help an industry or exporters).
  • To support households on low incomes (price ceilings on rent or food, subsidies on necessities).
  • To influence the level of production and consumption (taxes on demerit goods, subsidies on merit goods).
  • To correct market failure (see the next topic).
  • To promote equity.
Success criterion 2

Price controls

StakeholderMaximum price (ceiling)Minimum price (floor)
ConsumersThose who can buy pay less; others go without; quality may fallPay more and buy less
ProducersReceive a lower price; sell less; may leave the marketReceive a higher price; may overproduce
GovernmentMay need to ration or subsidise; parallel marketsMust buy and store the surplus, at an opportunity cost
WorkersJobs may be lost as output fallsMinimum wage: higher pay for some, unemployment for others
WelfareDeadweight loss; underallocationOverallocation of resources; welfare loss

Real-world example: Many cities have rent controls. Economists argue that, over time, controls reduce the supply and quality of rental housing; supporters say they protect tenants from steep rises.

Exam tip: Always explain the shortage or surplus with the quantities Qd and Qs from your diagram, then discuss at least two stakeholders.

Success criterion 3

Indirect taxes and subsidies

An indirect tax is either specific (a fixed amount per unit, so the new supply curve is parallel) or ad valorem (a percentage of price, so the gap widens as price rises). The incidence of the tax depends on elasticity: if demand is more inelastic than supply, consumers pay most of the tax.

A subsidy lowers producers’ costs. Consumers pay a lower price and buy more; producers receive more per unit; the government pays subsidy × quantity, which has an opportunity cost. Subsidies may protect inefficient firms and are hard to remove.

Success criterion 4

Other forms of intervention

  • Direct provision: the government provides services itself, such as schools, healthcare, roads and parks, funded through taxation.
  • Command and control regulation: laws that ban or limit activities (smoking bans, minimum ages, pollution limits) with penalties for breaking them.
  • HLConsumer nudges: changing choice architecture to steer behaviour (calorie labels on menus, healthy food at eye level, default options).
Success criterion 5

HLCalculating the effects of intervention

HLWorked example: specific tax

Before the tax: P = $5, Q = 1000. A tax of $3 per unit is imposed. New consumer price Pc = $7, producer price Pp = $4, Q = 700.

Consumer expenditure: before 5 × 1000 = $5000; after 7 × 700 = $4900. Producer revenue after tax: 4 × 700 = $2800. Government revenue: 3 × 700 = $2100.

Consumers pay $2 of the tax per unit ($1400 total); producers pay $1 per unit ($700). Welfare loss = ½ × 3 × (1000 − 700) = $450.

HLWorked example: price floor

Equilibrium P = $2, Q = 50 million kg. A minimum price of $3 gives Qd = 40m and Qs = 70m. Surplus = 30m kg. If the government buys it: cost = 3 × 30m = $90m. Farmers’ revenue = 3 × 70m = $210m (was $100m).

Key terms

Price ceiling (maximum price)
A legal maximum price set below equilibrium.
Price floor (minimum price)
A legal minimum price set above equilibrium.
Indirect tax
A tax on spending on goods and services, paid to the government by producers.
Specific tax
A fixed amount of tax per unit.
Ad valorem tax
A tax that is a percentage of price.
Subsidy
A payment from the government to producers to lower costs and increase supply.
Tax incidence
How the burden of a tax is shared between consumers and producers.
Parallel (black) market
Illegal trade at prices above a legal maximum.
Deadweight (welfare) loss
The loss of social surplus when output is not at the allocatively efficient level.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of maximum prices to make food affordable for low-income households. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define maximum price; diagram showing shortage and welfare loss.
  • Stakeholders: consumers who buy gain, those who cannot lose; farmers lose revenue; government faces rationing and parallel markets.
  • Evaluation: short v long run (supply falls further), alternatives such as subsidies or cash transfers, size of the gap below equilibrium, real-world example.

Model answer: A maximum price below equilibrium lowers the price for consumers who can obtain the good, but quantity supplied falls and a shortage appears. Some low-income households may be better off, but others go without, queues and parallel markets develop, and farmers earn less so supply may fall further in the long run. Targeted subsidies or cash transfers may help the poor without creating shortages, though they cost the government more.

Past paper questions

11 IB questions on this topic

These are real IB questions from May 2022 and May 2023, reworded here as a summary. Paper 2 and Paper 3 questions use texts and data from the paper, so ask your teacher for a copy of the paper before you answer. Write your answer, then check it against the tips.

  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(b)(ii)Sketch a minimum wage diagram showing unemployment.[3]
    How to answer
    • Labour market with a wage floor above equilibrium; label the unemployment.
  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q2(c)Using demand and supply, explain the effect of a subsidy on the biodiesel market.[4]
    How to answer
    • Supply shifts down by the subsidy; lower price, higher output; government cost.
  • May 2023 · P2 SL · Q2(b)(iii)Sketch a diagram showing unemployment caused by a minimum wage.[3]
    How to answer
    • Labour market: minimum wage above equilibrium; label Qd and Qs of labour and the unemployment gap.
  • May 2023 · P2 SL · Q2(e)Using demand and supply, explain how subsidies keep food prices low.[4]
    How to answer
    • Supply shifts right (down by the subsidy); price falls and quantity rises.
  • May 2023 · P3 HL · Q1(a)(iii)Calculate the producer’s revenue per unit after tax.[2]
    How to answer
    • Price paid minus the tax per unit (careful with percentage taxes).
  • May 2022 · P1 SL · Q1(a)Explain one policy a government could use to help firms and one it could use to make outcomes fairer (equity).[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Pick two clearly different policies, e.g. a subsidy or price floor to help firms, and a price ceiling, progressive tax or direct provision for equity.
    • Draw a diagram for each policy; only covering one aim caps the mark well below full marks.
  • May 2022 · P1 SL · Q1(b)Evaluate how an indirect tax on a particular good affects different stakeholders, using real examples.[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Tax diagram: supply shifts up, price rises, quantity falls; show tax revenue and the consumer and producer shares.
    • Cover several stakeholders: consumers, producers, workers, government, society.
    • Evaluate using PED and PES, regressive effects, specific v ad valorem, and whether it corrects an externality (e.g. a sugar tax).
  • May 2022 · P2 HL · Q2(f)Using demand and supply, explain how raising a maximum price changes the welfare loss.[4]
    How to answer
    • Draw both ceilings below equilibrium; the higher ceiling is closer to equilibrium, so the shortage and welfare loss triangle shrink.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(a)(ii)On a given diagram, draw the supply curve without the indirect taxes.[2]
    How to answer
    • Shift the supply curve down by the tax per unit.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(a)(iii)Calculate indirect tax revenue from a diagram.[2]
    How to answer
    • Tax per unit × quantity sold after the tax.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q2(a)(iv)Calculate the loss of consumer surplus caused by indirect taxes.[2]
    How to answer
    • The trapezium between the two prices: rectangle plus triangle, or ½ × (Q1 + Q2) × ΔP.

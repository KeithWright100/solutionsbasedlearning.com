2. HL: Critique of rational choice, behavioural economics and business objectives

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 2: Microeconomics · Topics · Topic 2 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand the assumptions of rational consumer choice, how behavioural economics challenges them, how nudges work, and the objectives firms pursue other than profit.

Syllabus Statement

2.4 Critique of the maximizing behaviour of consumers and producers (HL only): rational consumer choice; behavioural economics — biases, bounded rationality, bounded self-control, bounded selfishness, imperfect information; choice architecture (default, restricted and mandated choices) and nudge theory; business objectives — profit maximisation, corporate social responsibility, market share, satisficing, growth.

Success criterion 1

HLRational consumer choice

Standard theory assumes that consumers are rational: they have consistent preferences, they have perfect information, and they aim to maximise utility (satisfaction) from their limited income. Firms are assumed to maximise profit. These assumptions lie behind the demand curve and much of microeconomics.

Exam tip: In evaluation, the assumptions of rationality are a common target: if consumers are not rational, market outcomes may not be efficient, which can justify intervention.

Success criterion 2

HLBehavioural economics

Behavioural economics combines psychology and economics to explain how real people make decisions. It finds that people often depart from rationality in predictable ways.

Success criterion 3

HLChoice architecture and nudge theory

Choice architecture is the way choices are presented. Because presentation affects behaviour, governments and firms can design choices to influence decisions.

  • Default choice: an option that applies unless the person actively changes it (automatic enrolment in workplace pensions; opt-out organ donation).
  • Restricted choice: the number of options is limited to make decisions easier (a limited set of approved pension funds).
  • Mandated choice: people are required to make a decision (stating your organ donor preference when renewing a driving licence).

A nudge (Thaler and Sunstein) is any aspect of choice architecture that changes behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives. Nudges are cheap and preserve freedom of choice, but their effects may be small or fade over time, and they can be used by firms to manipulate consumers.

Real-world example: When the UK introduced automatic enrolment into workplace pensions (from 2012), participation among eligible private-sector employees rose from under half to over 85%, because staying in became the default.

Success criterion 4

HLBusiness objectives

Traditional theory assumes firms maximise profit (produce where MC = MR). In practice firms may pursue other objectives:

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): acting ethically towards workers, communities and the environment, which may build reputation and long-term profit.
  • Market share: aiming to dominate the market, sometimes accepting lower profits in the short run (e.g. low prices to win customers).
  • Satisficing: making “enough” profit to keep shareholders happy while meeting other goals, often because managers and owners have different aims.
  • Growth: increasing size or revenue, which may give managers status and economies of scale.
  • Revenue maximisation: where MR = 0, often linked to managers’ bonuses based on sales.

Key terms

Rational consumer choice
The assumption that consumers have consistent preferences and perfect information and maximise utility.
Behavioural economics
A branch of economics that uses psychology to explain how people actually make decisions.
Bounded rationality
Rationality limited by information, time and cognitive ability.
Choice architecture
The design of the way choices are presented to people.
Nudge
A change in choice architecture that alters behaviour without banning options or changing incentives significantly.
Satisficing
Aiming for a satisfactory level of profit rather than the maximum.
Corporate social responsibility
A firm taking responsibility for the effects of its activities on society and the environment.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of nudges by governments to change consumer behaviour. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define nudge and choice architecture; explain why consumers may not act rationally.
  • Use real examples: default pension enrolment, organ donation, calorie labelling.
  • Evaluate: cheap and freedom-preserving v small or temporary effects, ethical concerns about manipulation, need for stronger measures such as taxes.

Model answer: Nudges work because consumers have bounded rationality and self-control. Making pension saving the default dramatically increased participation in the UK at almost no cost. However, nudges may be less effective for addictive goods, such as tobacco, where taxes and bans have a larger impact; some critics argue nudging is paternalistic; and effects can fade. Nudges are best seen as a complement to, not a replacement for, other policies.

4. Sustainable development and measuring development

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 4: The global economy · Topics · Topic 4 of 5

Learning Intention

To understand the meaning of sustainable development and the SDGs, how economic development is measured, and the relationship between growth and development.

Success criterion 1

Sustainable development and the SDGs

Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987). It has economic, social and environmental dimensions.

The United Nations’ 17 Sustainable Development Goals (adopted 2015, to be met by 2030) include no poverty, zero hunger, good health, quality education, gender equality, clean water, affordable clean energy, decent work and economic growth, reduced inequalities, and climate action. Progress has been uneven, and the COVID-19 pandemic reversed some gains.

Success criterion 2

HLSustainability and poverty

Poverty and environmental damage reinforce each other. Poor households may have to overuse common resources (cutting forests for fuel, over-farming land) to survive, which degrades the environment; environmental degradation and climate change then reduce agricultural yields, water supplies and health, deepening poverty. Poorer countries are often the most exposed to climate change despite emitting the least, and they lack the resources to adapt. Reducing poverty (through access to clean energy, secure land rights and education) can therefore support sustainability.

Success criterion 3

The multidimensional nature of development and single indicators

Economic development is a broad improvement in living standards and well-being: higher incomes, reduced poverty, better health and education, more opportunities and freedom of choice, and greater equity. It is multidimensional and cannot be captured by one figure.

Indicator typeExamples
GDP/GNI per capita at PPPAverage income adjusted for cost of living
HealthLife expectancy at birth, infant and maternal mortality, access to doctors
EducationLiteracy rates, mean and expected years of schooling, enrolment rates
InequalityGini coefficient, share of income of the top 10%
EnergyEnergy use per capita, access to electricity, share of renewables
EnvironmentCO₂ emissions per capita, forest cover, air quality
Success criterion 4

Composite indicators

IndicatorWhat it measuresLimitations
Human Development Index (HDI)Health (life expectancy), education (mean and expected years of schooling), standard of living (GNI per capita at PPP)Ignores inequality, environment and freedom; equal weights are arbitrary
Gender Inequality Index (GII)Reproductive health, empowerment (seats in parliament, education) and labour market participationData gaps; does not cover all forms of inequality
Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI)HDI adjusted for inequality in each dimension; the gap with the HDI shows the loss due to inequalityData on distribution are harder to collect
Happy Planet IndexWell-being and life expectancy relative to ecological footprintSubjective well-being data; rewards low consumption

Real-world example: Some countries rank much higher on GNI per capita than on HDI (for example several oil-rich Gulf states), while others such as Sri Lanka and Cuba rank higher on HDI than their income would suggest, thanks to investment in health and education.

Success criterion 5

Growth and development

Economic growth can support development: higher incomes reduce poverty and provide tax revenue for health, education and infrastructure. But growth does not guarantee development if the gains are captured by a small elite, if growth comes from extracting natural resources with few links to the rest of the economy, or if it damages the environment. Development can also happen with relatively slow growth if resources are used well. The relationship works both ways: healthier, better-educated people are more productive, so development supports growth.

Exam tip: Paper 2 often gives you a table of development indicators for two countries. Compare them explicitly and explain what the differences suggest; do not just repeat the numbers.

Key terms

Sustainable development
Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
Seventeen UN goals for 2030 covering poverty, health, education, equality and the environment.
Economic development
A broad improvement in living standards, well-being and freedom, including reduced poverty and inequality.
Human Development Index (HDI)
A composite index of health, education and income.
Gender Inequality Index (GII)
A composite index of gender inequality in reproductive health, empowerment and the labour market.
IHDI
The HDI adjusted for inequality.
Composite indicator
An indicator that combines several measures into a single figure.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of the Human Development Index as a measure of economic development. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define economic development and HDI, including its three dimensions.
  • Strengths: broader than GDP, widely available, allows ranking and comparison over time.
  • Limitations: averages hide inequality (IHDI), ignores environment, gender (GII), freedom; arbitrary weights; real examples of countries ranked differently by income and HDI.

Model answer: The HDI combines life expectancy, years of schooling and GNI per capita at PPP, so it captures more of what development means than income alone and allows comparisons over time and between countries. However, it uses national averages, so it hides inequality: the IHDI shows how much human development is lost to inequality. It also ignores environmental sustainability, gender inequality and political freedom, and the equal weights are a value judgement. HDI is therefore a useful but incomplete measure, best used alongside the IHDI, GII and single indicators.

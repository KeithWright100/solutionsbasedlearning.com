3. Exchange rates and the balance of payments

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 4: The global economy · Topics · Topic 3 of 5

Learning Intention

To understand how exchange rates are determined in floating, fixed and managed systems, the consequences of exchange rate changes, and the structure of the balance of payments.

Syllabus Statement

4.5 Exchange rates: floating (determination, appreciation and depreciation, causes of changes), consequences of changes; fixed (devaluation, revaluation, how maintained); managed (overvalued, undervalued); HL: fixed v floating. 4.6 Balance of payments: current, capital and financial accounts; interdependence. HL: current account and exchange rate; persistent deficits and surpluses; correcting a deficit; Marshall–Lerner and the J-curve.

Success criterion 1

Floating exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. In a floating system it is determined by the demand for and supply of the currency on the foreign exchange market. A rise in its value is an appreciation; a fall is a depreciation.

Success criterion 3

Fixed and managed exchange rates

In a fixed exchange rate system the government or central bank sets the currency’s value against another currency (often the US dollar). A deliberate fall in the fixed rate is a devaluation; a rise is a revaluation. To maintain the rate when it is under pressure, the central bank can buy or sell its own currency using foreign currency reserves, change interest rates, or use exchange controls.

In a managed system (managed float) the currency floats but the central bank intervenes to keep it within a range or to prevent sharp movements. A currency may be kept overvalued (above its market rate) to keep import prices low, or undervalued to make exports competitive.

Real-world example: Hong Kong has pegged its dollar to the US dollar since 1983 within a narrow band of HK$7.75–7.85. Saudi Arabia and several Gulf states also peg to the US dollar.

Success criterion 4

The balance of payments

The balance of payments records all transactions between a country’s residents and the rest of the world over a period. Money flowing in is a credit (+); money flowing out is a debit (−).

AccountComponents
Current accountBalance of trade in goods; balance of trade in services; income (wages, interest, profit and dividends earned abroad minus those paid abroad; also called primary income); current transfers (such as remittances and aid; also called secondary income)
Capital accountCapital transfers (such as debt forgiveness); purchase and sale of non-produced, non-financial assets (such as patents, land for embassies)
Financial accountForeign direct investment; portfolio investment (shares and bonds); reserve assets; official borrowing

Because every transaction has two sides, the balance of payments always balances: current account + capital account + financial account (+ errors and omissions) = 0. A current account deficit must be matched by a surplus on the capital and financial accounts — for example, by borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners.

ExampleExports of goods $300bn, imports of goods $350bn, services balance +$40bn, net income −$5bn, net current transfers −$10bn. Current account = (300 − 350) + 40 − 5 − 10 = −$25bn: a deficit of $25bn.
Success criterion 5

HLDeficits, surpluses, Marshall–Lerner and the J-curve

A current account deficit increases the supply of the currency (to pay for imports), causing depreciation; a deficit financed by inflows on the financial account (for example high interest rates attracting hot money) can keep the currency strong for a time.

Implications of a persistent current account deficit: downward pressure on the exchange rate; the need for higher interest rates to attract finance; increased foreign ownership of domestic assets; rising foreign debt; lower credit ratings; pressure for contractionary demand management; and slower growth.

Methods to correct a persistent deficit: expenditure switching (depreciation or devaluation, protection — switching spending from imports to domestic goods), expenditure reducing (contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to cut spending, including on imports), and supply-side policies (raising productivity and competitiveness in the long run).

Implications of a persistent current account surplus: low domestic consumption and investment (resources go to exports), appreciation of the currency, possible inflation, reduced export competitiveness in the long run, and tension with trading partners in deficit (as with China and Germany).

Key terms

The price of one currency in terms of another.
Appreciation
A rise in the value of a currency in a floating system.
Depreciation
A fall in the value of a currency in a floating system.
Devaluation
A deliberate fall in the value of a fixed currency.
Revaluation
A deliberate rise in the value of a fixed currency.
Managed exchange rate
A floating rate with periodic central bank intervention.
Balance of payments
A record of all transactions between a country’s residents and the rest of the world.
Current account
Trade in goods and services, plus primary and secondary income.
Financial account
Flows of investment (FDI, portfolio), reserve assets and official borrowing.
Marshall–Lerner condition
HL: a depreciation improves the current account only if PEDx + PEDm > 1.
J-curve
HL: the current account worsens before it improves after a depreciation.
Expenditure switching
HL: policies that switch spending from imports to domestic goods.
Expenditure reducing
HL: policies that reduce total spending, including on imports.
Exam-style question

Explain two factors that could cause a country’s currency to depreciate in a floating exchange rate system. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define floating exchange rate and depreciation.
  • Draw a currency market diagram showing a fall in demand or rise in supply.
  • Explain two factors, e.g. lower relative interest rates and higher relative inflation, with the mechanism for each.

Model answer: In a floating system the exchange rate is set by the demand for and supply of the currency. If the central bank cuts interest rates relative to other countries, financial investors move their funds abroad: they sell the currency, so supply shifts right and the currency depreciates. If the country’s inflation rate is higher than its trading partners’, its exports become less competitive and imports more attractive; demand for the currency falls and supply rises, so again it depreciates.

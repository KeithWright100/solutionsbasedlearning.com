1. International trade and trade protection

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 4: The global economy · Topics · Topic 1 of 5

Learning Intention

To understand the benefits of international trade, the effects of tariffs, quotas, subsidies and administrative barriers, and the arguments for and against protection.

Syllabus Statement

4.1 Benefits of international trade (HL: absolute and comparative advantage). 4.2 Types of trade protection: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, administrative barriers, and their effects on markets and stakeholders (HL: calculations). 4.3 Arguments for and against trade control/protection; free trade versus trade protection.

Success criterion 1

The benefits of trade

  • Increased competition, which lowers prices and increases efficiency.
  • Greater choice for consumers.
  • Access to resources a country lacks (oil, minerals, technology).
  • More foreign exchange earnings and access to larger markets, allowing economies of scale.
  • More efficient resource allocation as countries specialise in what they do best.
Success criterion 2

HLAbsolute and comparative advantage

A country has an absolute advantage if it can produce more of a good than another country with the same resources. It has a comparative advantage if it can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost. David Ricardo showed that both countries can gain from specialising according to comparative advantage and trading, even if one country has an absolute advantage in both goods.

HLFinding comparative advantage
Wheat (max)Cloth (max)Opp. cost of 1 wheatOpp. cost of 1 cloth
Country A840.5 cloth2 wheat
Country B482 cloth0.5 wheat

A has the comparative advantage in wheat, B in cloth. Any terms of trade between 0.5 and 2 cloth per wheat benefit both.

Sources of comparative advantage: differences in factor endowments (land, climate, labour, capital), technology and skills. Limitations of the theory: it assumes constant costs, no transport costs, perfect mobility of factors within countries and immobility between them, perfect competition and full employment; specialisation can leave countries dependent on a few products and vulnerable to shocks.

Success criterion 3

Tariffs, quotas, subsidies and administrative barriers

Administrative barriers: health, safety and environmental standards, complicated customs procedures and paperwork, and product regulations that make it harder for foreign firms to sell. They can be legitimate (protecting consumers) or disguised protectionism.

Real-world example: In 2018 the USA imposed tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminium (10%) and on many Chinese goods; China retaliated with tariffs on US soybeans and other exports. Further rounds of tariffs from 2025 raised average US tariff rates to their highest level in decades.

Exam tip: Free trade v protection questions reward stakeholder analysis. Say who gains, who loses and whether the gain is short-term or long-term.

Success criterion 5

HLCalculating the effects of protection

HLTariff calculation

World price $10; tariff $4 per unit, so the domestic price becomes $14. Before: domestic supply 20, demand 80, imports 60. After: domestic supply 35, demand 65, imports 30.

Government tariff revenue = 4 × 30 = $120. Import expenditure falls from 10 × 60 = $600 to 10 × 30 = $300 (paid to foreign producers). Domestic producer revenue rises from 10 × 20 = $200 to 14 × 35 = $490.

Welfare loss = production triangle ½ × 15 × 4 = $30 plus consumption triangle ½ × 15 × 4 = $30, so $60 in total.

Key terms

Free trade
International trade without government restrictions.
Absolute advantage
HL: the ability to produce more of a good than another country using the same resources.
Comparative advantage
HL: the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods.
Quota
A physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported.
Administrative barriers
Rules, standards and procedures that make importing more difficult.
Dumping
Selling goods abroad at a price below their cost of production.
Infant industry
A new industry that cannot yet compete with established foreign firms.
Exam-style question

Using a tariff diagram, explain the effects of a tariff on different stakeholders. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define tariff and stakeholders.
  • Draw a tariff diagram: Pw and Pw + t, domestic supply and demand, imports before and after, revenue and welfare loss areas.
  • Explain the effect on consumers, domestic producers, foreign producers and the government.

Model answer: A tariff is a tax on imports. It raises the domestic price from Pw to Pw + t. Domestic consumers pay more and consume less (Q2 to Q4), losing consumer surplus. Domestic producers expand output from Q1 to Q3 and gain producer surplus. Imports fall from Q2 − Q1 to Q4 − Q3, so foreign producers sell less. The government gains tariff revenue equal to the tariff per unit times the new quantity of imports. Overall there is a welfare loss, because higher-cost domestic production replaces cheaper imports and some consumption is lost.

Past paper questions

6 IB questions on this topic

These are real IB questions from May 2022 and May 2023, reworded here as a summary. Paper 2 and Paper 3 questions use texts and data from the paper, so ask your teacher for a copy of the paper before you answer. Write your answer, then check it against the tips.

  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q2(e)Using a trade diagram, explain the effect of an EU tariff on imported biodiesel.[4]
    How to answer
    • Tariff diagram from the EU’s side: higher price, fewer imports from Indonesia.
  • May 2023 · P2 SL · Q2(f)Using a trade diagram, explain the effect of removing tariffs on imported fertiliser.[4]
    How to answer
    • Price falls to the world price; domestic production falls, consumption and imports rise; tariff revenue is lost.
  • May 2022 · P1 HL · Q3(a)Explain the limitations of the theory of comparative advantage as a basis for specialisation and trade.[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Start with a linear PPC diagram showing comparative advantage, then explain the unrealistic assumptions.
    • Limitations: transport costs, trade barriers, imperfect factor mobility, increasing costs, over-specialisation and vulnerability, ignores externalities.
  • May 2022 · P2 HL · Q1(d)Using a PPC showing comparative advantage, explain why each country exports the good it does.[4]
    How to answer
    • Draw two linear PPCs with different slopes; explain lower opportunity cost for each good.
  • May 2022 · P2 HL · Q2(a)(ii)Define tariffs.[2]
    How to answer
    • Give a precise, textbook-style definition: two marks need the full meaning, not an example.
  • May 2022 · P2 SL · Q1(f)Using a trade diagram, explain how removing import quotas affects domestic wine production.[4]
    How to answer
    • Start with the quota diagram and show the price falling to the world price when the quota goes.
    • Domestic production falls and imports rise.

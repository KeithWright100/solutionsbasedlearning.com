5. Barriers to and strategies for growth and development

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 4: The global economy · Topics · Topic 5 of 5

Learning Intention

To understand the economic, political and social barriers to growth and development and to evaluate strategies to overcome them.

Syllabus Statement

4.9 Barriers to economic growth and/or economic development: poverty traps/cycles; economic barriers (inequality, infrastructure, human capital, primary sector dependence, access to markets, informal economy, capital flight, indebtedness, geography, climate and disease); political and social barriers (institutions, gender inequality, corruption, unequal power). 4.10 Strategies: trade strategies, diversification, social enterprise, market-based and interventionist policies, merit goods, FDI, foreign aid, multilateral assistance, institutional change; government intervention v market-oriented approaches; progress towards the SDGs.

Success criterion 1

Poverty traps

A poverty trap is a self-reinforcing cycle that keeps people or countries poor. Breaking it requires an injection from outside the cycle: aid, FDI, remittances, government investment in health and education, or access to credit (microfinance).

Success criterion 2

Barriers to growth and development

TypeBarriers
EconomicRising inequality; lack of infrastructure and appropriate technology; low human capital (poor health and education); dependence on primary products (volatile prices, low YED, declining terms of trade); lack of access to international markets (trade barriers in rich countries); a large informal economy (low tax revenue, no legal protection); capital flight; high debt; landlocked geography (high transport costs); tropical climate and endemic diseases
Political and socialWeak institutions: unreliable legal systems, ineffective tax collection, weak banking systems, insecure property rights; gender inequality; corruption and poor governance; unequal political power and status; conflict

Real-world example: Many sub-Saharan African economies earn most of their export revenue from one or two commodities (Zambia: copper; Nigeria: oil). When world prices fall, government revenue and the currency fall too.

Exam tip: For the significance of barriers, rank them for a specific country. Corruption and weak institutions are often seen as root causes, because they undermine every other strategy.

Success criterion 3

Trade strategies, market-based policies and FDI

StrategyMeaningStrengths and limitations
Import substitutionProtect domestic industry to replace imports (Latin America, 1950s–70s)Builds an industrial base; but inefficiency, lack of competition, high costs, foreign exchange shortages
Export promotionEncourage export industries (the East Asian “tigers”)Access to large markets, economies of scale, foreign exchange; but dependence on world demand, risk of protectionism abroad
Economic integrationJoin trading blocs (AfCFTA)Larger markets; but trade diversion and unequal gains
DiversificationReduce reliance on a few primary productsMore stable revenue; but needs capital, skills and time
Market-based policiesTrade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulationEfficiency and investment; but inequality, loss of public control, short-term job losses

Inward foreign direct investment by multinational corporations (MNCs) brings capital, technology, jobs, skills and tax revenue, and links firms to global markets. But MNCs may repatriate profits, use transfer pricing to avoid tax, pay low wages, damage the environment, and outcompete local firms; governments may offer costly incentives to attract them.

A social enterprise is a business whose main aim is social or environmental, with profits reinvested in that mission (for example businesses selling affordable solar lamps or clean cookstoves).

Success criterion 4

Interventionist strategies, aid and institutional change

  • Interventionist policies: redistribution through progressive taxes, transfer payments and minimum wages; provision of merit goods — education, health programmes, and infrastructure including energy, transport, telecommunications, clean water and sanitation.
  • Foreign aid: humanitarian aid (emergency relief after disasters and conflict) and development aid (long-term projects); Official Development Assistance (ODA) from governments; aid from NGOs; debt relief (e.g. the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries Initiative). Aid can fill savings and foreign exchange gaps but may be tied, create dependency, be lost to corruption, or reflect donor interests.
  • Multilateral development assistance: the World Bank lends for long-term development projects; the IMF lends to countries with balance of payments crises, usually with conditions (fiscal austerity, liberalisation) that have been criticised for harming the poor.
  • Institutional change: improved access to banking, including microfinance (small loans to people without collateral, such as the Grameen Bank) and mobile banking (Kenya’s M-Pesa); increasing women’s empowerment; reducing corruption; secure property and land rights.

Success criterion 5

Evaluating approaches to development

ApproachStrengthsLimitations
Market-orientedEfficiency, competition and incentives; attracts investment; lower burden on government financesCan widen inequality; underprovision of merit and public goods; market failures; vulnerability to global shocks
InterventionistCorrects market failure; provides merit goods and infrastructure; reduces inequalityGovernment failure: inefficiency, corruption, high debt; can crowd out the private sector

Most successful developers, such as South Korea and China, combined market forces with strong state intervention (investment in education and infrastructure, industrial policy, export promotion). The best mix depends on the country’s institutions, resources and stage of development. When assessing progress towards the SDGs, compare two or more countries using data on poverty, health, education, gender and the environment.

Key terms

Poverty trap
A self-reinforcing cycle of factors that keeps people or countries in poverty.
Capital flight
The outflow of financial capital from a country, often due to uncertainty or instability.
Import substitution
A strategy of replacing imports with domestic production behind trade barriers.
Export promotion
A strategy of growth driven by expanding exports.
Diversification
Widening the range of goods and services an economy produces and exports.
Foreign direct investment (FDI)
Long-term investment by a firm in productive capacity in another country.
Official Development Assistance (ODA)
Aid given by governments of high-income countries to promote development.
Microfinance
Small loans and other financial services for people on low incomes without collateral.
Social enterprise
A business with a mainly social or environmental purpose that reinvests its profits in that purpose.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the effectiveness of foreign direct investment in promoting economic development. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define FDI, MNC and economic development; diagram (AD/AS or LRAS shift; poverty cycle broken by injection).
  • Benefits: capital, technology, jobs, skills, tax revenue, exports.
  • Costs: profit repatriation, tax avoidance, environmental damage, low wages, enclave economies; evaluate with real examples (e.g. Ireland, Vietnam, mining in Africa).

Model answer: FDI provides capital that poor countries lack, breaking the savings gap in the poverty cycle; it creates jobs, transfers technology and skills, and raises tax revenue and exports. Vietnam’s growth since the 1990s has been driven by FDI in electronics manufacturing, with large falls in poverty. However, MNCs may repatriate most profits, minimise tax through transfer pricing, use capital-intensive methods that create few jobs, and in extractive industries form enclaves with few links to the local economy. The impact depends on the sector, the strength of institutions and the terms negotiated.

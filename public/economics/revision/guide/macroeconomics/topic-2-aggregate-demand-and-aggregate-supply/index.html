2. Variations in economic activity: aggregate demand and aggregate supply

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 2 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand the components and determinants of AD, the short-run and long-run AS curves in the monetarist and Keynesian models, macroeconomic equilibrium and output gaps.

Syllabus Statement

3.2 Variations in economic activity — aggregate demand and aggregate supply: AD and its components and determinants; SRAS and its determinants; LRAS in the monetarist/new classical and Keynesian models; short-run and long-run equilibrium; recessionary (deflationary) and inflationary gaps; shifts in LRAS. HL: the Keynesian multiplier.

Success criterion 1

Aggregate demand

Aggregate demand (AD) is the total spending on goods and services in an economy at a given price level in a given period: AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Consumption is the largest component (around 60% of AD in many economies).

AD slopes downward because, as the average price level rises: (1) the wealth effect — the real value of savings falls, so consumption falls; (2) the interest rate effect — higher prices increase demand for money and interest rates, reducing borrowing; (3) the international trade effect — exports become less competitive and imports more attractive.

Success criterion 2

Determinants of AD

ComponentDeterminants that shift AD
Consumption (C)Consumer confidence, interest rates, wealth, personal income taxes, household debt
Investment (I)Interest rates, business confidence, technology, corporate taxes, level of debt
Government spending (G)Political and economic priorities, fiscal policy
Net exports (X − M)Income abroad, exchange rates, trade policies, relative inflation

Success criterion 3

Aggregate supply: two views

Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) slopes upward: in the short run, wages and some other costs are fixed, so higher prices raise profits and firms supply more. SRAS shifts with changes in costs of production: wages, raw material and energy prices, indirect taxes and subsidies, and supply shocks.

Monetarist / new classical: the LRAS is vertical at potential output (full employment level, where unemployment is at the natural rate). In the long run, wages and prices adjust, so the economy returns to potential output; AD only affects the price level in the long run.

Keynesian: the AS curve has three sections. With large spare capacity (I), output can rise without inflation; as the economy approaches full employment (II), bottlenecks raise costs; at full capacity (III), only prices can rise. Wages are “sticky” downward, so the economy can stay in a deflationary gap for a long time.

Success criterion 4

Equilibrium and output gaps

Long-run potential output grows (LRAS shifts right) when the quantity or quality of factors of production increases: more labour (migration, participation), better education and health (human capital), new technology, investment in capital, institutional change and lower unemployment.

Exam tip: State which model you are using. Examiners accept both, but your explanation must match your diagram. In a 15-mark answer, contrasting the two views is strong evaluation.

Success criterion 5

HLThe Keynesian multiplier

An increase in an injection (I, G or X) causes a larger final increase in national income, because one person’s spending becomes another’s income, part of which is spent again, and so on.

HLThe multiplier

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume and MPS, MPT and MPM are the marginal propensities to save, pay tax and import.

Example: MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.2. Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. If investment rises by $40bn, real GDP rises by 2 × 40 = $80bn.

The multiplier is smaller when leakages are high (open economies with high taxes), and works mainly when there is spare capacity; near full employment it mostly raises prices.

Key terms

Aggregate demand (AD)
Total planned spending on goods and services in an economy at each price level.
Aggregate supply (AS)
The total output firms are willing and able to produce at each price level.
Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)
AS when wages and some other costs are fixed.
Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)
AS when all costs can change; vertical at potential output in the monetarist model.
Potential output
The output the economy can produce when all resources are fully employed (unemployment at the natural rate).
Deflationary (recessionary) gap
Equilibrium real GDP below potential output.
Inflationary gap
Equilibrium real GDP above potential output.
Keynesian multiplier
HL: the ratio of the final change in real GDP to the initial change in an injection.
Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)
HL: the proportion of extra income that is spent on consumption.
Exam-style question

Using an AD/AS diagram, explain how a fall in business confidence could lead to a deflationary gap. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define AD, business confidence and deflationary gap.
  • Draw AD shifting left with SRAS and LRAS (or Keynesian AS); mark the new equilibrium below potential output.
  • Explain the fall in investment, the multiplier effect (HL) and resulting cyclical unemployment.

Model answer: When firms are pessimistic about future sales they cut planned investment, which is a component of AD. AD shifts left from AD1 to AD2. At the original price level there is now excess supply, so firms cut output and the price level falls slightly. The new equilibrium is below potential output (LRAS), so there is a deflationary gap: real GDP is lower and unemployment higher. The fall in investment also reduces incomes, which reduces consumption further (the multiplier).

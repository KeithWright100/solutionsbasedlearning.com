6. Supply-side policies

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 6 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how market-based and interventionist supply-side policies aim to increase potential output, and to evaluate their effectiveness.

Syllabus Statement

3.7 Supply-side policies: goals; interventionist policies (investment in human capital, new technology and infrastructure; industrial policies); market-based policies (policies to encourage competition, labour market reforms, incentive-related policies); effectiveness, including time lags and impact on the budget, equity and the environment.

Success criterion 1

Goals of supply-side policies

Supply-side policies aim to improve the quantity and quality of factors of production and the efficiency of markets, so that potential output grows. Goals: long-term economic growth, lower natural rate of unemployment, lower inflationary pressure, and more competitive exports (improving the current account).

Success criterion 2

Interventionist policies

  • Investment in human capital: education, training, healthcare — raises labour productivity. (Example: Singapore’s SkillsFuture credits for adult retraining.)
  • Investment in new technology: research and development grants, tax credits for R&D, support for start-ups.
  • Investment in infrastructure: transport, energy, broadband — lowers costs for firms.
  • Industrial policies: targeted support for particular industries, such as subsidies for semiconductor production or green energy (the US CHIPS Act 2022; the Inflation Reduction Act).
Success criterion 3

Market-based policies

TypeExamples
Encouraging competitionDeregulation, privatisation, trade liberalisation, anti-monopoly regulation
Labour market reformsReducing the power of trade unions, reducing unemployment benefits, abolishing or lowering minimum wages, making hiring and firing easier
Incentive-related policiesCutting personal income taxes to encourage work, cutting business and capital gains taxes to encourage investment

Success criterion 4

Evaluating supply-side policies

Policy typeStrengthsLimitations
InterventionistLong-term growth without inflation; can target market failures (underinvestment in education, R&D); can reduce inequality and support sustainabilityCostly, adding to the budget deficit; long time lags; risk of “picking losers”; opportunity cost
Market-basedLittle or no cost to the government (privatisation even raises revenue); increase efficiency and competitionCan increase inequality and reduce workers’ protection; environmental risks of deregulation; effect of tax cuts on work effort is uncertain

Many supply-side policies also affect AD: spending on infrastructure or education raises G now, and tax cuts raise C and I. So they can help reduce a deflationary gap in the short run as well as shift LRAS in the long run.

Exam tip: For full marks, link the policy to a specific type of unemployment or to inflation. Supply-side policies are the best answer to structural unemployment.

Key terms

Supply-side policies
Policies that aim to increase potential output by improving the quantity, quality and efficiency of factors of production.
Interventionist supply-side policies
Government action to improve the supply side, such as investment in education, infrastructure and technology.
Market-based supply-side policies
Policies that improve the working of markets, such as deregulation, privatisation and labour market reforms.
Deregulation
Removing rules that restrict competition.
Privatisation
The transfer of ownership of firms from the public to the private sector.
Industrial policy
Government support targeted at specific industries.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of interventionist supply-side policies to achieve long-term economic growth. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define supply-side policies and long-term growth; LRAS shifting right (or Keynesian AS).
  • Explain education, infrastructure and R&D investment.
  • Evaluate: cost and opportunity cost, time lags, effect on AD, equity and sustainability benefits, compare with market-based policies; real example.

Model answer: Investment in education, infrastructure and research increases the quality and quantity of factors of production, shifting LRAS right, so real GDP can grow without inflation. South Korea’s heavy investment in education and technology helped it become a high-income economy. Such policies also tackle market failures, since education and research have positive externalities. However, they are expensive, may raise government debt, and take many years to work; governments may also back the wrong industries. Market-based policies cost less but may raise inequality, so a combination is often best.

Past paper questions

4 IB questions on this topic

These are real IB questions from May 2022 and May 2023, reworded here as a summary. Paper 2 and Paper 3 questions use texts and data from the paper, so ask your teacher for a copy of the paper before you answer. Write your answer, then check it against the tips.

  • May 2022 · P1 SL & HL · Q2(a)Explain how supply-side policies could increase competition at home and make a country more competitive internationally.[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Deal with BOTH goals: domestic competition (deregulation, privatisation, competition law, trade liberalisation) and international competitiveness.
    • LRAS shifts right, lowering the price level, so exports become relatively cheaper.
  • May 2022 · P1 SL & HL · Q2(b)Evaluate whether interventionist supply-side policies are the best way to cut unemployment, using real examples.[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain how training, education, R&D and infrastructure raise capacity and labour demand; draw LRAS (and AD) shifting right.
    • Because the question says "most effective", compare with other policies: market-based supply-side, fiscal and monetary policy.
    • Link the policy to the type of unemployment: supply-side suits structural, demand-side suits cyclical.
  • May 2022 · P2 SL · Q1(c)Using AD/AS, explain the effect of supply-side policies on full employment output.[4]
    How to answer
    • Draw LRAS (or Keynesian AS) shifting right; label Yfe1 and Yfe2.
    • Explain the link from the policy in the text to more or better factors of production.
  • May 2022 · P3 HL · Q1(a)(vii)Sketch AD/AS to show the long-run effect of a cut in corporate tax.[2]
    How to answer
    • LRAS shifts right (more investment); AD may also shift right.

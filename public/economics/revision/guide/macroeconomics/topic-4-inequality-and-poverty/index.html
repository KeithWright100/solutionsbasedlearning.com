4. Economics of inequality and poverty

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 4 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how inequality and poverty are measured, their causes and consequences, and the policies used to reduce them.

Syllabus Statement

3.4 Economics of inequality and poverty: equality v equity; measuring inequality (Lorenz curve, Gini coefficient); poverty (absolute and relative), measurement (income, MPI); causes and consequences of poverty and inequality; the role of taxation (progressive, regressive, proportional; direct and indirect) and other policies. HL: constructing a Lorenz curve; tax calculations.

Success criterion 1

Equality, equity and poverty

  • Equality means everyone has the same (income, wealth or opportunity). Equity means fairness; an equitable distribution need not be equal.
  • Absolute poverty: income below what is needed for basic needs (the World Bank international poverty line of $2.15 a day at 2017 PPP, updated to $3.00 a day in 2025).
  • Relative poverty: income below a level considered acceptable in a particular society, usually 50% or 60% of median income.
  • The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) measures deprivation in health, education and living standards (ten indicators), not just income.
Success criterion 2

Measuring inequality

Income inequality and wealth inequality differ: wealth (the stock of assets) is usually much more unequal than income (a flow). Gini coefficients for income range from about 0.25 in some Nordic and central European countries to over 0.6 in South Africa.

Success criterion 4

Taxation and other policies

Tax systemMeaningExample
ProgressiveThe proportion of income paid in tax rises as income risesIncome tax with rising bands
ProportionalThe same proportion of income is paid at all incomesA flat income tax
RegressiveThe proportion of income paid falls as income risesMost indirect taxes (VAT)

Direct taxes are paid on income or wealth (personal income tax, corporate tax, wealth and inheritance taxes). Indirect taxes are paid on spending.

  • Transfer payments: unemployment benefits, pensions, child benefits, conditional cash transfers (Brazil’s Bolsa Família).
  • Merit goods: free or subsidised education and healthcare improve opportunities.
  • Minimum wages, universal basic income, and policies against discrimination.

Evaluation: high taxes and generous benefits may reduce incentives to work and invest and encourage tax avoidance; universal benefits are expensive; targeted benefits can create poverty traps. Policies that raise human capital take time but tackle root causes.

Success criterion 5

HLCalculations

HLTax calculations

Income tax: 0% on the first $10 000, 20% on $10 001–$40 000, 40% above $40 000. On an income of $60 000: 0 + 0.2 × 30 000 + 0.4 × 20 000 = $14 000.

Average tax rate = total tax ÷ income × 100 = 14 000 ÷ 60 000 = 23.3%. Marginal tax rate = the rate on the next dollar earned = 40%.

Indirect tax: a 15% VAT on a price of $80 before tax = $12 tax. If the price including VAT is $115, the tax = 115 − 115 ÷ 1.15 = $15.

Key terms

Equity
Fairness in the distribution of income, wealth or opportunity.
Absolute poverty
Income below the level needed to meet basic needs.
Relative poverty
Income well below the average in a society.
Lorenz curve
A curve showing the cumulative share of income received by cumulative shares of the population.
Gini coefficient
A measure of inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality).
Progressive tax
A tax that takes a larger proportion of income from people with higher incomes.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a larger proportion of income from people with lower incomes.
Transfer payment
A payment from the government for which no good or service is provided in return.
Marginal tax rate
HL: the rate of tax paid on an additional unit of income.
Average tax rate
HL: total tax paid as a percentage of total income.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of progressive income taxes to reduce income inequality. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define progressive tax and income inequality; Lorenz curve diagram moving towards the line of equality.
  • Explain redistribution and funding of transfers and merit goods.
  • Evaluate: incentives to work, tax avoidance and capital flight, the need for spending as well as taxes, examples (Nordic countries v others).

Model answer: A progressive income tax takes a larger share of income from high earners, so post-tax incomes are less unequal and the Lorenz curve moves towards the line of equality. The revenue can also fund transfer payments and merit goods, which raise the incomes and opportunities of the poor. Nordic countries combine progressive taxes with generous public services and have Gini coefficients around 0.25–0.28. However, very high marginal rates may reduce incentives to work or encourage avoidance and migration of high earners, and income taxes do not touch wealth inequality.

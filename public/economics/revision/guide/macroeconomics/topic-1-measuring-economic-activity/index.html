1. Measuring economic activity

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 1 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how national income is measured, how to use real and per capita figures, the business cycle, and the limitations of GDP as a measure of well-being.

Syllabus Statement

3.1 Measuring economic activity and illustrating its variations: the circular flow; output, income and expenditure methods; GDP and GNI; nominal and real; total and per capita; PPP; the business cycle; limitations of GDP/GNI; alternative measures of well-being (OECD Better Life Index, Happiness Index, Happy Planet Index). Calculations: nominal GDP and GNI, real GDP and GNI using a price deflator, and per capita values.

Success criterion 1

Three ways to measure national income

Because output = income = expenditure, national income can be measured in three ways:

  • Output method: the value of all final goods and services produced (value added at each stage, to avoid double counting).
  • Income method: the sum of all incomes earned — wages, rent, interest and profit.
  • Expenditure method: total spending on final goods and services: C + I + G + (X − M).

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. Gross national income (GNI) = GDP + income earned by nationals abroad − income paid to foreigners. GNI is higher than GDP for countries whose citizens own many assets abroad; it is lower for countries with lots of foreign-owned firms (such as Ireland).

Success criterion 2

Nominal and real, total and per capita, PPP

  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices; real GDP is adjusted for inflation, so it shows changes in actual output.
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population. It allows comparisons between countries of different size and gives a better guide to average living standards.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts figures for differences in the cost of living, using an exchange rate at which the same basket of goods costs the same in each country. GDP per capita at PPP gives the most useful comparison of living standards between countries.

Exam tip: If a question gives data in nominal terms for several years, comment on inflation before claiming that output has grown.

Success criterion 3

The business cycle

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP over time. The business cycle shows short-term fluctuations in growth around the long-term trend (potential output, which grows as the quantity and quality of resources increase). Phases: expansion/boom (rising output, falling unemployment, inflationary pressure), peak, contraction/recession (falling output, rising unemployment), trough, then recovery.

Success criterion 4

Limitations of GDP and alternative measures

LimitationExplanation
Inaccurate dataStatistics are estimates and may be revised; data quality varies.
Unrecorded activityThe informal economy and non-market activity (unpaid caring, housework, volunteering) are excluded.
Quality of lifeGDP ignores leisure, working conditions, health, crime and happiness.
DistributionPer capita figures are averages and say nothing about inequality.
Composition of outputSpending on weapons or cleaning up disasters adds to GDP but not to well-being.
EnvironmentResource depletion and pollution are not subtracted; growth may be unsustainable.

Alternative measures include the OECD Better Life Index (11 dimensions, from housing to life satisfaction, which users can weight themselves), the World Happiness Report (survey-based life evaluation) and the Happy Planet Index (well-being and life expectancy relative to ecological footprint). Each has its own limitations: survey answers are subjective and cultural differences make comparisons difficult.

Success criterion 5

Calculations

Nominal, real and per capita GDP

Expenditure method: C = 800, I = 150, G = 300, X = 250, M = 280 (all $bn). GDP = 800 + 150 + 300 + (250 − 280) = $1220bn.

Growth rate = (GDP this year − GDP last year) ÷ GDP last year × 100. If real GDP rises from $1000bn to $1030bn, growth = 3%.

GDP per capita = $1220bn ÷ 40m people = $30 500.

Real GDP using a price deflatorReal GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price deflator × 100. Nominal GDP of $1320bn with a deflator of 110 (base year = 100) gives real GDP = 1320 ÷ 110 × 100 = $1200bn. The same method converts nominal GNI to real GNI; divide by population for per capita figures.

Key terms

Gross domestic product (GDP)
The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period.
Gross national income (GNI)
GDP plus net income from abroad.
Real GDP
GDP adjusted for inflation.
Nominal GDP
GDP measured at current prices.
GDP per capita
GDP divided by the population.
Purchasing power parity (PPP)
An exchange rate that equalises the purchasing power of currencies, so that the same basket costs the same in each country.
Business cycle
Short-term fluctuations in economic activity around the long-term trend.
Recession
Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.
Informal economy
Economic activity that is not registered or taxed.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of GDP per capita as a measure of economic well-being. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define GDP per capita and economic well-being; business cycle diagram optional.
  • Strengths: widely available, comparable using PPP, linked to living standards.
  • Limitations: distribution, informal economy, environment, quality of life; compare with HDI, Better Life Index and Happy Planet Index.

Model answer: GDP per capita at PPP is a widely available and standardised measure, so it allows comparison of average material living standards between countries and over time. However, it is an average and ignores inequality: Qatar and Luxembourg have very high GDP per capita but very different distributions. It omits unpaid work and the informal economy, says nothing about health, leisure or the environment, and counts harmful activity as output. Composite measures such as the HDI or the Better Life Index give a fuller picture, but are themselves subjective. GDP per capita is therefore a useful starting point rather than a full measure of well-being.

    How to answer

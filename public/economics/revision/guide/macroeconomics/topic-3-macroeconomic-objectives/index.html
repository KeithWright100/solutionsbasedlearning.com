3. Macroeconomic objectives: growth, unemployment, inflation and debt

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 3 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand the main macroeconomic objectives, how they are measured, the causes and costs of unemployment and inflation, and the conflicts between objectives.

Syllabus Statement

3.3 Macroeconomic objectives: economic growth (causes and consequences); low unemployment (measurement, types, causes, costs); low and stable inflation (CPI, inflation, disinflation, deflation, causes and costs); potential conflicts between objectives. HL: weighted price index; sustainable government debt; the short-run and long-run Phillips curve.

Success criterion 1

Economic growth

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP. Short-run growth comes from higher AD using spare capacity; long-run growth comes from increases in potential output (LRAS).

Possible benefitsPossible costs
Higher living standards and incomesInflation if growth exceeds capacity
Lower unemploymentRising inequality: gains may go mainly to the rich
More tax revenue for public servicesEnvironmental damage and resource depletion
Firms invest and innovateCurrent account deficits as imports rise
Success criterion 2

Unemployment

Unemployment rateUnemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100. The labour force is everyone employed plus everyone unemployed (actively looking for work). Example: 1.5m unemployed and 28.5m employed: 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.

Official figures may underestimate true unemployment: they miss hidden unemployment (discouraged workers who have stopped looking), underemployment (part-time workers who want full-time jobs, graduates in low-skilled jobs) and people in the informal economy. Averages also hide differences by region, age, gender and ethnicity.

TypeCausePossible solution
Cyclical (demand-deficient)A fall in AD in a recessionExpansionary demand-side policies
StructuralChanges in the structure of the economy, technology or demand; mismatch of skills or location; labour market rigiditiesSupply-side policies: retraining, education, labour market reforms
FrictionalShort-term, between jobsBetter job information
SeasonalDemand varies with the seasons (tourism, farming)Hard to remove

Costs of unemployment: lost output (inside the PPC), lost tax revenue and higher benefit spending, loss of income and skills for individuals, poorer health, crime and social problems, and greater inequality. The natural rate of unemployment = structural + frictional + seasonal unemployment.

Success criterion 3

Inflation and deflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the average price level. Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation (prices still rise, but more slowly). Deflation is a sustained fall in the average price level. Inflation is measured with a consumer price index (CPI), based on a basket of goods weighted by household spending. Many central banks target 2%.

Costs of inflation: loss of purchasing power (especially for those on fixed incomes), uncertainty that discourages investment, redistribution from savers and lenders to borrowers, reduced export competitiveness, menu and shoe-leather costs, and fiscal drag into higher tax brackets. Costs of deflation: consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices, so AD falls further (a deflationary spiral); the real value of debt rises; firms cut wages and jobs. Japan suffered long periods of mild deflation from the late 1990s.

Problems of measuring inflation: the basket may not reflect all households’ spending, quality improvements are hard to account for, and new products and changes in buying habits take time to include.

Success criterion 4

Conflicts between objectives

  • Low unemployment v low inflation: expanding AD to reduce unemployment may cause demand-pull inflation.
  • High growth v low inflation: rapid growth can overheat the economy.
  • High growth v environmental sustainability: more output can mean more pollution and resource use.
  • High growth v equity: the benefits of growth may be unevenly shared.
  • High growth v the current account: rising incomes raise imports.

HLThe short-run Phillips curve shows the trade-off between unemployment and inflation. Monetarists argue that in the long run there is no trade-off: the LRPC is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment, so trying to keep unemployment below the NRU only causes accelerating inflation. Stagflation in the 1970s (high inflation and high unemployment together, caused by oil price shocks) challenged the original Phillips curve.

HLWeighted price index and government debt

HLWeighted price index

Weighted price index = 10 740 ÷ 100 = 107.4, so the average price level has risen by 7.4% since the base year.

HLGovernment (national) debt is the total amount the government owes, built up from past budget deficits; it is usually measured as a percentage of GDP. A budget deficit (G > tax revenue in one year) adds to the debt. High debt raises interest payments (an opportunity cost), may lower the country’s credit rating, raising borrowing costs, may require future tax rises or spending cuts, and limits the government’s ability to respond to the next recession. Whether debt is sustainable depends on interest rates relative to growth and on whether the debt is owed in the country’s own currency.

Key terms

Unemployment rate
The number unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
Underemployment
Workers who are employed part-time or below their skill level but want more work.
Structural unemployment
Unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers’ skills or location and available jobs.
Cyclical unemployment
Unemployment caused by a fall in AD.
Natural rate of unemployment (NRU)
Unemployment when the economy is at potential output: structural, frictional and seasonal.
Inflation
A sustained increase in the average price level.
Disinflation
A fall in the rate of inflation.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the average price level.
Consumer price index (CPI)
A weighted index of the prices of a basket of goods bought by typical households.
Demand-pull inflation
Inflation caused by AD growing faster than AS.
Cost-push inflation
Inflation caused by rising costs of production shifting SRAS left.
Stagflation
High inflation together with high unemployment and low growth.
Phillips curve
HL: a curve showing the relationship between unemployment and inflation.
Government debt
HL: the total accumulated borrowing of the government, usually shown as a percentage of GDP.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the view that low unemployment and low inflation cannot be achieved at the same time. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define unemployment and inflation; AD/AS diagram showing demand-pull trade-off; HL: SRPC and LRPC.
  • Explain the trade-off when AD expands near full employment.
  • Evaluate: supply-side growth (LRAS shift) can achieve both; long-run Phillips curve; real examples (e.g. US in the late 2010s had low unemployment and low inflation).

Model answer: When AD rises in an economy close to full employment, unemployment falls but firms compete for scarce labour and resources, so the price level rises: a trade-off. However, if growth comes from the supply side, LRAS shifts right and output and employment rise while price pressure falls. In the late 2010s the USA had unemployment below 4% with inflation close to 2%. Monetarists argue that in the long run unemployment returns to its natural rate whatever the inflation rate, so the trade-off is only temporary.

  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q2(a)Explain two costs of deflation.[10]
    How to answer

    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain the costs of each (purchasing power, uncertainty, competitiveness v lost output, income, social costs).
    • Judge which matters more depending on how high each is, who is affected and the time period; real examples (e.g. 2022 inflation surge, high youth unemployment in Spain).

  • May 2022 · P2 SL · Q1(b)(ii)Use your inflation figures to spot when disinflation began.[1]
    How to answer
    • Disinflation = inflation still positive but falling.
  • May 2022 · P2 SL · Q2(d)Using AD/AS, explain how higher fuel prices could cause inflation.[4]
    How to answer
    • Cost-push: SRAS shifts left, price level rises (and real GDP falls).

5. Demand management: monetary and fiscal policy

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 3: Macroeconomics · Topics · Topic 5 of 6

Learning Intention

To understand how central banks and governments use monetary and fiscal policy to manage AD, and to evaluate their effectiveness.

HL: money creation, tools of monetary policy, the money market. 3.6 Fiscal policy: sources of revenue and types of spending; the budget; expansionary and contractionary policy; effectiveness. Success criterion 1

Monetary policy

Monetary policy is carried out by the central bank and involves changing interest rates or the money supply to influence AD. Goals: low and stable inflation (many central banks have an inflation target, often 2%), low unemployment, a stable exchange rate, and reducing the business cycle.

  • Expansionary (loose): lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive, so C and I rise; the currency tends to depreciate, so X − M rises. AD shifts right.
  • Contractionary (tight): higher interest rates reduce C and I and strengthen the currency; AD shifts left to reduce inflation.
Real interest rateReal interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate. A nominal rate of 5% with inflation of 3% gives a real rate of 2%.

Real-world example: In 2022–23, after inflation rose sharply, the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate from near zero to over 5%, and the European Central Bank and Bank of England also raised rates repeatedly.

Success criterion 2

HLMoney creation and the tools of monetary policy

Commercial banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a new deposit. Because banks only keep a fraction of deposits as reserves, an initial deposit can support many times its value in loans (the fractional reserve system).

  • Open market operations: the central bank buys government bonds to increase the money supply (or sells them to reduce it).
  • Minimum reserve requirements: lowering the required reserves lets banks lend more.
  • Changes in the central bank’s minimum lending rate (policy rate).
  • Quantitative easing (QE): large-scale purchases of bonds and other assets to inject money when interest rates are already near zero.
Success criterion 3

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence AD. Sources of revenue: direct taxes, indirect taxes, sale of goods and services, and sale of state-owned assets. Spending: current expenditures (wages, running costs), capital expenditures (infrastructure, schools), and transfer payments.

  • Budget deficit: spending > revenue; surplus: revenue > spending; balanced budget.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy: higher G and/or lower taxes; AD shifts right.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy: lower G and/or higher taxes; AD shifts left.

Exam tip: A diagram for fiscal policy looks just like one for monetary policy. What matters is that you explain the specific mechanism: which component of AD changes and why.

Success criterion 5

HLCrowding out and automatic stabilisers

Automatic stabilisers work without any new policy decision. In a recession, tax revenue from progressive income taxes falls and unemployment benefit payments rise, which supports AD; in a boom the opposite happens and slows the economy. They reduce the size of the business cycle.

Keynesians argue that fiscal policy is powerful in a recession because of the multiplier; monetarists stress crowding out and favour monetary policy and rules.

Key terms

Monetary policy
Changes in interest rates or the money supply by the central bank to influence AD.
Inflation targeting
A central bank policy of keeping inflation close to a stated target.
Real interest rate
The nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation.
Quantitative easing
HL: large-scale purchases of financial assets by the central bank to increase the money supply.
Open market operations
HL: the central bank buying or selling government bonds to change the money supply.
Fiscal policy
The use of government spending and taxation to influence AD.
Budget deficit
When government spending is greater than revenue in a year.
Crowding out
HL: a fall in private spending caused by higher interest rates when the government borrows.
Automatic stabilisers
HL: taxes and benefits that change automatically with the business cycle and dampen fluctuations.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the effectiveness of monetary policy in bringing an economy out of a recession. (15 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 5 (13–15): relevant terms defined; correct, fully explained diagram(s); real-world examples used effectively; a synthesis or evaluation that is balanced and supported by appropriate reasoning (for example short run v long run, stakeholders, priorities, pros and cons).
  • Level 4 (10–12): mostly accurate theory and diagram(s), real-world examples, and an evaluation that is not fully balanced.
  • Level 3 (7–9): some theory and a diagram, a limited real-world example and evaluation that is mostly asserted.
  • Levels 1–2 (1–6): little relevant theory, no or incorrect diagram and no real evaluation.
  • Define monetary policy and recession; AD/AS diagram showing AD shifting right to close a deflationary gap.
  • Explain the transmission: lower rates, higher C and I, depreciation and higher net exports.
  • Evaluate: low confidence, rates near zero (liquidity trap), QE, time lags, comparison with fiscal policy, example (2008 or 2020).

Model answer: Cutting interest rates lowers the cost of borrowing, so consumption on credit and investment rise, and the currency depreciates, raising net exports; AD shifts right and the deflationary gap narrows. Monetary policy can be adjusted quickly by an independent central bank. However, in a deep recession households and firms may not borrow however low rates go, and once rates reach zero central banks must use QE, whose effects are uncertain. After 2008, years of near-zero rates and QE produced only a slow recovery in many countries, which is why many economists argue that fiscal policy is also needed.

  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(c)Using a business cycle diagram, explain how automatic stabilisers reduce the fall in GDP in a recession.[4]
    How to answer
    • Falling tax revenue and rising benefits support AD, so the downturn is smaller: draw two cycles with different amplitudes.
  • May 2023 · P2 SL · Q2(b)(ii)Calculate a real interest rate.[1]
    How to answer
    • Nominal interest rate minus inflation.
  • May 2023 · P2 SL · Q2(c)Using AD/AS, explain how expansionary fiscal policy supported growth.[4]
    How to answer
    • Higher G or lower taxes: AD shifts right, real GDP rises.
  • May 2022 · P2 HL · Q1(e)Using a money market diagram, explain how cutting the minimum reserve requirement affects interest rates.[4]
    How to answer
    • Banks can lend more, so the money supply (vertical Sm) shifts right and the interest rate falls.
  • May 2022 · P2 HL · Q2(d)Using AD/AS, explain how cutting government spending may lower inflation.[4]
    How to answer
    • Contractionary fiscal policy: AD shifts left, the price level falls (inflation slows).

2. The circular flow of income and how economics has developed

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 1: Introduction to economics · Topics · Topic 2 of 2

Learning Intention

To understand how income flows round the economy, the role of leakages and injections, and how economic thinking has changed over time.

Success criterion 1

The circular flow of income

The circular flow model shows how money, goods and services and factors of production flow between households and firms. In the simplest two-sector model:

  • Households own the factors of production and sell factor services to firms.
  • Firms pay households factor incomes: rent, wages, interest and profit.
  • Households spend their income on goods and services produced by firms (consumer expenditure, C).

This shows a key idea: national output = national income = national expenditure. These three measures are equal because one person’s spending is another person’s income.

Success criterion 2

Leakages and injections

Leakage (withdrawal)SectorInjection
Savings (S)Financial sectorInvestment (I)
Taxes (T)GovernmentGovernment spending (G)
Imports (M)Foreign sectorExports (X)

A leakage is income that is not passed on in the circular flow. An injection is spending that enters the flow from outside the household–firm loop.

  • If injections > leakages, the flow of income grows: national income rises.
  • If leakages > injections, the flow shrinks: national income falls.
  • If injections = leakages, national income is in equilibrium.

Exam tip: Do not confuse leakages with spending. When a household buys a foreign-made phone, the spending is an import — a leakage, because the income goes to a firm abroad.

Success criterion 3

How economic thinking has developed

School / economistMain ideas
Adam Smith (1776)The “invisible hand”: individuals pursuing their own interest in free markets allocate resources efficiently. Division of labour raises productivity.
Classical economists (19th century)Markets self-correct; wages and prices are flexible; little need for government (laissez-faire). Ricardo developed comparative advantage.
MarxCriticised capitalism for exploiting workers and creating inequality and instability.
Marginalist revolution (1870s)Decisions are made at the margin; value depends on marginal utility. The basis of modern demand and supply analysis.
Keynes (1936)After the Great Depression, argued that markets do not always self-correct; insufficient aggregate demand can cause long-term unemployment, so government should intervene.
Monetarist / new classical (1970s–)Friedman and others: inflation is caused by excessive money growth; markets work best with limited intervention; supply-side policies.
Behavioural economicsPeople are not always rational; biases and rules of thumb affect choices (see Unit 2).
Circular economy / Doughnut economicsGrowth must happen within planetary boundaries while meeting everyone’s social foundation; reuse and recycle resources rather than “take, make, dispose”.

Exam tip: You will not be asked a whole question on the history of economic thought, but showing that economists disagree (for example Keynesian v monetarist views of AS, or the limits of rational choice) is excellent evaluation.

Success criterion 4

New approaches: the circular economy

Traditional economics often assumes a linear model of production: take resources, make products, throw them away. The circular economy aims to keep resources in use for as long as possible by designing out waste, repairing, reusing and recycling products, and regenerating natural systems.

Doughnut economics (Kate Raworth) argues the goal of economic activity should be to meet the needs of all people (the inner “social foundation”) without overshooting the planet’s ecological limits (the outer “ecological ceiling”). Amsterdam adopted the model as a framework for city policy in 2020.

These ideas link directly to the key concepts of sustainability and economic well-being, and to the debate in Unit 3 and Unit 4 about whether GDP growth is the right objective.

Key terms

Circular flow of income
A model showing the flows of money, goods and services and factors of production between sectors of the economy.
Leakage (withdrawal)
Income that leaves the circular flow: savings, taxes and imports.
Injection
Spending that enters the circular flow from outside the household–firm loop: investment, government spending and exports.
National income
The total income earned by the factors of production in an economy; equal in principle to national output and national expenditure.
Laissez-faire
The view that governments should not intervene in markets.
Circular economy
An economic system that keeps resources in use for as long as possible through reuse, repair and recycling.
Exam-style question

Explain how changes in leakages and injections can change the level of national income. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define national income, leakages and injections.
  • Draw a circular flow diagram including the three leakages and three injections.
  • Explain that income rises when injections exceed leakages and falls when leakages exceed injections, with an example.

Model answer: National income is the total income earned in an economy. In the circular flow model, households receive factor incomes from firms and spend them on goods and services. Some income leaks out as savings, taxes and imports, while investment, government spending and exports are injected. If a government raises spending on infrastructure, injections exceed leakages: firms receive more revenue, hire more factors and pay out more income, so national income rises until leakages rise to equal the new level of injections. A fall in exports during a global recession would have the opposite effect.

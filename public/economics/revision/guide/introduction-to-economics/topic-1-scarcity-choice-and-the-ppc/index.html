1. Economics as a social science: scarcity, choice and the PPC

IB Economics Revision Guide · Unit 1: Introduction to economics · Topics · Topic 1 of 2

Learning Intention

To understand the basic economic problem of scarcity and choice, the factors of production, opportunity cost, and how the PPC model illustrates them.

Syllabus Statement

1.1 What is economics? Economics as a social science; scarcity; choice; opportunity cost; free and economic goods; the factors of production; the basic economic questions; the PPC model; sustainability; economic methodology (ceteris paribus, positive and normative economics).

Success criterion 1

Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

Economics is a social science: it studies human behaviour — how individuals, firms and governments make choices — using models and theories that are tested against real-world evidence. Unlike the natural sciences, economists usually cannot run controlled experiments, and people do not always behave predictably.

The basic economic problem is scarcity: human needs and wants are unlimited, but the resources available to satisfy them are limited. Because of scarcity, every economic agent must make choices, and every choice has an opportunity cost — the value of the next best alternative given up.

Example: A government that spends an extra $2 billion on a high-speed rail link might have used that money to build hospitals. The hospitals that are not built are the opportunity cost of the rail line.

Exam tip: Opportunity cost is always the next best alternative forgone, not all the alternatives added together.

Success criterion 2

Factors of production; free and economic goods

FactorWhat it includesReward
LandAll natural resources: soil, minerals, forests, fish, water, oilRent
LabourHuman physical and mental effort used in production; its quality depends on human capital (education, skills, health)Wages
CapitalMan-made goods used to make other goods: machinery, factories, roads, IT networksInterest
EntrepreneurshipOrganising the other factors and taking the risk of starting and running a businessProfit

An economic good is scarce and has an opportunity cost, so it must be allocated (most goods and services). A free good is not scarce — using it has no opportunity cost (air, sunlight, sea water). Some “free” goods are becoming economic goods as pollution and overuse make them scarce, for example clean air in heavily polluted cities.

Success criterion 3

The production possibilities curve (PPC)

The PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or types of goods) an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, with a given level of technology.

  • Scarcity: points beyond the curve (D) are unattainable.
  • Choice: the economy must choose a point on the curve.
  • Opportunity cost: moving along the curve means giving up some of one good to get more of the other. A curved (concave) PPC shows increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods; a straight-line PPC shows constant opportunity cost.
  • Unemployment / inefficiency: a point inside the curve (C) means resources are unemployed or used inefficiently. Moving from C to the curve is actual growth with no opportunity cost.
  • Economic growth: an outward shift of the PPC is growth in potential output, caused by more or better factors of production or better technology. Choosing more capital goods today (point B rather than A) means fewer consumer goods now but a larger PPC in the future.

Exam tip: Label the axes with the two goods, label the curve PPC and mark the points you discuss. When you explain growth, say whether you mean actual growth (moving towards the curve) or potential growth (shifting the curve).

Success criterion 4

The basic economic questions and sustainability

Every economy must answer three questions: What to produce and in what quantities? How to produce it (which combination of resources and technology)? For whom to produce (how output is distributed)?

Economies answer them in different ways. In a free market economy the price mechanism decides; in a planned economy the government decides; most countries are mixed economies, with markets and government both playing a part.

Sustainability means meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Because resources are scarce and some are non-renewable, choices made today affect the resources available later, so sustainability is one of the nine key concepts of the course.

Success criterion 5

Economic methodology

Economists build simplified models (such as the PPC or demand and supply) to explain and predict behaviour. Models rely on assumptions; the most important is ceteris paribus — “all other things being equal” — so that the effect of one variable can be isolated.

A positive statement is about what is, was or will be, and can be tested against evidence (“Youth unemployment in Spain is higher than in Germany”). A normative statement contains a value judgement about what ought to be (“The government should cut youth unemployment”).

Economists use marginal analysis (thinking about the effect of one more unit) and must accept that theories can be refuted by evidence. Models are useful simplifications, but real people do not always behave as models assume.

Key terms

Scarcity
The situation where unlimited wants exceed the limited resources available to satisfy them.
Opportunity cost
The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
Factors of production
The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship.
Free good
A good that is not scarce, so its use has no opportunity cost.
Production possibilities curve (PPC)
A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with full and efficient use of its resources and given technology.
Ceteris paribus
The assumption that all other things are held constant, so that the effect of one variable can be studied.
Positive statement
A statement of fact that can be tested against evidence.
Normative statement
A statement containing a value judgement about what ought to be.
Sustainability
Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
Exam-style question

Using a production possibilities curve diagram, explain the concepts of scarcity, choice and opportunity cost. (10 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Level 4 (9–10): a clear, accurate and fully labelled diagram, fully explained; precise definitions of key terms; a well-developed explanation applied to the question.
  • Level 3 (6–8): relevant terms defined and a correct diagram, but the explanation is not fully developed or the diagram is not fully explained.
  • Level 2 (3–5): some understanding, with a diagram that has errors or is not linked to the explanation.
  • Level 1 (1–2): little understanding; few or no relevant terms or diagram.
  • Define scarcity, choice and opportunity cost.
  • Draw a concave PPC with labelled axes and points on, inside and outside the curve.
  • Explain movement along the curve as opportunity cost, and the unattainable point as scarcity.

Model answer: Scarcity means limited resources cannot satisfy unlimited wants, so societies must choose what to produce. On a PPC for consumer and capital goods, every point on the curve uses all resources; a point outside it is unattainable, which illustrates scarcity. Because the economy can only be at one point, it must choose; moving from A to B gains capital goods but gives up consumer goods — the consumer goods forgone are the opportunity cost. The curve is concave because resources are not equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost increases.

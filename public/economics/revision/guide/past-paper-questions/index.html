IB Economics Revision Guide / Past paper questions

IB Economics past paper questions

May 2022 and May 2023, Papers 1, 2 and 3, with each question linked to the revision guide topic that covers it.

How to use this page

These are real IB questions from May 2022 and May 2023, reworded here as a summary. Paper 2 and Paper 3 questions use texts and data from the paper, so ask your teacher for a copy of the paper before you answer. Write your answer, then check it against the tips.

Paper 1 and Paper 2 are taken by SL and HL students; the SL and HL versions of Paper 1 share some questions. Paper 3 is HL only.

May 2023

Paper 1 HL TZ1

  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q1(a)Explain why firms in monopolistic competition only make abnormal profit in the short run. (HL: Market power and equity)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Short-run and long-run firm diagrams: entry shifts each firm’s demand curve left until AR = AC.
    • Explain low barriers to entry and product differentiation.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q1(b)Discuss whether monopolistic competition is more desirable than oligopoly, using real examples. (HL: Market power and equity)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Compare efficiency, prices, choice and innovation in each structure; game theory and collusion in oligopoly.
    • Balance: oligopolies may gain economies of scale and fund R&D; monopolistic competition has excess capacity.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q2(a)Explain the natural rate of unemployment. (Macroeconomic objectives)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Define the NRU as frictional + structural + seasonal unemployment when the economy is at potential output.
    • LRAS diagram and/or labour market diagram; HL: LRPC vertical at the NRU.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q2(b)Evaluate how effective monetary policy is at closing a large deflationary gap, using real examples. (Monetary and fiscal policy)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • AD/AS diagram with a deflationary gap; explain the transmission of lower interest rates and QE.
    • Evaluate: low confidence, rates at zero, time lags, and compare with fiscal policy (e.g. 2008–09 and 2020).
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q3(a)Explain how education and health programmes can promote development. (Barriers and strategies)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Human capital: higher productivity, income and life chances; poverty cycle or LRAS diagram.
    • Cover BOTH education and health.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ1 · Q3(b)Discuss whether weak institutions are the most significant barrier to growth in the poorest countries, using real examples. (Barriers and strategies)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain legal systems, tax collection, banking and property rights as barriers.
    • "Most significant": compare with economic barriers (infrastructure, debt, primary dependence) and political ones.

    How to answer

    • YED formula; necessities, luxuries and inferior goods with an Engel curve or demand shift diagram.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ2 · Q1(b)Discuss whether consumers always maximise utility, using real examples. (HL: Behavioural economics)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Rational choice assumptions v behavioural economics: biases, bounded rationality, self-control, selfishness.
    • Real examples of nudges and choice architecture; conclude on how far the assumption holds.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ2 · Q2(a)Explain two economic costs of high unemployment. (Macroeconomic objectives)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Two costs, e.g. lost output (inside the PPC), lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, loss of skills.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ2 · Q2(b)Discuss whether low unemployment always means higher inflation, using real examples. (Macroeconomic objectives)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Short-run Phillips curve and AD/AS trade-off.
    • Counter: supply-side growth, the LRPC, and real periods of low unemployment with low inflation.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ2 · Q3(a)Explain how relying on primary production can hold back economic growth. (Barriers and strategies)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Low PED and PES cause volatile prices; low YED; declining terms of trade; little value added.
  • May 2023 · P1 HL TZ2 · Q3(b)Evaluate whether market-based policies fail to promote development in the poorest countries, using real examples. (Barriers and strategies)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain trade liberalisation, privatisation and deregulation.
    • Evaluate against market failure, inequality and weak institutions; compare with interventionist strategies.

Paper 1 SL TZ1

  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q1(a)Explain why positive externalities in healthcare and education can lead to market failure. (Externalities and public goods)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Positive consumption externality diagram: MSB above MPB, Qm < Qopt, welfare loss shaded.
    • Apply it to BOTH healthcare and education (external benefits to society, underconsumption).
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q1(b)Discuss whether subsidies are the best way to raise consumption of merit goods, using real examples. (Externalities and public goods)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Subsidy diagram moving output towards the optimum.
    • Because of "best way", compare with direct provision, regulation (compulsory schooling, vaccination), information and nudges.
    • Evaluate cost, opportunity cost, PED, targeting and who gains.
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q2(a)Explain two costs of deflation. (Macroeconomic objectives)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Two developed costs, e.g. delayed spending (deflationary spiral), higher real value of debt, falling profits and unemployment.
    • AD/AS diagram showing a fall in AD and the price level.
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q2(b)Discuss whether high inflation is a bigger problem than high unemployment, using real examples. (Macroeconomic objectives)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain the costs of each (purchasing power, uncertainty, competitiveness v lost output, income, social costs).
    • Judge which matters more depending on how high each is, who is affected and the time period; real examples (e.g. 2022 inflation surge, high youth unemployment in Spain).
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q3(a)Explain how a poverty cycle can stop a country growing. (Barriers and strategies)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Draw and explain a poverty cycle: low income, low saving, low investment, low productivity, low income.
    • Link it to economic growth (PPC or LRAS not shifting out).
  • May 2023 · P1 SL TZ1 · Q3(b)Discuss whether economic growth always brings economic development, using real examples. (Sustainable and measuring development)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Explain how growth can fund health, education and lower poverty.
    • Counter-arguments: inequality, environmental damage, resource-led growth with few links, corruption; use HDI/IHDI comparisons.

    • Multiply (or convert using the exchange rate) carefully and give units.
  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(b)(ii)Sketch a minimum wage diagram showing unemployment. (Government intervention)[3]
    How to answer
    • Labour market with a wage floor above equilibrium; label the unemployment.
  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(c)Using a business cycle diagram, explain how automatic stabilisers reduce the fall in GDP in a recession. (Monetary and fiscal policy)[4]
    How to answer

  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(d)Using demand and supply, explain why the oil price is likely to keep falling. (Demand, supply and equilibrium)[4]

  • May 2023 · P2 HL · Q1(f)Using a poverty cycle, explain how a rural finance project raises incomes. (Barriers and strategies)[4]

    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.

  • May 2022 · P1 HL · Q1(b)Discuss the effects of large firms with significant market power, using real examples. (HL: Market power and equity)[15]

    • Monopoly or oligopoly diagram: higher price, lower output, welfare loss, abnormal profit.
    • Balance: economies of scale, R&D and innovation (dynamic efficiency), natural monopoly; v consumer harm and collusion.
    • Bring in regulation and competition authorities (e.g. EU fines on large tech firms).
  • May 2022 · P1 HL · Q3(a)Explain the limitations of the theory of comparative advantage as a basis for specialisation and trade. (Trade and protection)[10]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (a) is marked with the 10-mark bands: define the key terms, draw a fully labelled diagram and explain it, and answer every part of the question.
    • Start with a linear PPC diagram showing comparative advantage, then explain the unrealistic assumptions.
    • Limitations: transport costs, trade barriers, imperfect factor mobility, increasing costs, over-specialisation and vulnerability, ignores externalities.
  • May 2022 · P1 HL · Q3(b)Discuss the pros and cons for a country of joining a trading bloc, using real examples. (Economic integration)[15]
    How to answer
    • Paper 1 (b) is marked with the 15-mark bands: theory and a diagram, at least one developed real-world example, and balanced evaluation that reaches a judgement.
    • Distinguish the types of bloc; HL: use trade creation and trade diversion.
    • Advantages: market access, economies of scale, bargaining power, labour mobility. Disadvantages: sovereignty, trade diversion, weaker multilateral talks.
    • Use a real bloc (EU, ASEAN, USMCA, Mercosur) and conclude on what makes membership beneficial.

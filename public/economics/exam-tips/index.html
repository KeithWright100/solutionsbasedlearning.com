IB Economics

Examination Tips & Advice

What kind of economist the course is trying to build, how to read command terms, and how much depth each one expects from your answer.

1. Know your assessment structure

IB Economics is assessed across three exam papers plus an Internal Assessment. Knowing exactly what each paper is based on and how it is marked helps you prepare — and pace — correctly.

ComponentBased onTimeStructureMarksWeighting
Paper 1 (SL & HL) Extended-response essay questions 1h 15 Choose 1 of 3 essay questions, each with a part (a) worth 10 marks and a part (b) worth 15 marks 25 SL 30% / HL 20%
Paper 2 (SL & HL) Unseen data response — a case study with statistics, text extracts and diagrams 1h 45 Answer 1 of 2 case-study questions, with parts (a)–(f) worth 25 marks and a final part (g) worth 15 marks 40 SL 40% / HL 30%
Paper 3 (HL only) The “policy paper” — unseen quantitative and policy-based questions 1h 45 2 compulsory multi-part questions, each combining short calculations, diagram work and a 10-mark policy recommendation 60 HL 30%
Internal Assessment (SL & HL) Three commentaries, each based on a different published news article (max 800 words each) — Each commentary applies economic theory to a different unit of the syllabus and a different key concept 45 SL 30% / HL 20%

A calculator is required for Papers 2 and 3 — check with your teacher on the exact model your school allows.

2. What studying IB Economics is meant to build in you

IB Economics doesn't just want you to memorise theory — it's built around ten broader qualities the IB calls its Learner Profile, and the subject asks something specific of each one. Here's what each looks like in an economics classroom.

Learner Profile traitWhat that looks like in IB Economics
InquirersInvestigating real economic problems on your own and with others — questioning why some economies grow faster than others, why a market has failed, or who actually gains and who loses from a given policy.
KnowledgeableBuilding a working toolkit of concepts, theories and models, and using that toolkit with confidence to make sense of live data, current events and situations you haven't seen before.
ThinkersPulling together evidence and ideas from across the syllabus to reach a conclusion, and building an argument that is properly supported rather than simply asserted.
CommunicatorsWriting tight, precise economic commentary — using diagrams, terminology and evidence the way a professional economist would, not just describing what you know in general terms.
PrincipledHandling data and evidence honestly, and recognising that economic knowledge is never neutral — it shapes real decisions that affect real people, which is itself a responsibility.
Open-mindedAccepting that economists genuinely disagree with each other — different schools of thought explain the same problem in different ways and propose different solutions — and treating that disagreement as part of the subject rather than a flaw in it.
CaringKeeping sight of the fact that economics is ultimately about people — that behind a statistic on unemployment or growth is a real effect on someone's wellbeing.
Risk-takersGoing beyond the textbook answer to propose your own policy response to a real economic problem, and being willing to defend a judgement that doesn't have one single correct answer.
BalancedWeighing up competing viewpoints before settling on a position, and backing up your judgement with reasoning solid enough that a critic couldn't easily knock it down.
ReflectiveLearning to tell the difference between a statement of fact (positive economics) and a statement of opinion or value (normative economics) — and asking how objective any one piece of economic theory can really claim to be.

3. Know your command terms

Every command term in an IB Economics question sits under one of four assessment objectives, and that objective tells you how far your answer needs to go. AO1 terms check that you know the content. AO2 terms want you to use that knowledge — applying it to the scenario or data in front of you, or breaking down how something works. AO3 terms expect you to weigh evidence up and reach a reasoned, supported judgement, not just describe or explain in isolation. AO4 is different again: instead of essay-style command terms, it covers the practical skills you bring to a question — plotting a diagram accurately, working through a calculation, or deriving a value. As a general rule, depth increases as you move from AO1 through to AO3, so use the assessment objective behind a command term as your guide to how much an examiner expects from that answer.

ObjectiveTypical command termsWhat it's really asking for
AO1 — Knowledge and understanding Define, Describe, List, Outline, State Show that you know a term, concept or piece of information and can present it accurately — no analysis or judgement is expected at this level.
AO2 — Application and analysis Analyse, Apply, Comment, Distinguish, Explain, Suggest Take your economic knowledge and use it — applying it to the scenario or data you've been given, or breaking an idea down to show how its parts connect and why.
AO3 — Synthesis and evaluation Compare, Compare and contrast, Contrast, Discuss, Evaluate, Examine, Justify, Recommend, To what extent Bring separate pieces of evidence or argument together into one reasoned judgement, weighing their strengths and weaknesses rather than simply listing them side by side.
AO4 — Use and application of appropriate skills Calculate, Construct, Derive, Determine, Draw, Identify, Label, Measure, Plot, Show, Show that, Sketch, Solve Demonstrate a practical skill — plotting a diagram accurately, working through a calculation, or deriving a value — rather than writing about the concept in prose.

Command terms aren't spelled out anywhere else in the syllabus content itself, so it's worth learning this classification directly — it's the fastest way to judge how much depth a question wants before you start writing.

5. Supply

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.1 The Market System · 1.1.3 Demand, supply and equilibrium · Lesson 5 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how price and non-price factors affect the supply of a product.

Syllabus Statement

The relationship between price and quantity supplied; the distinction between movements along and shifts of the supply curve; factors that shift the supply curve: costs of production, technology, weather and natural events, government policy (indirect taxes, subsidies, regulation), and the number of firms.

Success criterion 1

Supply and the supply curve

Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at a given price over a period of time.

As price rises, quantity supplied rises, so the supply curve slopes upwards from left to right. A higher price means each unit is more profitable, and it can cover the extra costs of producing more.

Success criterion 2

Movements along vs shifts

  • A change in the good’s own price → movement along the supply curve (extension when price rises, contraction when price falls).
  • A change in any other factor → shift of the supply curve (right = increase, left = decrease).
Success criterion 3

Factors that shift supply

Most supply shifts come down to costs: anything that makes production cheaper shifts supply right.

FactorEffect on supply
Costs of productionHigher wages, raw material or energy prices raise costs → supply decreases (left).
TechnologyNew technology raises productivity and lowers unit costs → supply increases (right).
Weather and natural eventsGood harvests increase agricultural supply; droughts, floods or disease decrease it.
Indirect taxesA tax on a good (e.g. VAT, excise duty) adds to costs → supply decreases.
SubsidiesA government payment to producers lowers costs → supply increases.
RegulationExtra rules (e.g. safety standards) raise costs → supply decreases.
Number of firmsMore firms entering the market → supply increases.

Activity: shift or movement?

Decide how each change affects the supply of rice.

  • Severe floods in rice-growing regions
  • A new high-yield rice variety
  • The market price of rice rises
  • Government places a tax on rice
  • Government gives rice farmers a subsidy
  • Fertiliser prices double
  • More farmers start growing rice

Key terms

Supply
The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at a given price over a period of time.
Extension of supply
A rise in quantity supplied caused by a rise in the good’s own price.
Indirect tax
A tax on spending, collected from producers, e.g. VAT or excise duty.
Subsidy
A payment from the government to producers to lower their costs.
Productivity
Output per worker (or per unit of input) per period of time.
Exam-style question

Explain how a subsidy to dairy farmers would affect the supply of milk. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: a subsidy is a payment from the government that reduces producers’ costs.
  • 1 mark: lower costs make it more profitable to produce at each price.
  • 1 mark: supply increases / the supply curve shifts to the right.

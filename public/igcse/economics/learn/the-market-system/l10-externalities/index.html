10. Externalities

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.1 The Market System · 1.1.6 Externalities · Lesson 10 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand external costs and benefits and how they cause market failure.

Syllabus Statement

External costs of production (e.g. pollution, congestion, environmental damage) and external benefits of consumption (e.g. education, healthcare, vaccinations); private, external and social costs and benefits.

Success criterion 1

External costs

An externality is a cost or benefit that affects a third party — someone not directly involved in producing or consuming the good.

External costs are the negative effects of an activity on third parties that are not paid for by the producer or consumer. Examples:

  • Pollution — a factory releases smoke, causing breathing problems for local residents.
  • Congestion — extra cars on the road slow down journeys for everyone.
  • Environmental damage — deforestation for palm oil destroys habitats and adds to climate change.
  • Noise — a new airport disturbs people living nearby.

Because the producer does not pay these costs, the good is overproduced and its price is too low. This is market failure.

Success criterion 2

External benefits

External benefits are the positive effects of an activity on third parties. Examples:

  • Education — a better-educated workforce is more productive, benefiting businesses and the whole economy.
  • Health care — healthier people take fewer days off work.
  • Vaccinations — a vaccinated person is less likely to pass on a disease to others.

Because consumers only consider their own (private) benefits, these goods are underconsumed. Governments may provide them free or subsidise them.

Activity: external cost or external benefit?

Decide whether each is an external cost or an external benefit.

  • Smoke from a cement factory damaging crops nearby
  • Neighbours enjoying a beautiful private garden
  • Traffic jams caused by a new shopping centre
  • Fewer flu cases after a vaccination campaign
  • Litter from a fast-food outlet
  • Firms hiring skilled graduates from a new university

Success criterion 3

Private, external and social costs and benefits

Social cost = private cost + external cost
Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit

TermMeaningExample
Private costCost paid by the producer or consumerA factory’s spending on wages, materials and energy
External costCost paid by third partiesHealth costs of residents breathing polluted air
Private benefitBenefit to the person consumingA student’s higher future earnings from a degree
External benefitBenefit to third partiesSociety gains from a more skilled workforce

Worked example: a factory’s private costs are $8m a year and the pollution it causes costs local people $3m. Social cost = $8m + $3m = $11m.

If social costs exceed social benefits, too much is produced. Governments can respond with taxes, regulation, fines or subsidies (covered in 1.2.6).

Key terms

Externality
A cost or benefit that affects a third party not directly involved in the activity.
External cost
A negative effect on third parties that is not paid for by the producer or consumer.
External benefit
A positive effect on third parties.
Social cost
Private cost plus external cost.
Social benefit
Private benefit plus external benefit.
Exam-style question

A new airport has private costs of $400m and external costs of $150m. Calculate the social cost of the airport and explain one external cost it may create. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: social cost = $400m + $150m = $550m.
  • 1 mark: identify an external cost, e.g. noise pollution / air pollution / congestion.
  • 1 mark: explain its effect on a third party, e.g. local residents suffer sleep disturbance and lower house prices.

4. Demand

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.1 The Market System · 1.1.3 Demand, supply and equilibrium · Lesson 4 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how price and non-price factors affect the demand for a product.

Syllabus Statement

The relationship between price and quantity demanded; the distinction between movements along and shifts of the demand curve; factors that shift the demand curve: changes in the price of substitutes and complements, income, tastes and fashion, advertising, population and demographics, and government policy (taxes on income, subsidies).

Success criterion 1

Demand and the demand curve

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a period of time. ‘Able’ matters: wanting a car without the money to buy it is not demand. This is called effective demand.

The law of demand: as price falls, quantity demanded rises; as price rises, quantity demanded falls. The demand curve therefore slopes downwards from left to right. Lower prices make a good more affordable and more attractive than alternatives.

Success criterion 2

Movements along vs shifts

  • A change in the price of the good itself causes a movement along the demand curve: an extension (price falls, more demanded) or a contraction (price rises, less demanded).
  • A change in any other factor causes a shift of the whole curve: to the right (increase in demand) or to the left (decrease in demand).

Exam tip: never write “price rises so demand shifts left”. A price change moves along the curve. Use “extension” and “contraction” for movements; “increase” and “decrease” for shifts.

Success criterion 3

Factors that shift demand

A useful memory aid is PASIFIC-style lists, but you only need to know these:

FactorEffect on demand
Price of substitutesIf the price of a substitute (e.g. tea for coffee) rises, demand for coffee increases → shift right.
Price of complementsIf the price of a complement (e.g. printers and ink) rises, demand for ink decreases → shift left.
IncomeHigher incomes increase demand for most goods (normal goods); demand for inferior goods (e.g. cheap own-brand food) may fall.
Tastes and fashionA product becoming fashionable increases demand; falling popularity decreases it.
AdvertisingSuccessful advertising increases demand and can make it more brand-loyal.
Population and demographicsA larger population, or an ageing population, changes how much of certain goods is demanded (e.g. more care homes).
Government policyLower income tax raises disposable income → demand increases; subsidies to consumers also raise demand.

Activity: shift or movement?

Decide whether each change causes a movement along or a shift of the demand curve for coffee.

  • The price of coffee falls
  • The price of tea (a substitute) rises
  • The price of milk (a complement) rises sharply
  • A celebrity advertising campaign for coffee
  • A health report links coffee to poor sleep
  • The price of coffee rises
  • Income tax is cut

Key terms

Demand
The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a period of time.
Extension of demand
A rise in quantity demanded caused by a fall in the good’s own price.
Contraction of demand
A fall in quantity demanded caused by a rise in the good’s own price.
Substitute
A good that can be used in place of another, e.g. tea and coffee.
Complement
A good used together with another, e.g. cars and petrol.
Exam-style question

Using a demand diagram, explain the likely effect on the demand for electric cars of a fall in the price of electricity. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: identify electricity and electric cars as complements.
  • 1 mark: lower running costs make electric cars more attractive, so demand increases at every price.
  • 1 mark: correctly labelled diagram with axes (Price, Quantity) and original demand curve D.
  • 1 mark: shift of demand to the right (D to D1) shown, with higher quantity at the same price.

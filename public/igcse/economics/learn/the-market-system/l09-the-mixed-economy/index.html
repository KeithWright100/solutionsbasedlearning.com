9. The mixed economy

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.1 The Market System · 1.1.5 The mixed economy · Lesson 9 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how a mixed economy allocates resources and why governments intervene.

Syllabus Statement

The mixed economy: public and private sectors (ownership, control and aims); how a mixed economy answers what, how and for whom to produce; market failure; public goods (non-excludability, non-rivalry, the free-rider problem); privatisation and its effects on consumers, workers, businesses and government.

Success criterion 1

Public and private sectors

A mixed economy is one in which resources are allocated by both the market (private sector) and the government (public sector). Almost every economy today is mixed, though the balance differs: the public sector is relatively larger in France or Sweden, and smaller in Singapore or the USA.

Public sectorPrivate sector
OwnershipOwned by the government / the stateOwned by private individuals and shareholders
ControlControlled by government ministers, local councils or public corporationsControlled by owners, directors and managers
Main aimsTo provide services for the benefit of society, e.g. health care, education, defenceUsually to make a profit; also growth and survival

Every economy must answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. In a mixed economy, the price mechanism decides most of these, but the government steps in — for example by producing public services, taxing harmful goods, setting minimum standards and redistributing income through benefits.

Success criterion 2

Market failure and public goods

Market failure happens when the free market leads to an inefficient allocation of resources: too much of some goods (e.g. pollution-causing products) and too little or none of others. This is a key reason for government intervention.

Public goods are goods that the market would not provide at all. They have two features:

  • Non-excludable — once provided, nobody can be stopped from benefiting, e.g. street lighting.
  • Non-rival — one person’s use does not reduce the amount available to others, e.g. national defence.

This creates the free-rider problem: people can benefit without paying, so private firms cannot make a profit and will not supply the good. The government provides public goods instead, funded by taxation. Examples: national defence, street lighting, flood defences, lighthouses.

Success criterion 3

Privatisation

Privatisation is the transfer of ownership of a business or industry from the public sector to the private sector, for example selling state-owned railways, airlines or telecom companies to shareholders.

StakeholderPossible benefitsPossible drawbacks
ConsumersMore competition can lead to lower prices, more choice and better quality.A private monopoly may raise prices; unprofitable services (e.g. rural routes) may be cut.
WorkersPossible higher pay and training linked to performance.Job losses as firms cut costs to raise profit.
BusinessesFreedom to raise finance and invest; incentive to be efficient.Pressure from shareholders for short-term profit.
GovernmentReceives revenue from the sale; no longer funds losses.Loses future profits and some control over key industries.

Key terms

Mixed economy
An economy where resources are allocated by both the private sector and the public sector.
Public sector
The part of the economy owned and controlled by the government.
Private sector
The part of the economy owned and controlled by individuals and businesses.
Market failure
When the market leads to an inefficient allocation of resources.
Public good
A good that is non-excludable and non-rival, so it would not be provided by the market.
Free-rider problem
When people benefit from a good without paying for it.
Privatisation
The transfer of ownership from the public sector to the private sector.
Exam-style question

Explain why street lighting is usually provided by the government rather than by private firms. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: street lighting is a public good.
  • 1 mark: it is non-excludable (people cannot be stopped from using it) and/or non-rival.
  • 1 mark: this creates a free-rider problem, so private firms cannot charge and make a profit; the government funds it through taxation.

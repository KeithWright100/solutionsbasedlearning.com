8. Price elasticity of supply and income elasticity of demand

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.1 The Market System · 1.1.4 Elasticity · Lesson 8 of 10

Learning Intention

To calculate and interpret PES and income elasticity of demand, and explain why elasticity matters to businesses and government.

Syllabus Statement

Price elasticity of supply (PES): definition, formula, calculation, interpretation and influencing factors, including for primary and manufactured products. Income elasticity of demand (YED): definition, formula and interpretation for luxury, normal and inferior goods. The significance of elasticities for businesses and government.

Success criterion 1

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

PES measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price

PES is positive because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. Values: 0 = perfectly inelastic; 0–1 = inelastic; 1 = unitary; >1 = elastic; ∞ = perfectly elastic.

Example: price rises 10% and quantity supplied rises 4% → PES = 0.4 (inelastic).

FactorEffect on PES
Factors of productionIf extra labour and machinery can easily be found or moved, supply is more elastic.
Availability of stocksFirms holding large stocks can respond quickly → more elastic.
Spare capacityFirms with unused capacity can raise output easily → more elastic.
TimeSupply is more elastic in the long run, when firms can build new factories or plant new crops.

Primary products (e.g. coffee, cocoa, rubber) usually have inelastic supply in the short run because crops take months or years to grow. Manufactured products (e.g. phones, clothing) are usually more elastic because factories can add shifts or use stocks.

Success criterion 2

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

Income elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in income.

YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income

Type of goodYED valueMeaning
Luxury goodPositive and greater than 1Demand rises more than proportionally as income rises, e.g. sports cars, cruises.
Normal goodPositive (between 0 and 1)Demand rises as income rises, e.g. clothing, meals out.
Inferior goodNegativeDemand falls as income rises, e.g. bus travel, cheap own-brand food.

Example: incomes rise by 5% and demand for bus travel falls by 2% → YED = −0.4, so bus travel is an inferior good.

Success criterion 3

Why elasticity matters

  • Businesses use PED to set prices: raise prices on inelastic products to increase revenue; cut prices on elastic products. YED helps them plan: in a boom, demand for luxuries grows fast; in a recession, inferior goods sell better.
  • Government places indirect taxes on goods with inelastic demand (petrol, tobacco, alcohol) because quantity falls little, so tax revenue is high. To reduce consumption of a harmful good, a tax works better if demand is elastic.
  • When an indirect tax is imposed, the more inelastic demand is, the more of the tax is passed on to consumers as a higher price. A subsidy raises output most when demand is elastic.

Key terms

Price elasticity of supply
The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.
Spare capacity
When a firm is producing below its maximum possible output.
Income elasticity of demand
The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in income.
Inferior good
A good with negative income elasticity: demand falls as income rises.
Luxury good
A good with income elasticity greater than 1.
Exam-style question

Explain why the supply of cocoa beans is likely to be price inelastic. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: cocoa is a primary product grown on trees.
  • 1 mark: it takes several years for new cocoa trees to grow and produce beans, so farmers cannot increase output quickly when price rises.
  • 1 mark: stocks are limited / perishable, so quantity supplied changes by a smaller % than price (PES < 1).

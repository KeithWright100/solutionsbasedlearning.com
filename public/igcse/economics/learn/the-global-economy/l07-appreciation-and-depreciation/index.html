7. Appreciation and depreciation

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 2.2 The Global Economy · 2.2.3 Exchange rates · Lesson 7 of 7

Learning Intention

To understand the effects of a rise or fall in the exchange rate on trade and the current account.

Syllabus Statement

Appreciation and revaluation; depreciation and devaluation; their effects on import and export prices, the demand for imports and exports, and the current account of the balance of payments; applying these effects to an economy.

Success criterion 1

Key definitions

  • Appreciation — a rise in the value of a currency in a floating system, caused by market forces.
  • Revaluation — a deliberate rise in the value of a fixed currency by the government or central bank.
  • Depreciation — a fall in the value of a floating currency caused by market forces.
  • Devaluation — a deliberate fall in the value of a fixed currency by the government.

Exam tip: remember SPICED: Strong Pound (currency), Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. And the reverse: a weak currency makes imports dearer and exports cheaper.

Success criterion 2

The effects of an appreciation

Worked example: the baht appreciates from $1 = 35 baht to $1 = 30 baht.

ProductPrice at 35 bahtPrice at 30 bahtEffect
Thai export: 700,000 baht car$20,000$23,333Exports dearer → demand for exports falls
US import: $100 pair of trainers3,500 baht3,000 bahtImports cheaper → demand for imports rises

Effect on the current account: export revenue falls and spending on imports rises, so the current account worsens (a deficit grows or a surplus shrinks). An appreciation can also help reduce inflation, because imported goods and raw materials are cheaper.

Success criterion 3

The effects of a depreciation

  • Export prices fall in foreign currency → foreign demand for exports rises.
  • Import prices rise in domestic currency → demand for imports falls.
  • The current account improves (a deficit shrinks).
  • Exporters and tourism benefit; output and jobs may rise.
  • However, imported inflation may rise: dearer imported fuel, food and raw materials increase costs (cost-push inflation).

How much the current account changes depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports: if demand is inelastic, quantities change little.

Activity: rise or fall after a depreciation?

Decide whether each is likely to rise or fall after the currency depreciates.

  • The foreign-currency price of exports
  • The domestic price of imported oil
  • The number of foreign tourists
  • Demand for imported cars
  • Export revenue (if demand is price elastic)
  • The risk of cost-push inflation

Key terms

Appreciation
A rise in the value of a floating currency.
Depreciation
A fall in the value of a floating currency.
Revaluation
A deliberate rise in the value of a fixed currency.
Devaluation
A deliberate fall in the value of a fixed currency.
Imported inflation
Inflation caused by rising prices of imported goods and materials.
Exam-style question

Discuss the likely effects of a depreciation of a country’s currency on its economy. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks: exports become cheaper and imports dearer, so demand for exports rises and imports fall, improving the current account.
  • Up to 2 marks: possible benefits for output and employment in exporting industries and tourism; but imported inflation as raw materials and fuel cost more.
  • Up to 2 marks: reasoned judgement, e.g. depends on price elasticity of demand for exports and imports and how dependent the country is on imported inputs.

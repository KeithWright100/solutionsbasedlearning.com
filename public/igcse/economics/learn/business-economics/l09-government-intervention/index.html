9. Government intervention in markets

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.2 Business Economics · 1.2.6 Government intervention · Lesson 9 of 10

Learning Intention

To evaluate how governments deal with externalities and promote competition.

Syllabus Statement

Taxation, subsidies, fines, regulation and pollution permits to deal with externalities, and their advantages and disadvantages; government regulation to promote competition, limit monopoly power, protect consumers and control mergers and takeovers.

Success criterion 1

Taxes and subsidies

When markets ignore external costs or benefits, governments can change prices to correct the market failure.

  • Taxation — a tax on goods with external costs (e.g. petrol, cigarettes, plastic bags) raises their price and reduces consumption. Advantages: raises revenue; the polluter pays. Disadvantages: if demand is inelastic, consumption falls only a little; taxes can hit poorer households hardest.
  • Subsidies — payments to producers of goods with external benefits (e.g. public transport, solar panels, vaccinations) lower price and increase consumption. Advantages: encourages beneficial goods. Disadvantages: costly for the government (opportunity cost); firms may become dependent.
Success criterion 2

Fines, regulation and pollution permits

MethodHow it worksAdvantagesDisadvantages
FinesPenalties for breaking rules, e.g. littering or illegal dumpingDeters harmful behaviour; raises revenueHard to detect and enforce; fine may be too small to deter large firms
RegulationLaws that set limits or ban activities, e.g. emission limits, smoking bansClear and direct; can ban the most harmful activitiesCostly to monitor; may raise firms’ costs and prices
Pollution permitsFirms are given or sold permits to pollute a set amount; unused permits can be soldCaps total pollution; rewards firms that cut emissionsHard to set the right number of permits; big firms can simply buy more
Success criterion 3

Promoting competition and protecting consumers

Governments use competition policy to stop firms abusing market power:

  • Promoting competition — banning collusion and cartels, fining firms that fix prices, opening markets to new firms.
  • Limiting monopoly power — regulators can cap prices of utilities (water, energy) or set service standards.
  • Protecting consumers — laws on product safety, accurate labelling and fair advertising.
  • Controlling mergers and takeovers — a competition authority can block a merger that would give one firm too much market share and lead to higher prices.

Key terms

Indirect tax
A tax on spending on goods and services.
Regulation
Rules set by government to control the behaviour of firms or consumers.
Pollution permit
A licence allowing a firm to emit a set amount of pollution, which can be traded.
Competition policy
Government action to promote competition and limit monopoly power.
Merger
The joining together of two firms.
Exam-style question

Evaluate the use of a tax on petrol to reduce air pollution from cars. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks: explain how the tax works: higher price reduces consumption of petrol, reducing emissions; polluter pays and revenue can fund public transport.
  • Up to 2 marks: limitations: demand for petrol is price inelastic, so consumption may fall only slightly; hits low-income drivers and rural households hardest.
  • Up to 2 marks: a reasoned judgement, e.g. a tax works better combined with subsidies for electric cars and public transport.

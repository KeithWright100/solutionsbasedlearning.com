2. Productivity

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.2 Business Economics · 1.2.2 Productivity and division of labour · Lesson 2 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand what productivity is and how the productivity of land, labour and capital can be increased.

Syllabus Statement

The meaning of productivity; how fertiliser, drainage, irrigation and reclamation affect land productivity; how education, training and migration affect labour productivity; how the quantity of capital and technological advances affect productivity.

Success criterion 1

What is productivity?

Productivity is a measure of output per unit of input per period of time. It shows how efficiently factors of production are used.

Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers

Worked example: a garment factory with 50 workers makes 4,000 shirts a week. Labour productivity = 4,000 ÷ 50 = 80 shirts per worker per week. If new sewing machines raise output to 5,000 shirts with the same workers, productivity rises to 100.

Higher productivity lowers the cost per unit, allowing firms to cut prices, raise wages or increase profits, and helps the whole economy grow.

Success criterion 2

Increasing the productivity of land

  • Fertiliser adds nutrients to soil, increasing crop yields per hectare.
  • Drainage removes excess water from waterlogged land so it can be farmed.
  • Irrigation supplies water to dry land, allowing more crops or more harvests per year.
  • Reclamation creates new usable land, e.g. from the sea (Singapore, the Netherlands) or by restoring damaged land.

These methods can have costs: fertiliser run-off can pollute rivers, and irrigation can lower water tables.

Success criterion 3

Increasing the productivity of labour and capital

  • Education and training improve human capital — the skills, knowledge and experience of workers — so each worker produces more or higher-quality output.
  • Migration can bring skilled workers into an economy, filling skill shortages; emigration of skilled workers (‘brain drain’) can reduce productivity.
  • More capital per worker (machinery, tools, computers) means each worker can produce more.
  • Technological advances (automation, robotics, better software, AI) make both labour and capital more productive.

Activity: which factor is made more productive?

Decide which factor of production each change mainly affects.

  • Irrigation channels on a dry farm
  • An apprenticeship scheme for electricians
  • Replacing old looms with faster machines
  • Draining a marsh for farmland
  • Hiring skilled nurses from abroad
  • A warehouse installs robotic pickers

Key terms

Productivity
Output per unit of input per period of time.
Labour productivity
Output per worker per period of time.
Human capital
The skills, knowledge and experience of workers.
Irrigation
Supplying water to land to help crops grow.
Reclamation
Creating new usable land, e.g. from the sea or derelict land.
Exam-style question

A factory employs 40 workers who produce 2,000 units a day. After training, the same workers produce 2,600 units a day. Calculate the change in labour productivity and explain one benefit to the firm. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: productivity before = 2,000 ÷ 40 = 50 units per worker.
  • 1 mark: productivity after = 2,600 ÷ 40 = 65 units per worker (an increase of 15 units, or 30%).
  • 1 mark: identify a benefit, e.g. lower cost per unit.
  • 1 mark: develop, e.g. the firm can lower prices to become more competitive or earn higher profit.

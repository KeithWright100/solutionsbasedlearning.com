8. The labour market

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.2 Business Economics · 1.2.5 The labour market · Lesson 8 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how the demand for and supply of labour determine wages and employment, and the role of trade unions.

Syllabus Statement

Demand for labour as a derived demand and its determinants; factors affecting the supply of labour; the importance of the quantity and quality of labour; labour market diagrams showing equilibrium wage and employment and the effect of shifts; the role of trade unions.

Success criterion 1

Demand for and supply of labour

The demand for labour is a derived demand: firms want workers not for their own sake, but because of the demand for the goods and services they produce. If demand for houses rises, demand for builders rises.

The demand for labour depends on:

  • demand for the final product;
  • the availability and cost of substitutes, including machines (automation);
  • the productivity of workers — more productive workers are worth employing.

The supply of labour depends on:

FactorEffect
Population size and migrationA larger population or net immigration increases the supply of workers.
Age distributionMore people of working age means more labour; an ageing population reduces it.
Retirement and school-leaving agesRaising the retirement age increases supply; raising the school-leaving age reduces it in the short run.
Female participationMore women entering paid work (e.g. with childcare support) increases supply.
Skills and qualificationsJobs needing long training (doctors, pilots) have a limited supply.
Mobility of labourGeographical mobility (moving area) and occupational mobility (changing job) affect how easily workers fill vacancies.

Businesses care about both the quantity and the quality of labour. Education and training improve human capital, raising productivity.

Success criterion 2

Labour market diagrams

Wages are the price of labour. The demand curve slopes down (firms hire more workers at lower wages); the supply curve slopes up (more people want to work at higher wages).

Success criterion 3

Trade unions

A trade union is an organisation of workers that acts on their behalf to protect and improve their pay and working conditions.

  • Collective bargaining — negotiating with employers as a group gives workers more power than individuals.
  • Higher wages — unions may push wages above the market equilibrium.
  • Better working conditions — health and safety, holidays, hours, protection against unfair dismissal.
  • Industrial action — if talks fail, unions may organise strikes or an overtime ban.

Drawbacks: higher wages raise firms’ costs and may lead to fewer jobs or more automation; strikes cause lost output.

Key terms

Derived demand
Demand for a factor of production that comes from the demand for the goods it produces.
Equilibrium wage
The wage at which the demand for labour equals the supply of labour.
Geographical mobility
The ease with which workers can move to a different area for work.
Trade union
An organisation of workers that protects and improves their pay and conditions.
Collective bargaining
Negotiation between a trade union and employers on behalf of workers.
Exam-style question

Using a labour market diagram, explain the likely effect on the wages of nurses of a large increase in the number of nurses migrating into a country. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: correctly labelled diagram (Wage rate, Quantity of labour) with DL and SL.
  • 1 mark: supply of labour shifted to the right (SL to SL1).
  • 1 mark: new equilibrium shows a lower wage and higher employment.
  • 1 mark: explanation: at the old wage there is an excess supply of nurses, so wages fall until equilibrium is restored.

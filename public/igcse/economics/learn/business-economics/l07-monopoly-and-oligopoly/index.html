7. Monopoly and oligopoly

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 1.2 Business Economics · 1.2.4 Business competition · Lesson 7 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand the features, barriers to entry and effects of monopoly and oligopoly.

Syllabus Statement

Monopoly: features, price-maker, barriers to entry (legal barriers, patents, marketing budgets, technology, start-up costs); advantages and disadvantages. Oligopoly: features, differentiated products, barriers to entry, collusion, price and non-price competition; advantages and disadvantages.

Success criterion 1

Monopoly and barriers to entry

A pure monopoly is when one business is the only supplier of a product. In practice, a firm with a large market share (in the UK, more than 25%) may be treated as having monopoly power.

  • One dominant firm controls the market.
  • A unique product with no close substitutes.
  • The monopolist is a price-maker: it can set the price (but then must accept the quantity consumers demand at that price).
  • High barriers to entry stop new firms competing.
Barrier to entryHow it blocks new firms
Legal barriersThe law gives one firm the sole right to supply, e.g. a state water company or postal service.
PatentsLegal protection for an invention (usually 20 years), e.g. a new medicine.
Large marketing budgetsHuge advertising makes it hard for newcomers to win customers.
TechnologyControl of advanced or secret technology others cannot copy.
High start-up costsBuilding a railway network or chip factory costs billions.

Success criterion 3

Oligopoly

An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms, e.g. mobile phone networks, supermarkets, airlines, soft drinks (Coca-Cola and PepsiCo).

  • Few, large firms hold most of the market share.
  • Differentiated products — each firm builds a distinct brand.
  • Barriers to entry such as brand loyalty and high start-up costs.
  • Interdependence — each firm must consider how rivals will react to its decisions.

Collusion is when firms secretly agree to fix prices or share out the market, acting like a monopoly. A formal agreement is a cartel (e.g. OPEC in oil). Collusion is illegal in most countries because it leads to higher prices.

Because price cuts can be quickly matched and may start a price war that hurts everyone, oligopolists often prefer non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty cards, product quality, packaging and after-sales service.

Key terms

Monopoly
A market with a single supplier (or one dominant firm).
Barriers to entry
Obstacles that prevent new firms entering a market.
Price-maker
A firm able to set the price of its product.
Oligopoly
A market dominated by a few large firms.
Collusion
When firms agree to fix prices or share a market.
Non-price competition
Competing through branding, quality, advertising and service rather than price.
Exam-style question

Assess the impact on consumers of a market dominated by a monopoly. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks: negative effects, e.g. higher prices, less choice, poorer quality because consumers cannot switch.
  • Up to 2 marks: possible benefits, e.g. economies of scale may lower costs and prices; profits funding innovation (e.g. new medicines protected by patents).
  • Up to 2 marks: a reasoned judgement, e.g. depends on whether the monopoly is regulated by government and whether economies of scale are passed on to consumers.

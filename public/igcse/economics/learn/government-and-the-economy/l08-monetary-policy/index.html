8. Monetary policy

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 2.1 Government and the Economy · 2.1.2 Government policies · Lesson 8 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how central banks use interest rates and asset purchases to influence the economy.

Syllabus Statement

Definition of monetary policy and interest rates; the role of central banks; how changes in interest rates affect consumers and businesses, and economic growth, inflation, unemployment and other objectives; the basic purpose of asset purchasing.

Success criterion 1

Interest rates and central banks

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates (and the money supply) to influence the economy, usually to control inflation.

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing money and the reward for saving.

Central banks (e.g. the Bank of Thailand, the Bank of England, the US Federal Reserve) set the base interest rate. Many are independent of government and have an inflation target (for example 1–3%). Commercial banks then adjust their lending and saving rates.

Success criterion 2

How interest rates affect consumers and businesses

Effect of a rise in interest ratesConsumersBusinesses
BorrowingLoans, mortgages and credit cards become more expensive, so households borrow and spend lessLoans for investment become more expensive, so firms invest less
SavingSaving becomes more attractive, so spending fallsFirms may hold cash rather than invest
Disposable incomeMortgage repayments rise, leaving less to spendInterest payments on existing debts rise, reducing profit
Confidence and demandLower spending reduces demand for firms’ productsFalling sales lead firms to cut output and hiring

A cut in interest rates has the opposite effects: borrowing is cheaper, saving is less attractive and spending rises.

Success criterion 3

Monetary policy and the objectives

ObjectiveHigher interest ratesLower interest rates
Economic growthSlowsRises
InflationFallsRises
UnemploymentRisesFalls
Exchange rateRises (foreign savers buy the currency), making exports dearerFalls, making exports cheaper

Asset purchasing (quantitative easing) — when interest rates are already close to zero, a central bank can create new money to buy assets such as government bonds from banks. This increases the money banks have to lend and pushes down long-term interest rates, encouraging borrowing and spending to boost growth and avoid deflation.

Activity: what happens if interest rates rise?

Decide whether each is likely to rise or fall after an increase in interest rates.

  • Consumer spending on cars bought with loans
  • The amount households save
  • Business investment in new machinery
  • Monthly mortgage repayments
  • The rate of inflation
  • Demand from foreign savers for the currency

Key terms

Monetary policy
Using interest rates and the money supply to influence the economy.
Interest rate
The cost of borrowing and the reward for saving.
Central bank
The bank that sets interest rates and manages a country’s currency, e.g. the Bank of Thailand.
Inflation target
The rate of inflation a central bank aims to achieve.
Asset purchasing (quantitative easing)
A central bank creating money to buy assets such as government bonds.
Exam-style question

Explain how an increase in interest rates may reduce inflation. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: borrowing becomes more expensive / saving more attractive.
  • 1 mark: consumers spend less (e.g. higher mortgage repayments reduce disposable income).
  • 1 mark: firms invest less.
  • 1 mark: total demand falls, reducing demand-pull pressure, so the rate of increase in prices slows.

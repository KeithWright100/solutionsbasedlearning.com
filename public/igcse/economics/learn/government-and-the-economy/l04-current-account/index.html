4. The current account of the balance of payments

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 2.1 Government and the Economy · 2.1.1 Macroeconomic objectives · Lesson 4 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand the current account, what causes deficits and surpluses, and why a deficit can be a problem.

Syllabus Statement

The current account of the balance of payments: deficit and surplus; trade in goods (visibles) and services (invisibles); the relationship with exchange rates; causes of deficits and surpluses (quality and price of domestic and foreign goods, exchange rates); consequences of a deficit (leakage, imported inflation, low demand for exports, funding with foreign reserves).

Success criterion 1

What is the current account?

The balance of payments records all money flowing into and out of a country. The current account mainly records trade in goods and services (plus income flows such as profits and remittances).

  • Trade in goods (visibles) — physical items: rice, cars, oil, electronics.
  • Trade in services (invisibles) — tourism, banking, insurance, shipping, education of overseas students.

Current account balance = value of exports − value of imports

A deficit means the value of imports exceeds exports; a surplus means exports exceed imports.

Success criterion 2

Causes of a deficit or surplus

  • Quality of domestic goods — high-quality products (e.g. German cars) win export orders → surplus.
  • Quality of foreign goods — if imports are seen as better, consumers buy more of them → deficit.
  • Price of domestic goods — high inflation or costs make exports uncompetitive → deficit.
  • Price of foreign goods — cheap imports (e.g. low-cost manufactured goods) → deficit.
  • Exchange rates — a strong currency makes exports dearer and imports cheaper → deficit; a weak currency does the opposite → surplus.

Exchange rates in practice: if $1 = 35 Thai baht, a $20,000 car from the USA costs 700,000 baht. If the baht strengthens to $1 = 30 baht, the same car costs only 600,000 baht, so Thai imports rise.

Activity: towards deficit or surplus?

Decide whether each change pushes the current account towards a deficit or a surplus.

  • Domestic inflation is much higher than in trading partners
  • A boom in foreign tourists visiting the country
  • The currency appreciates strongly
  • Domestic firms win a reputation for high quality
  • Consumers prefer imported brands
  • The currency depreciates

Success criterion 3

Consequences of a current account deficit

  • Leakage — money spent on imports leaves the economy, reducing demand for domestic firms and possibly jobs.
  • Imported inflation — if prices rise abroad, a country that relies on imports will see its own prices rise.
  • Low demand for exports — reflects weak competitiveness, leading to lower output and employment in export industries.
  • Funding the deficit — the deficit must be paid for using foreign currency reserves or borrowing from abroad; reserves can run out.

A deficit is not always a problem: it may be short-term, or caused by importing capital goods that will raise future output.

Key terms

Current account
The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services and income flows.
Visibles
Trade in physical goods.
Invisibles
Trade in services.
Current account deficit
When the value of imports exceeds the value of exports.
Foreign currency reserves
Stocks of foreign currency held by a central bank.
Exam-style question

Explain how an appreciation of a country’s currency may lead to a current account deficit. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: appreciation makes exports more expensive for foreign buyers.
  • 1 mark: it makes imports cheaper for domestic consumers.
  • 1 mark: export revenue falls and spending on imports rises, so imports exceed exports (deficit).

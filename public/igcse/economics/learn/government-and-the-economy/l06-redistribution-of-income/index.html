6. Redistribution of income

Edexcel IGCSE Economics · 2.1 Government and the Economy · 2.1.1 Macroeconomic objectives · Lesson 6 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand poverty and inequality and how governments redistribute income.

Syllabus Statement

Income inequality; absolute and relative poverty; reasons for reducing poverty and inequality (basic needs, standards of living, ethical reasons); progressive taxation, benefits, and investment in education and healthcare.

Success criterion 1

Poverty and inequality

  • Income inequality is the unequal distribution of income between households in an economy.
  • Absolute poverty is when people cannot afford basic needs such as food, clean water, shelter and clothing. The World Bank uses an international poverty line of about $2–3 per person per day.
  • Relative poverty is when people are poor compared with others in their society — often defined as income below 60% of the median income. It is possible to be in relative poverty in a rich country.
Success criterion 2

Why reduce poverty and inequality?

  • Meeting basic needs — everyone should have enough food, shelter, water and health care.
  • Raising standards of living — better health and education allow people to contribute more to the economy.
  • Ethical reasons — many people believe extreme inequality is unfair; it can also cause social tension and crime.
Success criterion 3

Policies to redistribute income

PolicyHow it reduces inequality and povertyPossible drawbacks
Progressive taxationThe percentage of income paid in tax rises as income rises, so high earners pay a larger share.Some high earners may work less, move abroad or avoid tax.
Benefit paymentsPayments to people on low incomes, the unemployed, elderly or disabled raise their income.Costly; may reduce the incentive to work if set too high.
Investment in educationGives people from poorer backgrounds skills to earn higher wages in future.Takes many years to have an effect; high opportunity cost.
Investment in healthcareFree or subsidised health care means poorer families are not pushed into poverty by illness, and stay able to work.Expensive and funded through taxes.

Progressive tax example: 0% on the first $10,000, 20% on income from $10,001 to $40,000, and 40% above $40,000. Someone earning $60,000 pays $0 + $6,000 + $8,000 = $14,000 (23% of income); someone earning $20,000 pays $2,000 (10%).

Activity: absolute or relative poverty?

Decide whether each describes absolute or relative poverty.

  • A family that cannot afford enough food each day
  • A household earning much less than the national median
  • No access to clean drinking water
  • A child unable to afford school trips that classmates go on
  • Living on less than $2 a day
  • A pensioner who cannot afford what most people consider normal

Key terms

Income inequality
The unequal distribution of income between households.
Absolute poverty
Being unable to afford basic needs such as food, water and shelter.
Relative poverty
Being poor compared with others in society, e.g. below 60% of median income.
Progressive tax
A tax where the percentage paid rises as income rises.
Benefits
Payments from the government to support people on low incomes.
Exam-style question

Explain how progressive taxation can reduce income inequality. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: progressive taxation takes a higher percentage of income from high earners.
  • 1 mark: this reduces the gap in disposable (after-tax) income between rich and poor.
  • 1 mark: revenue can be used to fund benefits or public services for low-income households.

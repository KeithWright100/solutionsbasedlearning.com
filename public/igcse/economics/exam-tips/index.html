IGCSE Economics

Examination Tips & Advice

How to read command words, manage your time, and structure data-response and extended answers so every point you make actually earns marks.

Extended answers

Develop your ability to answer 6, 8 and 12 mark exam questions

1. Know your command words

Economics questions build in difficulty across each part — part (a) is usually definition or calculation, later parts ask you to explain, analyse and evaluate the same context.

Command wordWhat it wants
Define / StateA short, accurate economics definition or fact — no explanation needed.
CalculateShow your working, not just the final number — method marks are available even if the answer is wrong.
ExplainA chain of reasoning: cause → effect → effect. One well-developed chain beats several short, disconnected points.
AnalyseExplain in more depth, usually applied directly to the data or context given (a named business, country or industry).
Discuss / Evaluate / Assess / To what extentConsider more than one side (e.g. advantages and disadvantages, short run and long run) and reach a justified judgement.

2. Manage your time by the mark

Work on roughly one minute per mark. A 2-mark question needs a couple of sentences; a 12-mark evaluative question needs a full, structured argument — don’t let an early low-mark question eat into the time you need later.

  • Check the mark allocation for every part before you start the question.
  • For calculation questions, always show your formula and working — you can pick up method marks even with an arithmetic slip.
  • Save enough time for the final evaluative part of each question — it usually carries the most marks in the whole question.

3. Structuring your answers

The chain of reasoning

For “explain” and “analyse” questions, use a clear chain: state the point → explain the economic mechanism → explain the consequence. For example: “A rise in interest rates (point) increases the cost of borrowing, so consumers spend less on credit-financed goods (mechanism), which reduces aggregate demand and slows economic growth (consequence).”

Evaluative (discuss / evaluate / to what extent) questions

  • One side — develop a full chain of reasoning for one view, applied to the data or context in the question.
  • The other side — develop an equally full chain for the counter-argument, not just a one-line mention.
  • Judgement — conclude which factor matters most, and why (e.g. depends on the size of the change, the time period, or the type of business/economy).

Always refer back to the specific context given — the named business, country, or figures in the resource booklet — rather than writing a generic answer that could apply to any question on the topic.

4. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Defining a term instead of explaining or analysing it once the question has moved past part (a).
  • Giving a list of unconnected points instead of one or two fully developed chains of reasoning.
  • Forgetting to apply your answer to the specific business, country or data given in the question.
  • Skipping your working on calculation questions — the answer alone often can’t earn full marks.
  • Writing an evaluation with only one side of the argument, or no final judgement.

5. Final checklist before you submit

  • I’ve answered the command word, not just the topic.
  • My calculations show full working, not just the final figure.
  • My explanations are full chains of reasoning, not lists of points.
  • My evaluative answers cover both sides and end with a clear judgement.
  • I’ve applied my answer to the specific context or data in the question.

IGCSE History

Examination Tips & Advice

How to read command words, manage your time, and handle sources so your answers do exactly what the examiner is looking for.

Extended answers

Develop your ability to answer 6, 8 and 12 mark exam questions

1. Know your command words

History questions test different skills — knowledge, source analysis, and judgement — and the command word tells you which one is being tested.

Command wordWhat it wants
Describe / State / GiveAccurate factual knowledge, in your own words — no sources or explanation needed.
Explain whyCausation: give a reason, then explain how it led to the outcome. Several linked reasons score higher than one.
What can you learn from Source A about…Read the source closely and infer what it shows or suggests, using its content and, where relevant, its provenance (who wrote/produced it, when, why).
How useful is Source A for…Weigh up the source’s content and its provenance (author, date, purpose, audience) against what you know from your own knowledge.
In what ways… / How did… changeIdentify and explain more than one way or factor, usually across a time period.
How far do you agree… / To what extent…A balanced argument with evidence on both sides, ending in a supported judgement.

2. Manage your time by the mark

Work on roughly one minute per mark. Source-analysis questions can feel slow because you have to read carefully first — build that reading time into your plan rather than letting it eat into your writing time.

  • Read every source and caption twice before answering a source-based question.
  • Give the highest-mark question (usually the extended “how far do you agree” essay) the most time and a clear plan before you start writing.
  • Don’t retell the whole story of an event when the question only asks about causes, or only about consequences — stay tightly focused on what’s asked.

3. Structuring your answers

Using sources

Never just describe what a source shows — use it as evidence for a point, and where the question asks for usefulness or reliability, weigh up its content against its provenance (who made it, when, and why) and against your own knowledge of the period.

Explain why / causation questions

Use a Point → Evidence → Explain structure for each cause, and try to link causes together where possible (e.g. one cause making another more likely) — this shows a more sophisticated understanding than a simple list.

The extended essay (“how far do you agree”)

  • Case for — develop the strongest evidence supporting the statement.
  • Case against — develop the strongest evidence against it, given equal weight.
  • Judgement — decide which factor(s) mattered most, and justify why, rather than simply restating both sides.

4. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Describing a source instead of using it as evidence, or ignoring provenance when the question asks about usefulness or reliability.
  • Retelling everything you know about an event instead of answering the specific question asked.
  • Giving a list of causes with no explanation of how each one led to the outcome.
  • Writing a one-sided “how far do you agree” essay, or one with no final judgement.
  • Confusing similar dates, names or events under exam pressure — precise recall matters.

5. Final checklist before you submit

  • I’ve answered the command word, not just retold the story.
  • I’ve used sources as evidence, including provenance where the question asks for it.
  • My “explain why” answers are chains of reasoning, not lists.
  • My extended essay covers both sides and ends with a clear judgement.
  • I’ve checked I haven’t missed a question or part-question.

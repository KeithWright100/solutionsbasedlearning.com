19. SALT 1 and the extent of détente in 1972

Success criterion 1

SALT 1, May 1972

Several years of difficult negotiations led to the Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty, signed in May 1972 (called SALT 1 after a second treaty in 1979). It had three parts:

PartTerms
Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) TreatyABMs allowed at only two sites in each country, with a maximum of 100 missiles each
Interim TreatyLimits on ICBMs and SLBMs until 1977; the Soviet Union was allowed more ICBMs (1,618 v 1,054) because the USA had more bombers; both 740 SLBMs
Basic Principles AgreementRules for avoiding nuclear war: no warheads on the seabed and both sides to “make every effort” to avoid war
Success criterion 2

Weaknesses of SALT 1

  • It was unrealistic to think a piece of paper would stop a nuclear war if one looked likely.
  • Both countries still had enough weapons to destroy each other many times over.
  • It did not cover the latest technology: MIRVs (multiple independently-targetable re-entry vehicles) carrying several warheads on one missile. The USA already had them but did not admit it, so it was happy to appear generous.
Success criterion 3

The extent of détente in 1972

SALT 1 had major symbolic importance: both superpowers wanted agreement and to show better relations publicly. Shortly after signing, Nixon visited Brezhnev in Moscow, and in 1973 Brezhnev visited Washington. Negotiations for SALT 2 began in 1974. Together with the 1960s treaties, the Shanghai Communique and Ostpolitik, relations in 1972 were far better than at the time of Cuba — although both sides kept huge arsenals and their rivalry continued.

Key terms

SALT 1
The Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty signed in May 1972.
ABM
An anti-ballistic missile, designed to shoot down incoming missiles.
SLBM
A submarine-launched ballistic missile.
MIRV
A missile carrying several warheads, each able to hit a different target.
Interim
Temporary or short term.

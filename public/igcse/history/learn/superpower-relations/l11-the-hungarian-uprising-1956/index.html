11. The Hungarian Uprising, 1956

Edexcel IGCSE History · A world divided: superpower relations, 1943–72 · 3. The Cold War in the 1950s · Lesson 11 of 19

Learning Intention

To understand why the Soviet Union invaded Hungary in 1956 and the international reaction.

Syllabus Statement

The impact of Soviet rule on Hungary, Rakosi, de-Stalinisation, Nagy and his demands. Reasons for the Soviet invasion of Hungary, its effects and the international reaction.

Success criterion 1

Soviet rule and de-Stalinisation

From 1945 Hungary was under strict Soviet control: non-communist parties were banned and key officials chosen by Stalin. The Communist leader Matyas Rakosi, a firm Stalin supporter, dealt harshly with opponents: from 1949 to 1956 more than 300,000 Hungarians were imprisoned and over 2,000 executed.

Khrushchev’s 1956 speech criticising Stalin (de-Stalinisation) encouraged satellite states to push for less Soviet control. In Poland, Gomulka introduced moderate reforms, which encouraged the Hungarians.

Success criterion 2

Nagy, his demands and the invasion

DateEvent
1956Protests over lack of political freedom, fuel shortages and poor harvests
October 1956Riots in Budapest. Khrushchev sends in the Red Army, replaces Rakosi with Imre Nagy, and the army withdraws
End of OctoberNagy proposes leaving the Warsaw Pact, becoming neutral, and sharing power with non-communist groups
4 November 1956Soviet tanks enter Budapest. Up to 20,000 Hungarians are killed; Janos Kadar becomes leader
1958Nagy is executed — “a lesson to the leaders of all Socialist countries”, said Khrushchev

Why did Khrushchev invade? If Hungary left the Warsaw Pact, others might follow, and the whole strategy of surrounding the Soviet Union with pro-communist governments would be under threat.

Success criterion 3

International reaction and effects

The rebels expected Western help: Radio Free Europe had encouraged rebellion and the USA had given Marshall Aid. But no military support came. Containment meant stopping communism spreading, not interfering in an already communist country, and an attack on a Soviet satellite could start a nuclear war. Britain, France and the USA were also arguing over the Suez Canal. Some NATO countries took in refugees.

Effects on the Soviet UnionEffects on the West and relations
Khrushchev’s position in the Soviet Union and the Warsaw Pact was strengthened; members knew they could not expect US helpThe West was seen to encourage rebellion without backing it up
Khrushchev became more confident that the USA would not risk military actionThe USA strongly opposed the invasion, damaging relations and ending the thaw

Key terms

De-Stalinisation
Khrushchev’s criticism of Stalin and his repressive policies.
Matyas Rakosi
The harsh Stalinist leader of Hungary until 1956.
Imre Nagy
The reforming Hungarian prime minister executed after the uprising.
Neutral
Not belonging to either side or alliance.
Radio Free Europe
A US-funded radio station encouraging Eastern Europeans to rebel.

11. The Kornilov Revolt

Success criterion 1

Kerensky and Kornilov

Kerensky took credit for putting down the July Days and became leader of the Provisional Government. To restore army discipline and order in the cities, on 18 July 1917 he appointed General Kornilov as commander-in-chief, and they agreed to bring troops to Petrograd in case of unrest.

Kornilov wanted martial law and the soviets broken; many middle-class people supported this. Kerensky saw it as a challenge and dismissed him, but on 24 August Kornilov sent troops to Petrograd to shut down the Soviet.

Success criterion 2

The revolt fails

  • Kerensky feared a military takeover, so he allowed the Bolsheviks to arm their supporters to defend the city.
  • The Bolsheviks organised defences with armed Red Guard units.
  • Railway workers blocked Kornilov’s route.
  • Bolsheviks persuaded Kornilov’s troops to stop and desert their officers.
  • Kornilov was arrested on 1 September.
Success criterion 3

Impact: a turning point

Provisional GovernmentBolsheviks
Kerensky looked weak because he relied on the Bolsheviks; the Provisional Government lost crucial supportThe Bolsheviks appeared to be the defenders of the revolution
Army discipline collapsed; hundreds of officers were murdered and tens of thousands desertedMore Bolsheviks were elected; on 25 September Trotsky became president of the Petrograd Soviet

Key terms

General Kornilov
The commander-in-chief who sent troops towards Petrograd in August 1917.
Kornilov Revolt
Kornilov’s failed attempt in August 1917 to shut down the Petrograd Soviet.
Martial law
Rule by the army, with normal laws suspended.
Counter-revolution
An attempt to reverse a revolution.
Desert
To run away from the army.

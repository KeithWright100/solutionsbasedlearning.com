Success criterion 1

Project C in Birmingham

The Project C (for “confrontation”) campaign was led by the SNCC, SCLC and the ACMHR (Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights) to end segregation in Birmingham, Alabama. Birmingham was chosen because:

  • it had not desegregated any facilities at all;
  • the ACMHR was well led by Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth;
  • nearly 45 per cent of the city’s half a million people were African American;
  • it had a very active Ku Klux Klan (the city was nicknamed “Bombingham”) and police chief “Bull” Connor, so violence against peaceful protestors seemed almost certain, bringing publicity.
DateEvent
3 April 1963The first march; police begin arresting protestors, including King and Shuttlesworth.
2 May 1963The first large children’s march: around 1,000 children, some as young as 6, are arrested.
3 May 1963Connor sets dogs and powerful fire hoses on child marchers. Footage is shown around the world.
10 May 1963Talks begin, but state troops sent by the Governor of Alabama disrupt them; violence on 11–12 May leads to the first serious black riots.
Success criterion 2

The impact of Birmingham

Kennedy said he felt “sick and ashamed” and sent Federal troops to restore calm. Most white businessmen preferred concessions to losing trade, so most shops and lunch counters were desegregated and African Americans could apply for some jobs previously for whites only. However, many facilities stayed segregated and violence continued.

Similar protests spread and more cities desegregated. Most importantly, Kennedy feared more rioting, and the civil rights bill his administration had begun working on gained new importance. On 11 June 1963 he called it “a moral crisis as a country and a people”.

Success criterion 3

The March on Washington

Protestors wanted to show support for the civil rights bill being debated in Congress. Kennedy tried to persuade them to call it off; 1,000 extra police and 2,000 National Guard were placed on stand-by, but were not needed. On 28 August 1963, exactly 100 years after slavery ended, over 250,000 people, black and white, marched for “Jobs and Freedom” to the Lincoln Memorial. It was broadcast live.

King was the final speaker. His “I have a dream” speech referred to the Constitution, the Declaration of Independence, Abraham Lincoln, the Bible and the American Dream. In 1964 he was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.

Importance: it showed huge support for civil rights from both races; it was peaceful and well organised; celebrities such as Bob Dylan brought publicity; it pressured Congress during the bill’s debate; and it fixed King as leader of the whole movement. Kennedy assured leaders he was committed to the bill, but he was assassinated in November 1963 and Vice President Lyndon Johnson, a Southern Democrat from Texas, became president.

Key terms

Project C
The 1963 Birmingham campaign of sit-ins, boycotts and marches; C stood for “confrontation”.
ACMHR
The Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights, led by Fred Shuttlesworth.
“Bull” Connor
The Birmingham police chief who used dogs and fire hoses on protestors.
March on Washington
The march of over 250,000 people for “Jobs and Freedom” on 28 August 1963.
American Dream
The idea that anyone in the USA can succeed through hard work because everyone has the same opportunities.

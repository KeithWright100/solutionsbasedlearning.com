Success criterion 1

Students and protest

By 1970 nearly 40 per cent of young people went on to higher education. Most were white and middle class, and their parents expected them to conform. During the 1960s students campaigned on civil rights, gay rights, women’s rights, poverty, foreign policy, nuclear disarmament and the environment. Many rejected older people’s views; a popular slogan was “Don’t trust anyone over 30”. Most activists were left-wing, and many called society “the machine” or “the system”. Some students supported traditional conservative views, but they were less influential.

Success criterion 2

Students for a Democratic Society

SDS held its first meeting in 1960 at the University of Michigan. Its 1962 Port Huron Statement said it would campaign against “racial injustice, war and the violation of human rights”.

  • Many members took part in the sit-ins and freedom rides of 1961–62.
  • Its first focus was student rights: sit-ins and rallies for more say in university policies and against strict rules, such as curfews for returning to accommodation.
  • By the end of the decade it had groups at 150 colleges, with 100,000 members; membership rose dramatically in 1966 because of opposition to the Vietnam War.
Success criterion 3

The Berkeley Free Speech Movement, 1964

DateEvent
September 1964Students at the University of California, Berkeley, some back from Freedom Summer, protest against racial discrimination. The university bans protests on campus for “off campus political and social action”.
Autumn 1964Students ignore the ban; some are suspended. Around 400 sign a petition demanding suspension too. Police arrest Jack Weinberg, but students block the police car for 32 hours.
Late 1964The Free Speech Movement (FSM) forms, led by Weinberg and Mario Savio. At its peak nearly 14,000 students, about half, support it.
2 December 1964After four students are charged, around 6,000 support a rally and sit-in; police take 12 hours to make 750 arrests.
Days laterStaff vote for no limits on campus protest, but detailed rules remain. Those arrested are convicted and fined; leaders get 1 to 4 months in prison.

Achievements: as the FSM became more confrontational it lost moderate students and never had local support; after a swear word appeared on signs it was mocked as the “Filthy Speech Movement”. Student protests often won rights at college, but few other aims, partly because of the huge variety of issues. One issue united them: opposition to the Vietnam War.

Key terms

SDS
Students for a Democratic Society, a student protest organisation founded in 1960.
Port Huron Statement
The 1962 SDS statement of its aims.
Free Speech Movement
The 1964 Berkeley campaign for the right to protest and speak freely on campus.
Left-wing
Liberal, progressive views favouring equality and government action.
Right-wing
Conservative views that value tradition and individual freedom, with minimal government.

22. Women’s Liberation, abortion and opposition

Success criterion 1

The Women’s Liberation Movement

NOW wanted big changes within society as it was. Women’s Liberation (Women’s Lib) feminists wanted to destroy the existing system and free women from patriarchal society; some groups regarded men as the enemy.

Their early protests were aggressive, such as sit-ins in offices that created sexist adverts. At the 1968 Miss America contest in Atlantic City they threw make-up and other products into a rubbish bin and crowned a sheep Miss America. The protest got huge, mostly negative, coverage (the “bra-burning” myth began here, though there is little evidence bras were burned).

Many groups then set up small local discussion groups, popularising the idea that “the personal was political”. By 1974 they were also helping women deal with rape and domestic violence.

Success criterion 2

Abortion and Roe versus Wade

In 1960 abortion was illegal unless the mother’s life was threatened. Campaigners argued it was a woman’s right to choose and that illegal abortions were dangerous. They held marches and “speak outs”.

DateEvent
1970New York State allows abortion on demand until the 24th week; Alaska, California, Hawaii, Oregon and Washington pass similar laws.
1970Norma McCorvey, as “Jane Roe”, takes Dallas County District Attorney Henry Wade to court for the right to an abortion. She wins, but only for her case, so the case is appealed to the Supreme Court.
22 January 1973By seven votes to two, the Supreme Court rules abortion laws break a woman’s right to privacy under the 14th Amendment. Abortions after 6 months are allowed only if the woman’s life is in danger.

Abortion became more available across the USA and most of the women’s movement saw it as a huge victory, but it was the most controversial issue of all.

Success criterion 3

Opposition and impact

Protestors were often abused and media coverage mocked them. Other protest movements rarely supported women, sharing society’s traditional views. Some believed in traditional gender roles; far more opposed particular demands, above all abortion, which produced an anti-feminist backlash.

Phyllis Schlafly, a married Catholic mother of six who had stood for Congress in 1952, believed women’s natural role was housewife and mother. When Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) in 1972, she founded “Stop ERA”, arguing women might have to serve in the military, lose financial support from husbands, and that it would weaken the family. Ratification was delayed until the time limit in 1982, when only 35 of the 38 states needed had ratified.

Key terms

Women’s Liberation
The radical part of the women’s movement that wanted to destroy patriarchal society.
Patriarchal
A society where men hold the power and authority.
Roe versus Wade
The 1973 Supreme Court case that made abortion more widely available.
Equal Rights Amendment
A proposed amendment making women totally equal to men under the Constitution.
Phyllis Schlafly
The anti-feminist leader who founded Stop ERA.

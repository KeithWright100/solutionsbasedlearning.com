5. Segregation and discrimination in 1950

Learning Intention

Success criterion 1

Segregation in the South and the North

After the American Civil War (1861–65) black slaves were freed and, under the Constitution, African Americans were equal citizens. In practice they faced racism, discrimination and violence well into the 20th century.

RegionSegregation
South“Jim Crow” laws, passed by state legislatures at the end of the 19th century, segregated almost all aspects of life: separate schools, separate areas in restaurants, libraries, cinemas and parks, and separation on public transport. Facilities for African Americans were almost always poorer.
NorthFewer legal barriers, but racism meant African Americans mostly lived separately. Black workers generally earned half of what a white person earned for the same job and had higher unemployment, so they could only afford the poorest areas. Facilities were often segregated without the need for laws.
Success criterion 2

Voting and violence

All US citizens had the right to vote, but in the 1950s very few African Americans in the South could. Southern states prevented black people from registering by:

  • a difficult literacy test (white people were exempted);
  • a poll tax most African Americans could not afford;
  • violence or threats of violence against those who tried to register.

Black men suspected of crimes were often attacked by white mobs. Lynchings had declined by the 1950s but were greatly feared. Some police were racist themselves, and white people accused of attacking African Americans were usually found “not guilty” by all-white, all-male juries.

Activity: North or South?

Was each feature mainly found in the North or the South in 1950?

  • “Jim Crow” laws covering almost all aspects of life
  • Segregation without the need for laws
  • Literacy tests and poll taxes to stop black people voting
  • African Americans living in the poorest areas because of low wages
  • Separate seating on public transport required by law
  • Fewer legal barriers to equality

Success criterion 3

Why did government accept segregation?

  • Congress often needed the support of Southern politicians, who were racist themselves or did not want to annoy racist voters.
  • Presidents needed Southern support for other policies, so even those who wanted change did not act.
  • The Supreme Court was independent and could have banned segregation laws, but in Plessy versus Ferguson (1896) it had ruled that separate facilities were allowed if they were equal. They rarely were, but this precedent was used again and again against civil rights cases. Many judges were also against civil rights.

Senator James Eastland of Mississippi claimed in 1954 that “segregation is not discrimination” and that it promoted racial harmony.

Key terms

Segregation
Keeping people of different races apart, for example in schools and on transport.
Discrimination
Treating a group of people unfairly because of their race or another characteristic.
Jim Crow laws
Southern state laws that kept black and white people separate.
Lynching
A killing, usually a hanging, by a group, of someone suspected of a crime but not tried.
Plessy versus Ferguson
The 1896 Supreme Court ruling that “separate but equal” facilities were allowed.
Precedent
A ruling in a law case that is used by other courts when deciding similar cases.

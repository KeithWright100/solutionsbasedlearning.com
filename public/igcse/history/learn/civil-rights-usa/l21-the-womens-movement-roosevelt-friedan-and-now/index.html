Success criterion 1

Women in 1960 and Eleanor Roosevelt

The world wars had increased women’s jobs, but by 1960 most Americans still believed a woman’s main role was to look after the home. Women were expected to give up work when they married, and some companies dismissed married women.

Eleanor Roosevelt, First Lady from 1933, campaigned for women’s rights until 1962:

  • She held press conferences open only to women, so newspapers had to employ female journalists.
  • She pressured presidents to employ more women and wrote her own newspaper column.
  • She backed Kennedy only if he set up a commission on women; in December 1961 he created the President’s Commission on the Status of Women, with her as chair (she died before it reported).

The report found women overwhelmingly in low-paid jobs: only 4 per cent of lawyers and 7 per cent of doctors were women, and women earned 50–60 per cent of a man’s pay for the same job. Partly as a result, Kennedy signed the Equal Pay Act in June 1963, and the 1964 Civil Rights Act made gender discrimination illegal.

Success criterion 2

Factors for change and Betty Friedan

By 1960 households needed more than one wage to afford gadgets and appliances, and appliances saved time for paid work. The contraceptive pill, available from 1960, let women plan families, education and careers; in 1965 (Griswold versus Connecticut) the Supreme Court said all married women could use birth control, extended to all women in 1972.

Most historians credit journalist Betty Friedan with starting the movement. Her 1963 book The Feminine Mystique challenged the myth that women could only be fulfilled as housewives and mothers. After interviewing fellow graduates at her 1957 college reunion, she found many educated women bored and unhappy, described their homes as “comfortable prison camps”, and urged them to find fulfilment in paid work. It became a bestseller.

Success criterion 3

NOW

New laws were not enough, so in June 1966 Friedan and others set up the National Organization for Women (NOW) as a pressure group, with Friedan as first president. Its 1967 Bill of Rights included the Equal Rights Amendment, enforcing the ban on sex discrimination in jobs, maternity leave, childcare facilities, and removing limits on contraception and abortion.

  • By 1970 it had about 40,000 members and used marches, strikes, petitions, lobbying and legal action.
  • Between 1966 and 1971 it won $30 million in back pay for women.
  • In February 1970 members disrupted the Senate to demand the Equal Rights Amendment.
  • On 26 August 1970 it organised the Women’s Strike for Equality; about 50,000 marched down New York’s Fifth Avenue.

Critics said NOW was too radical (supporting the ERA and abortion) or not radical enough, and its mostly middle- and upper-class supporters did little for poor women.

Key terms

Feminist
Someone who believes men and women should have equal rights and opportunities.
Equal Pay Act
The 1963 law making it illegal to pay people different rates for the same job.
The Feminine Mystique
Betty Friedan’s 1963 book challenging the idea that women should only be housewives.
NOW
The National Organization for Women, set up in June 1966.
Lobby
Try to persuade a government to change or make a law.

10. The Depression and the Manchurian Incident, 1931

Success criterion 1

The Great Depression

Between 1929 and 1931 Japanese exports fell by 50 per cent, wages fell and unemployment rose. The collapse of the American market for silk hit small farmers hard. In 1931 the rice crop failed in northern Japan and famine followed; some peasants sold their daughters to brothels. Most army recruits and young officers came from the countryside, so the army was deeply affected.

Many Japanese now believed they must protect their “special interests” in China, by taking full control of Manchuria if necessary, especially as Chiang Kai-Shek’s Guomindang threatened to reclaim it.

Success criterion 2

The Manchurian Incident

Tensions grew in 1931: Chinese farmers attacked Korean workers, and a Japanese intelligence officer was shot as a spy. Two officers of the Guandong Army (Japan’s field army in Manchuria), Itagaki Seishiro and Ishiwara Kanji, planned a takeover without authorisation from Tokyo. An order from the war minister to stop them was deliberately delayed.

On the evening of 18 September 1931 a section of the Japanese-owned South Manchurian Railway was blown up near Mukden. The Guandong Army blamed the Chinese, but it is generally believed Japan staged it as an excuse to invade.

Success criterion 3

Consequences

By 1932 all of Manchuria was in Japanese hands. The incident sparked powerful nationalism among ordinary people hit by the Depression. Hirohito worried that Japan had broken treaties outlawing war, such as the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928), and said he could not sleep. The Guandong Army had ignored his wishes, showing a deep crisis in political authority.

In 1932 Manchuria became Manchukuo, a Japanese puppet state ruled by Pu Yi, the last emperor of China, providing iron, coal and land. The League of Nations condemned Japan after the Lytton Commission’s report, but Japan went on to occupy Harbin and Shanghai (1932) and Jehol (1933).

Key terms

Guandong Army
Japan’s field army stationed in Manchuria.
Manchurian Incident
The blowing up of the South Manchurian Railway on 18 September 1931, used as an excuse to invade.
Manchukuo
The Japanese puppet state created in Manchuria in 1932.
Lytton Commission
The League of Nations investigation into the Manchurian Incident.
Puppet state
A country that appears independent but is controlled by another.

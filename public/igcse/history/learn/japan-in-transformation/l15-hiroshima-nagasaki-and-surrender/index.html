15. Hiroshima, Nagasaki and Japan’s surrender

Success criterion 1

The Potsdam Declaration

At Potsdam (17 July–2 August 1945) Attlee, Truman and Stalin discussed the post-war world. On 26 July 1945 a declaration called on Japan to surrender unconditionally or face “complete and utter destruction”. After the fire bombings, this meant little to Japan, which did not know of the atomic bomb; Truman had told only the British leader, not Stalin. Despite a deep split, the government rejected the declaration. US Secretary of War Henry Stimson said the atomic bomb was “an eminently suitable weapon” to show the ultimatum meant what it said.

Success criterion 2

Hiroshima and Nagasaki

On 6 August 1945 the B-29 bomber Enola Gay dropped an atomic bomb, “Little Boy”, on Hiroshima. About 13 square kilometres were reduced to rubble, 70,000 buildings destroyed and an estimated 80,000 people killed instantly. The heat at the centre was three times that needed to melt iron. Survivors described people with skin peeled off their faces.

On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and attacked Manchuria; many Japanese now knew they had lost. On 9 August a second bomb was dropped on Nagasaki.

Success criterion 3

Surrender

On 10 August the cabinet accepted the Potsdam terms, asking only that the emperor remain sovereign; the Allies agreed. At noon on 15 August 1945 Hirohito’s recorded message was broadcast, and millions wept. It was the first time Japan faced being ruled by outsiders.

US troops arrived in Tokyo a fortnight later under General Douglas MacArthur, whose tasks were disarmament and demilitarisation. On 2 September 1945 Japan formally surrendered on the USS Missouri. MacArthur compared himself to Perry 92 years earlier.

Key terms

Potsdam Declaration
The Allies’ demand of 26 July 1945 that Japan surrender unconditionally.
Enola Gay
The B-29 bomber that dropped the atomic bomb on Hiroshima.
Unconditional surrender
Surrender without any guaranteed terms.
USS Missouri
The US battleship where Japan formally surrendered on 2 September 1945.
Demilitarisation
Removing a country’s armed forces and weapons.

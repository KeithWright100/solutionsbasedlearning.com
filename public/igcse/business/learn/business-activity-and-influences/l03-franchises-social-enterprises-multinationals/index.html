3. Franchises, social enterprises and multinationals

Edexcel IGCSE Business · 1 Business activity and influences on business · 1.2 Types of organisations · Lesson 3 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand how franchises, social enterprises and multinationals are organised.

Syllabus Statement

Different forms of business organisation: franchises, social enterprises, multinationals.

Success criterion 1

Franchises

A franchise is an agreement where one business (the franchisor) allows another business (the franchisee) to use its name, logo and way of operating in return for a fee and a share of the revenue (a royalty). Fast-food chains, coffee shops and gyms often grow this way.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
FranchisorGrows quickly without paying for every new outlet; receives fees and royaltiesLess control over quality; a bad franchisee can damage the brand
FranchiseeUses a well-known brand, so less risk; gets training, support and national advertisingMust pay fees and royalties; has to follow strict rules on products and prices

Activity: franchisor or franchisee?

Who gets each benefit or drawback?

  • Benefits from the brand’s national advertising
  • Expands without paying for new premises
  • Must pay a royalty on revenue
  • Its reputation can be damaged by poor service in one outlet
  • Receives training in how to run the outlet
  • Receives a regular stream of fees

Success criterion 2

Social enterprises

A social enterprise is a business whose main objective is to help society or the environment, rather than to maximise profit for its owners. It still sells goods or services and must cover its costs, but most profits are reinvested in its social purpose.

Examples include a café that employs and trains young people who were homeless, or a company that sells water filters and uses its profits to fund clean-water projects. Social enterprises can attract customers who want their spending to do good, but may find it harder to raise finance from investors who want high returns.

Success criterion 3

Multinationals

A multinational (multinational company, MNC) is a business that has its headquarters in one country and operates (produces or provides services) in more than one country. Simply selling (exporting) abroad does not make a business a multinational: it must have factories, offices or outlets in other countries.

Multinationals are usually very large public limited companies, for example car makers, technology companies and fast-food chains. You will study the benefits and drawbacks of multinationals in more detail in 1.5 Business and the international economy.

Key terms

Franchise
An agreement allowing a business to trade using another business’s name and methods.
Franchisor
The business that owns the brand and sells the franchise.
Franchisee
The business that buys the right to use the franchisor’s brand.
Royalty
A payment to the franchisor, usually a percentage of revenue.
Social enterprise
A business whose main aim is to benefit society, reinvesting its profits.
Multinational
A business with operations in more than one country.
Exam-style question

Analyse one advantage to an entrepreneur of opening a franchise rather than starting an independent business. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for an advantage: e.g. using an established brand.
  • 1 mark for development: e.g. customers already recognise and trust the brand.
  • 1 mark for a consequence: e.g. so sales are likely to be higher from the start and the risk of failure is lower.

Model answer: A franchise uses a brand that customers already know and trust. This means the entrepreneur does not need to build a reputation from nothing, so sales should be higher from the first day and the risk of the business failing is lower than for an unknown independent business.

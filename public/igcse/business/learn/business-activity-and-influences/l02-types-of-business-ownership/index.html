2. Types of business ownership

Edexcel IGCSE Business · 1 Business activity and influences on business · 1.2 Types of organisations · Lesson 2 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand the main types of business ownership and how risk, liability and control differ between them.

Syllabus Statement

The main types of business ownership: sole trader, partnerships, limited companies (private and public), public corporations. Characteristics relating to size: concepts of risk, ownership and limited liability; public corporations – reasons for and against public ownership; ownership, control, sources of finance, use of profits, stakeholders and shareholders; appropriateness of different forms of ownership.

Success criterion 1

Sole traders, partnerships and limited companies

Businesses in the private sector are owned by individuals or groups of people.

TypeOwned byLiabilityMain financeAdvantages and disadvantages
Sole traderOne personUnlimitedOwner’s savings, bank loansEasy to set up, owner keeps all profit and makes all decisions; but long hours, limited finance, all the risk
Partnership2 to 20 partners (usually)Unlimited (for most partners)Partners’ savings, bank loansMore capital and shared skills; but profits are shared and partners may disagree
Private limited company (Ltd)Shareholders, often family and friendsLimitedSelling shares privately, loansLimited liability and continuity; but shares cannot be sold to the public and accounts must be filed
Public limited company (plc)Shareholders, who can be anyoneLimitedSelling shares on the stock marketCan raise very large sums; but expensive to set up, risk of takeover, owners and managers are separate

Shareholders own a company; they receive part of the profits as dividends. In a large plc the shareholders own the business but directors and managers control it day to day.

Success criterion 2

Risk and limited liability

Unlimited liability means the owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business. If the business cannot pay, the owner may have to sell personal possessions such as their house or car.

Limited liability means the shareholders can only lose the money they invested in the company. Their personal possessions are safe. This makes it easier to persuade people to invest, because the risk is lower.

A limited company is a separate legal entity from its owners: it can own property, sue and be sued, and it continues even if a shareholder dies or sells their shares (continuity).

Activity: limited or unlimited liability?

Decide which type of liability each business owner has.

  • A sole trader running a hair salon
  • A shareholder in a plc such as a supermarket chain
  • A partner in a firm of accountants (ordinary partnership)
  • A family member who owns shares in a private limited company
  • A self-employed plumber
  • An investor who buys shares on the stock market

Success criterion 3

Public corporations and choosing the right form

A public corporation is a business owned and controlled by the government on behalf of the public, in the public sector. Examples include national railways, postal services, water or electricity companies in many countries.

Reasons for public ownershipReasons against public ownership
Provide essential services to everyone, even in unprofitable areasCan be inefficient because there is no profit motive
Protect jobs in key industriesPaid for by taxpayers if losses are made
Control natural monopolies so prices stay fairGovernment may interfere for political reasons

Which form is most appropriate? It depends on the size of the business, how much finance it needs, how much risk the owner will accept and how much control they want to keep. A small café may suit a sole trader; a growing tech start-up needing investors may become a private limited company; a large retailer wanting to raise millions may become a plc.

Key terms

Sole trader
A business owned by one person, who has unlimited liability.
Partnership
A business owned by two or more people who share the profits and decisions.
Limited liability
Owners can only lose the money they invested in the business.
Unlimited liability
Owners are personally responsible for all of the business’s debts.
Public corporation
A business owned and controlled by the government.
Exam-style question

Discuss whether a sole trader running a bakery should become a private limited company. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks for knowledge: e.g. limited liability; shares can be sold to friends and family.
  • Up to 2 marks for analysis: e.g. this reduces the personal risk of the owner, so they can borrow to expand without risking their home.
  • Up to 2 marks for a judgement: e.g. it depends on whether the owner wants to keep full control, as shareholders will share profits and decisions.

Model answer: Becoming a private limited company would give the owner limited liability, so if the bakery failed she would only lose the money invested and not her house. She could also raise more finance by selling shares to family, which could pay for a second shop. However, she would have to share the profits as dividends and may lose some control over decisions, and there are more legal requirements such as publishing accounts. If she plans to grow, becoming a private limited company is sensible, but if she wants to stay small and keep full control she should remain a sole trader.

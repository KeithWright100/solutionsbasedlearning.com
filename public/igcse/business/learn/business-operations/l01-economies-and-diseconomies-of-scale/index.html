1. Economies and diseconomies of scale

Edexcel IGCSE Business · 5 Business operations · 5.1 Economies and diseconomies of scale · Lesson 1 of 7

Learning Intention

To understand internal and external economies of scale and the limits of growth.

Syllabus Statement

Economies of scale: internal (falling average total costs as output increases); external (falling average total costs due to external factors). Diseconomies of scale: limits of growth.

Success criteria

Success criterion 1

Internal economies of scale

Economies of scale are the cost advantages a business gains as it grows: average total cost (cost per unit) falls as output increases. Internal economies come from inside the business:

Average cost per unit = total costs ÷ output

TypeExplanation
Purchasing (bulk buying)Large firms buy raw materials in bulk and receive discounts
TechnicalLarge firms can afford advanced, efficient machinery and spread its cost over more units
FinancialLarge firms find it easier and cheaper to borrow, with lower interest rates
MarketingAdvertising costs are spread over more units; large firms can afford national campaigns
ManagerialLarge firms can employ specialist managers, e.g. in finance and marketing

Example

A bakery’s total costs are $2,000 to make 1,000 loaves (average cost $2.00). After expanding, it makes 4,000 loaves for $6,000 (average cost $1.50), so it can cut prices or earn more profit per loaf.

Success criterion 2

External economies of scale

External economies lower average costs because of factors outside the business, usually because an industry is concentrated in one area:

  • a pool of skilled labour trained in the area;
  • specialist suppliers and services located nearby;
  • better infrastructure such as roads, ports and broadband;
  • local colleges offering relevant training.

Example: technology firms clustered in one city benefit from skilled programmers and specialist suppliers.

Activity: internal or external?

Classify each economy of scale.

  • A supermarket gets a discount for buying 1 million bananas
  • Many car parts suppliers are located near a car factory
  • A large firm borrows at a lower interest rate
  • A local college trains workers for the region’s textile industry
  • A firm spreads the cost of a TV advert over millions of units
  • The government builds a new port near an industrial area

Success criterion 3

Diseconomies of scale and the limits of growth

If a business grows too large, average costs may start to rise. These are diseconomies of scale:

  • Communication problems: longer chains of command, messages are slow or distorted.
  • Coordination and control: it is harder to manage many sites and departments.
  • Motivation: workers feel less valued in a huge organisation, so productivity falls.

Diseconomies set a limit to growth. Businesses try to avoid them by decentralising, improving communication and motivating staff.

Key terms

Economies of scale
Falling average costs per unit as a business increases output.
Internal economies of scale
Cost savings that come from the growth of the business itself.
External economies of scale
Cost savings that come from outside the business, such as the growth of an industry in an area.
Diseconomies of scale
Rising average costs per unit when a business grows too large.
Average cost
Total cost divided by output.
Exam-style question

Explain one internal economy of scale a large supermarket chain might benefit from. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for an economy: e.g. purchasing (bulk buying).
  • 1 mark for development: e.g. it buys huge quantities from suppliers and receives discounts.
  • 1 mark for consequence: e.g. so its average cost per unit falls and it can charge lower prices than small shops.

Model answer: A large supermarket chain benefits from purchasing economies of scale. Because it buys huge quantities from suppliers, it receives large discounts. This lowers its average cost per item, so it can charge lower prices than small shops and still make a profit.

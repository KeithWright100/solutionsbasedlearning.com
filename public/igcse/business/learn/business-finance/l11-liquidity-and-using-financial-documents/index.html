11. Liquidity and using financial documents

Edexcel IGCSE Business · 3 Business finance · 3.5 Accounts analysis · Lesson 11 of 11

Learning Intention

To interpret liquidity and use financial documents to assess performance and make decisions.

Syllabus Statement

Liquidity: the concept and importance of liquidity; comparisons with previous years and/or with other business organisations. The use of financial documents: assess the performance of the business; inform decision making.

Success criterion 1

What is liquidity?

Liquidity is the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts when they are due. A liquid asset can be turned into cash quickly: cash is the most liquid, then receivables, then inventory; buildings are the least liquid.

RatioOften seen as healthyWhat problems look like
Current ratioAbout 1.5 to 2Below 1: may not be able to pay short-term debts. Very high (e.g. 3+): too much money tied up in stock or idle cash
Acid test ratioAbout 1Below 1: relies on selling inventory to pay debts, which may be risky

“Ideal” figures vary between industries. A supermarket sells stock very quickly for cash, so a low acid test may not be a problem.

Success criterion 2

Making comparisons

A single ratio means little on its own. It should be compared:

  • with previous years (is it improving or getting worse?);
  • with other businesses in the same industry (is it better or worse than competitors?).

Analysis: profitability has fallen (GPM 42% → 36%, possibly because supplier costs rose or prices were cut) and is now below the competitor. Liquidity has worsened sharply: an acid test of 0.6 suggests the business may struggle to pay its short-term debts without selling stock.

Activity: improving or worsening?

For each change, decide whether performance has improved or worsened.

  • ROCE rises from 9% to 14%
  • Current ratio falls from 1.6 to 0.9
  • Gross profit margin falls from 40% to 31%
  • Acid test rises from 0.6 to 1.0
  • Operating profit margin rises from 5% to 9%
  • Operating profit margin falls while revenue rises

Success criterion 3

Using financial documents in decisions

Financial documents help managers, owners, lenders and investors to assess performance and make decisions:

  • Low gross profit margin → find cheaper suppliers or raise prices.
  • Low operating profit margin with a steady gross margin → cut expenses, e.g. move to cheaper premises.
  • Poor liquidity → reduce inventory, chase customers who owe money, arrange longer-term finance, delay buying assets.
  • High ROCE → investors may be willing to buy shares; banks more willing to lend.

Limitations: the documents show the past, not the future; they ignore non-financial factors such as staff morale, customer satisfaction and the quality of management; and comparisons with other firms are less useful if they are different sizes or in different markets.

Key terms

Liquidity
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts.
Liquid asset
An asset that can be turned into cash quickly.
Current ratio
Current assets ÷ current liabilities; a measure of liquidity.
Acid test ratio
A stricter liquidity measure that excludes inventory.
Performance
How well a business is doing, judged by measures such as profitability and liquidity.
Exam-style question

A business’s current ratio has fallen from 1.7 to 0.9 in a year. Discuss what this suggests and what the business could do. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks for knowledge: current ratio measures liquidity; below 1 means current liabilities exceed current assets.
  • Up to 2 marks for analysis: the business may be unable to pay suppliers or its overdraft, risking insolvency; causes could include falling cash or rising short-term debts.
  • Up to 2 marks for evaluation: a justified recommendation, e.g. chase receivables, reduce stock, replace an overdraft with a long-term loan; depends on the industry.

Model answer: A fall from 1.7 to 0.9 means current liabilities are now greater than current assets, so the business may not be able to pay its short-term debts such as suppliers and its overdraft. If suppliers stop giving credit, it could become insolvent. This may be because cash has fallen or because it has used short-term borrowing to buy assets. It should chase customers who owe money, reduce inventory, and perhaps replace its overdraft with a long-term loan. However, in some industries, such as supermarkets, a ratio below 1 is normal, so it should also compare with competitors.

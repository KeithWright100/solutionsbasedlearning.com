7. The mixed economy and government intervention

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 2 The allocation of resources · Syllabus 2.10 · Part 5 of 5 in this topic

Success criterion 1

The mixed economic system

A mixed economy combines government-controlled resource allocation (the public sector) with the market system (the private sector). The government provides essential services such as state education, healthcare and postal services, and redistributes income through unemployment benefits and state pensions; private firms provide most other goods for profit. The UK, France, Australia, Sweden and Italy are examples. Almost all real economies are mixed.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Essential services provided for everyone, while most goods are competitively marketed.Disadvantages of the market economy still apply, e.g. higher prices because of the profit motive.
Producers and workers still have incentives to work, invest and save.Public services must be funded by taxes, creating opportunity costs for taxpayers and the government.
A large degree of economic freedom and choice.Government intervention may be inefficient or poorly targeted.
Success criterion 2

Maximum and minimum prices

A maximum price is set by the government below the equilibrium price to make a product more affordable (e.g. rents or basic foods).

  • Advantages: consumers who can buy benefit from lower prices.
  • Disadvantages: a shortage (excess demand): long waiting lists, and unofficial or parallel markets may appear.

A minimum price is set above the equilibrium price to encourage supply or protect producers’ incomes (e.g. guaranteed prices for farmers).

  • Advantages: producers receive a higher, more stable income.
  • Disadvantages: a surplus (excess supply) that the government may have to buy and store; consumers pay more.

Exam tip: A maximum price only has an effect if it is set below equilibrium; a minimum price only if set above equilibrium. Specimen paper question 3(c) asks you to analyse both.

Success criterion 3

Indirect taxes and subsidies

An indirect tax on a demerit good (such as cigarettes) raises producers’ costs, shifting supply to the left by the amount of the tax.

  • Advantages: the higher price should reduce consumption; it raises tax revenue that can fund services.
  • Disadvantages: demand for cigarettes, alcohol and petrol is price inelastic (and nicotine is addictive), so consumption falls only slightly; indirect taxes are regressive, hitting low-income earners hardest.

A subsidy is money from the government to producers that lowers their costs, shifting supply to the right (e.g. subsidised public transport or flu vaccines).

  • Advantages: lower prices encourage consumption of merit goods and goods with external benefits (more bus travel means less congestion and pollution).
  • Disadvantages: an opportunity cost for the government and taxpayers; may need higher taxes or borrowing; firms may become inefficient and rely on subsidies; the subsidy may not be passed on to consumers.

Exam tip: The size of the tax or subsidy is the vertical distance between the two supply curves.

Key terms

Mixed economic system
An economy that combines the public sector and the market (private sector) in allocating resources.
Maximum price
A legal price set below equilibrium to make a product more affordable; causes a shortage.
Minimum price
A legal price set above equilibrium to encourage supply; causes a surplus.
Indirect tax
A tax on spending, paid to the government by producers, which shifts supply left.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a larger share of the income of low-income earners.
Subsidy
A payment from the government to producers that lowers costs and shifts supply right.
Regulation
Rules and laws that control economic activity.
Privatisation
The transfer of assets from the public sector to the private sector.
Nationalisation
The purchase of private sector assets by the government.
Direct provision
The government providing goods and services itself, often free at the point of use.
Quota
A limit on the quantity of a good that can be produced, extracted or traded.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse how the setting of a maximum price and a minimum price can result in market disequilibrium. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for stating (or showing on a diagram) that a maximum price is set below equilibrium and 1 mark for a minimum price set above equilibrium.
  • Maximum price below equilibrium (1): price falls, demand extends (1), supply contracts (1), causing a shortage / excess demand (1).
  • Minimum price above equilibrium (1): price rises, demand contracts (1), supply extends (1), causing a surplus / excess supply (1).
Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not a government should subsidise toothpaste. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why it should: toothpaste may be a merit good; consumers may under-estimate its value, so it is under-consumed; a subsidy increases supply, lowers price and demand extends; less tooth decay; lower healthcare costs.
  • Why it should not: toothpaste is cheap and affordable anyway; rich consumers would benefit too; the subsidy may make producers inefficient; it may not be passed on to consumers; it may lead to over-consumption; opportunity cost of government spending.

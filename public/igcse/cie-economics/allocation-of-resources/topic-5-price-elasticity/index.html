Success criterion 1

Calculating and interpreting PED

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive the quantity demanded of a product is to a change in its price.

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

% change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. PED is negative because of the law of demand, but we usually ignore the minus sign when judging its size.

Worked example: PED for football tickets

Price rises from $50 to $60: % change in price = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = +20%.

Quantity demanded falls from 50,000 to 45,000: % change = (45,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = −10%.

PED = −10 ÷ 20 = −0.5. This is less than 1, so demand is price inelastic.

PED value (ignoring minus sign)DescriptionMeaning
0Perfectly price inelasticQuantity demanded does not change at all when price changes (vertical curve).
Between 0 and 1Price inelastic% change in quantity is smaller than % change in price, e.g. salt, petrol, electricity, cigarettes, rice in Asia.
1Unitary elastic% change in quantity equals % change in price; spending is unchanged.
Greater than 1Price elastic% change in quantity is greater than % change in price, e.g. chocolate bars, soft drinks, airline travel.
∞ (infinity)Perfectly price elasticAny price rise causes quantity demanded to fall to zero (horizontal curve).
Success criterion 2

Determinants of PED

Remember the main influences as THIS, plus a few more:

DeterminantEffect
TimeDemand becomes more elastic over time as people find alternatives (parents with children in fee-paying schools will not move them at once).
Habits, addictions and tastesHabit-forming or fashionable products (tobacco) have inelastic demand.
Income (proportion spent)Products taking a small share of income (salt, toothpicks) are inelastic; expensive items (a $12,000 cruise) are elastic.
SubstitutesThe key determinant: the more close substitutes, the more elastic demand (Pepsi has Coca-Cola as a substitute).
NecessityNecessities (food, fuel, medicine) are inelastic; luxuries (designer handbags) are elastic.
Advertising and brand loyaltyLoyal customers are less sensitive to price.
DurabilityDemand for durable goods (furniture, TVs) is more elastic because replacement can be delayed.
Costs of switchingContracts and incompatible chargers make demand less elastic.
Breadth of definition“Food” is very inelastic; a particular brand of cereal is elastic.

Success criterion 3

PED, spending and revenue, and why PED matters

Total revenue (= consumer spending on the product) = price × quantity sold. It is not the same as profit (revenue − costs).

PEDIf price is raisedIf price is cut
Price elastic (PED > 1)Revenue fallsRevenue rises
Unitary (PED = 1)No changeNo change
Price inelastic (PED < 1)Revenue risesRevenue falls

Worked example: revenue and PED

A retailer sells 5,000 laptops at $700: revenue = $3,500,000.

It cuts the price to $650 (−7.14%) and sales rise to 5,500 (+10%). PED = 10 ÷ −7.14 ≈ −1.4: price elastic.

New revenue = $650 × 5,500 = $3,575,000, an increase of $75,000.

Significance of PED for decision-makers:

  • Firms: raise prices if demand is inelastic and cut them if elastic to increase revenue; use price discrimination (different prices for children, adults and peak or off-peak times).
  • Government: tax products with inelastic demand (alcohol, tobacco, petrol) to raise large tax revenue; but taxes on demerit goods must be very high to cut consumption.
  • Workers: if demand for the product they make is inelastic, they can ask for a bigger pay rise because price increases will hardly reduce sales.
  • Consumers: they spend more when prices of inelastic necessities rise, which reduces spending on other goods.
Success criterion 4

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in price.

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price

PES is positive because of the law of supply.

Worked example: PES for beans

Price rises from $2.00 to $2.20 a kilo: +10%.

Quantity supplied rises from 10,000 to 10,500 kilos: +5%.

PES = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5: supply is price inelastic.

PESMeaning and example
0Perfectly inelastic: supply cannot change (seats in a stadium).
Less than 1Inelastic: e.g. fresh fruit and vegetables that take time to grow.
1Unitary: any straight supply curve through the origin.
Greater than 1Elastic: e.g. mass-produced goods such as soft drinks and toothpaste.
∞Perfectly elastic: supply can rise with no change in price (software downloads).

Determinants of PES:

  • Spare capacity: firms with unused capacity can raise output easily (elastic). Supply is more elastic in a recession.
  • Level of stocks (inventories): firms holding stocks of finished goods can respond quickly; perishable goods are hard to store.
  • Number of producers: more firms in the industry makes supply more elastic; few firms (pharmaceuticals) makes it inelastic.
  • Time period: supply is inelastic in the short run and more elastic in the long run.
  • Ease and cost of factor substitution: if labour and capital can switch easily between products (a printing press), supply is elastic.

Key terms

Price elasticity of demand (PED)
The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price: % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
Price inelastic demand
PED less than 1: quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price.
Price elastic demand
PED greater than 1: quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price.
Unitary elasticity
PED (or PES) equal to 1: quantity changes by the same percentage as price.
Total revenue
Price × quantity sold; equal to consumer spending on the product.
Price discrimination
Charging different customers different prices for essentially the same product because of differences in PED.
Price elasticity of supply (PES)
The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price: % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price.
Spare capacity
Unused resources that allow a firm to increase output quickly.
Stocks (inventories)
Raw materials, components and finished goods held by a firm.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain two causes of an increase in the price elasticity of demand for rice. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for identification and 1 mark for explanation (× 2).
  • Closer substitutes become available (1), e.g. rival products fall in price or improve in quality (1).
  • An increase in income (1) means rice takes up a smaller proportion of income (1).
  • A longer time period (1) gives people time to find alternatives (1).
  • Rice seen less as a necessity (1) as other foods are added to people’s diet (1).
Calculation practice

Sharma Fabrics sells 1,350 units of wool a month at $4.00. When it raises the price to $4.60, sales fall to 1,215 units. Calculate the PED and the change in total revenue. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • % change in price = 0.60 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = +15% (1).
  • % change in quantity = −135 ÷ 1,350 × 100 = −10% (1).
  • PED = −10 ÷ 15 = −0.67 (price inelastic) (1).
  • Revenue rises from $5,400 to $5,589, an increase of $189 (1).

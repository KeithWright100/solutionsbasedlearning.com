6. The market economy and market failure

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 2 The allocation of resources · Syllabus 2.8–2.9 · Part 4 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To evaluate the market economic system and explain why free markets can fail to allocate resources efficiently.

2.8 Market economic system: definition, advantages and disadvantages. 2.9 Market failure: definition; public goods, merit goods, demerit goods, private, external and social costs and benefits, monopoly; causes and consequences of market failure.

Success criterion 1

The market economic system

An economic system is the way an economy is organised to allocate scarce resources. A market economy relies on the market forces of demand and supply in the private sector, with minimal government intervention. Economic agents are driven by incentives such as wages and profits. Singapore, Switzerland and Ireland are close to market economies.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Efficiency: competition makes firms respond to what consumers want and stimulates innovation.Income and wealth inequality: production is geared to the wealthy; basic services for the poor may be neglected.
Freedom of choice: people choose what to buy and which career to follow.Environmental damage: resource depletion, pollution and climate change.
Incentives: the profit motive and chance to earn wealth encourage hard work, raising productivity and growth.Social hardship: public goods such as street lighting and defence may not be provided; poverty relief relies on charities.
Wasteful competition: excess packaging and advertising; products may be less safe.
Success criterion 2

Market failure: private, external and social costs and benefits

Market failure occurs when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, often because production or consumption affects third parties who are not part of the transaction.

TermMeaningExample
Private costsCosts paid by the individual, firm or government taking the decision.A car owner pays for the car, fuel and insurance.
External costsNegative side-effects on third parties, who are not compensated.Congestion and air pollution from driving; noise from a building site; litter.
Social costsPrivate costs + external costs: the full cost to society.
Private benefitsBenefits to the person or firm consuming or producing.Owning a garden you enjoy.
External benefitsPositive side-effects enjoyed by third parties, who do not pay.Neighbours enjoy the garden; bees pollinate; a vaccination protects others.
Social benefitsPrivate benefits + external benefits: the full benefit to society.

Exam tip: In the specimen paper: “External cost is a cost to a third party whereas social cost is the total cost, including external and private costs.”

Success criterion 3

Public goods, merit goods and demerit goods

TypeFeaturesExamplesFree market outcome
Public goodsNon-excludable (people who do not pay cannot be stopped from using them) and non-rivalrous (one person’s use does not reduce another’s).Street lighting, road signs, flood defences, national defence, lighthouses, law and orderNot provided at all by the market, because firms cannot make people pay (free riders).
Merit goodsCreate external benefits; social benefits exceed private benefits.Education, healthcare, vaccinations, training, libraries, museumsUnder-consumed and under-provided if left to the market.
Demerit goodsCreate external costs when consumed.Cigarettes and e-cigarettes, alcohol, junk food, sugary drinks, gamblingOver-consumed and over-produced if left to the market.

Free riders are people who benefit from a good without paying for it, which is why private firms will not supply public goods. Bangladesh’s flood embankments are a public good: once built, they protect everyone in the area.

Success criterion 4

Causes and consequences of market failure

The free market misallocates resources because of:

CauseConsequence
Public goodsNon-provision: essential goods are missing from the market.
Merit goods and goods with external benefitsUnder-consumption and under-production: people under-estimate the benefits (or cannot afford them), so society loses the external benefits.
Demerit goods and goods with external costsOver-consumption and over-production: third parties bear costs such as pollution and extra healthcare.
Abuse of monopoly powerA single firm restricts supply and charges higher prices, with less incentive to be efficient or innovate (for example the 2024 US lawsuit accusing Apple of monopolistic practices).

Example: an oil spill off the Philippines in 2023 harmed 21,000 fishing families, tourism and marine areas: external costs that the polluting firm did not pay for.

Key terms

Market economic system
An economic system that relies on the private sector and market forces to allocate resources, with minimal government intervention.
Market failure
When the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently.
Private costs
Costs paid by the consumer or producer making the decision.
External costs
Costs imposed on third parties who are not compensated.
Social costs
Private costs plus external costs.
Private benefits
Benefits received by the consumer or producer.
External benefits
Benefits received by third parties who do not pay.
Social benefits
Private benefits plus external benefits.
Public good
A good that is non-excludable and non-rivalrous, so it is not provided by the market.
Merit good
A good that creates external benefits and is under-consumed in a free market.
Demerit good
A good that creates external costs and is over-consumed in a free market.
Free rider
Someone who benefits from a good without paying for it.
Monopoly
A market with a single supplier.
Paper 2-style question (a)

Identify the difference between external cost and social cost. [2]

Show mark guidance
  • External cost is a cost to a third party (1) whereas social cost is the total cost / includes external and private costs (1).
  • Or: social cost includes private cost (2).
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse why a free market may fail to provide public goods. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • Public goods are non-excludable (1): people cannot be stopped from benefiting even if they do not pay (1).
  • This creates a free-rider problem (1): people wait for others to pay.
  • Firms cannot charge enough to make a profit (1), so there is no profit motive to supply them (1).
  • Non-rivalrous: one person’s use does not reduce the amount for others (1), so charging is not practical.
  • Result: the good is not provided / a missing market, e.g. street lighting or flood defences (1).

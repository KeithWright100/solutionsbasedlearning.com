3. Markets, demand and supply

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 2 The allocation of resources · Syllabus 2.1–2.3 · Part 1 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To understand how markets bring buyers and sellers together, and what causes movements along and shifts of demand and supply curves.

Syllabus Statement

2.1 The role of markets in allocating resources. 2.2 Demand: individual and market demand, movements along and shifts of a demand curve. 2.3 Supply: individual and market supply, movements along and shifts of a supply curve.

Success criterion 1

Markets, buyers and sellers

A market is any place where buyers and sellers come together to trade, for example shops, supermarkets, restaurants, hotels, online stores and stock markets. Markets allocate scarce resources through the forces of demand (buyers) and supply (sellers).

WhoRole
Buyers (consumers, firms, government)Create demand for goods and services. They tend to buy more when the price falls.
Sellers (producers and suppliers)Supply goods and services. They tend to supply more when the price rises.

Together, buyers and sellers answer the three basic economic questions: consumers’ spending signals what to produce; sellers decide how to produce; and goods go to those willing and able to pay (for whom). The market system (or price mechanism) is this method of allocating resources through demand and supply.

Success criterion 2

Demand and the demand curve

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are both willing and able to buy at various prices over a given period of time. Wanting something without being able to pay is not demand.

The law of demand: there is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded. As price falls, quantity demanded rises, because (1) consumers’ real income rises, so they can afford more with the same income, and (2) more customers are now able to pay. The demand curve therefore slopes downwards from left to right.

Market demand is the sum of all individual demand at each price.

Success criterion 3

Movements along and shifts of the demand curve

A change in the price of the product itself causes a movement along the demand curve:

  • a price rise causes a contraction in demand (quantity demanded falls);
  • a price fall causes an extension in demand (quantity demanded rises).

A change in any non-price factor causes a shift of the whole demand curve: an increase in demand (shift right) or a decrease in demand (shift left). Remember the causes as HIS AGE:

FactorHow it shifts demand
Habits, fashions and tastesFashionable products (the latest smartphone) see demand rise; unfashionable ones fall.
IncomeHigher incomes increase demand for most goods.
Substitutes and complementsA fall in the price of a substitute (Pepsi for Coca-Cola) reduces demand; a rise in the price of a complement (tennis balls for racquets) reduces demand.
AdvertisingPersuasive marketing increases demand.
Government policiesTaxes and regulations reduce demand; subsidies encourage it.
EconomyA boom raises demand; a recession or pandemic reduces it.

Other factors include the weather (ice cream, umbrellas) and the size and age structure of the population.

Exam tip: Do not confuse a change in quantity demanded (movement along the curve, caused by the product’s own price) with a change in demand (shift of the curve, caused by a non-price factor).

Activity: movement or shift of demand?

For the demand for coffee, is each change a movement along or a shift of the demand curve?

  • The price of coffee rises
  • The price of tea (a substitute) falls
  • A health report says coffee is good for you
  • A café cuts the price of coffee
  • Consumers’ incomes rise
  • The price of milk (a complement) rises

Success criterion 4

Supply and the supply curve

Supply is the quantity of goods and services that firms are willing and able to provide at various prices over a given period of time. The law of supply: there is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied, so the supply curve slopes upwards. Higher prices mean existing firms can earn more profit, and new firms can join the market because they can now cover their costs.

Market supply is the sum of all individual firms’ supply at each price (if Airbus supplies 300 aircraft and Boeing 320 at a given price, market supply is 620).

A change in the product’s own price causes a movement along the supply curve (an extension when price rises, a contraction when it falls). A change in a non-price factor shifts the supply curve. Remember TWO TIPS:

FactorHow it shifts supply
TimeIn a short time period, firms (especially farmers) cannot easily increase output.
WeatherGood weather increases agricultural supply (shift right); bad weather reduces it.
Opportunity costIf the price of apples rises, farmers switch land from corn to apples, reducing the supply of corn.
TaxesTaxes on producers add to costs, so supply falls (shift left).
InnovationsNew technology and automation increase supply (shift right).
Production costsHigher costs of raw materials or labour reduce supply; lower costs increase it.
SubsidiesGovernment payments reduce costs, so supply rises (shift right).

Key terms

Market
Any place where buyers and sellers come together to trade.
Demand
The quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices over a period of time.
Law of demand
The inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
Market demand
The sum of all individual demand at each price.
Extension / contraction in demand
A rise / fall in quantity demanded caused by a fall / rise in the product’s price.
Increase / decrease in demand
A shift of the demand curve to the right / left caused by a non-price factor.
Substitutes
Goods that can be used instead of each other, e.g. tea and coffee.
Complements
Goods that are demanded together, e.g. tennis balls and racquets.
Supply
The quantity firms are willing and able to provide at various prices over a period of time.
Law of supply
The positive relationship between price and quantity supplied.
Market supply
The sum of all individual firms’ supply at each price.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain two reasons why the demand for a product may increase. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for each reason identified and 1 mark for explanation (× 2).
  • Higher incomes (1) mean consumers are able to buy more at every price (1).
  • A rise in the price of a substitute (1) makes this product relatively cheaper so consumers switch to it (1).
  • Successful advertising / change in fashion (1) makes consumers more willing to buy (1).
  • A fall in the price of a complement (1).
Paper 2-style question (b)

Draw a demand and supply diagram to show the effect of severe flooding on the market for rice. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • Axes correctly labelled price and quantity (1).
  • Original demand and supply curves correctly labelled (1).
  • Supply curve shifted to the left (1).
  • Original and new equilibrium price and quantity shown, with price rising and quantity falling (1).

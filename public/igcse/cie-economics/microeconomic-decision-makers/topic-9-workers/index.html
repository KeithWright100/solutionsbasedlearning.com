9. Workers: occupations, wages and the division of labour

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 3 Microeconomic decision-makers · Syllabus 3.3 · Part 2 of 5 in this topic

Success criterion 1

Choosing an occupation

Wage factors are the financial rewards for work:

PaymentExplanation
WagesPaid per hour, day or week (e.g. $10 an hour for part-time shop staff).
SalariesA fixed amount paid monthly (e.g. teachers, nurses, accountants).
Piece rate and commissionPaid per item made, or a percentage of sales (estate agents).
Bonuses and profit shareExtra payments linked to performance or profits.
Fringe benefitsNon-wage perks with a money value: pensions, health insurance, company car, free meals.

Non-wage factors also matter: the level of challenge, career prospects, danger (firefighters, miners), length of training, education and experience required, recognition, job security, working hours and conditions, and personal satisfaction (some people do voluntary work for no pay).

  • Long holidays and flexible hours

Success criterion 2

Wage determination

Wages are set by the demand for and supply of labour.

  • The demand for labour is the number of workers firms are willing and able to hire at each wage. It is a derived demand: workers are wanted for the goods and services they produce. It depends on the demand for the product, labour productivity, and the cost of labour compared with machinery.
  • The supply of labour is the number of people of working age willing and able to work at each wage. It depends on the wage, the participation rate, welfare benefits, social attitudes (more women working), migration and training.

Trade unions are organisations that protect workers’ interests, including pay and conditions. Through collective bargaining they negotiate pay rises, especially when the cost of living has risen, profits or productivity have increased, or similar workers have had a rise. They may take industrial action such as strikes. A union’s bargaining power is stronger when it has many members, labour is a small share of the firm’s costs, the firm is very profitable, and workers are hard to replace with machines. It is weaker in a recession or when cheap substitutes for the product or for the workers exist.

Government policy: a national minimum wage (NMW) is the lowest wage firms can legally pay.

Advantages of an NMWDisadvantages of an NMW
Workers receive a fair, liveable wage and are not exploited.If set too high, it may cause unemployment as firms cut demand for labour.
May encourage unemployed people to work rather than rely on benefits.Higher costs for firms; may be passed on as higher prices.
Low-income earners spend more, which may create jobs.Workers above the NMW may demand rises to keep their pay differentials.
Success criterion 3

Why wages differ

ReasonHow it affects wages
Demand and supplyWorkers in high demand and short supply (neurosurgeons) earn more than those in low demand and plentiful supply.
SkillsSkilled workers earn more than unskilled workers because few people have the skills and the training takes a long time.
Economic sectorPrimary sector jobs (farming, fishing) often pay least because much work is unskilled and output has low value; tertiary jobs in finance and IT often pay most. (But a low-skilled supermarket worker earns less than a skilled miner.)
Bargaining strengthStrong trade unions, more experience and more education raise pay; earnings often fall after about age 60.
Discrimination (e.g. male/female)Women earn less on average: more part-time work, career breaks for children, and discrimination. Equal pay laws aim to reduce this.
Private or public sectorPrivate sector pay can be higher (bonuses) but jobs are less secure; public sector jobs often have more security and pensions.
Government policyMinimum wages, equal pay and anti-discrimination laws.

Exam tip: Use supply elasticity to explain pay: the supply of doctors is inelastic (long training), so a rise in demand raises their wage a lot; the supply of waiters is elastic, so a rise in demand raises employment more than the wage.

Success criterion 4

Mobility of labour and the division of labour

Geographical mobility is how willing and able workers are to move to a different place for work. It is reduced by family ties, children’s schooling and differences in the cost of living (housing may be too expensive in the city).

Occupational mobility is how easily workers can change to a different job. It depends on the cost and length of retraining. Highly skilled workers often have more occupational mobility.

Causes of changes in mobility: government retraining schemes (US coal miners retraining for renewable energy), better transport links, cheaper housing, technology allowing home working, and immigration rules (such as Brexit). Consequences: more mobile labour reduces unemployment, fills skill shortages and raises productivity and growth; immobility leaves regions with high unemployment.

Division of labour means breaking production into separate tasks, with each worker concentrating on a particular task (checkout operators, factory machine operators).

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Workers become experts, so productivity and efficiency rise.Work can become repetitive and boring, reducing motivation.
Quality of the product may improve.Workers may become alienated, especially in low-skilled jobs.
Skilled workers may earn more.Production depends on each worker: if one stops, the whole process stops.
Lower average costs and higher output for firms.Workers lose other skills, so they are less flexible and less occupationally mobile.

Key terms

Wage factors
Financial rewards for work, such as wages, salaries, bonuses and commission.
Non-wage factors
Non-financial influences on job choice, such as job security, training and satisfaction.
Fringe benefits
Perks with a monetary value, such as health insurance or a company car.
Demand for labour
The number of workers firms are willing and able to employ at each wage rate.
Derived demand
Demand for a factor of production that comes from demand for the product it makes.
Supply of labour
The number of people of working age willing and able to work at each wage rate.
Trade union
An organisation that protects the interests of its worker members, including pay and conditions.
National minimum wage (NMW)
The lowest wage firms are legally allowed to pay.
Geographical mobility
The willingness and ability of workers to move location for work.
Occupational mobility
The ease with which workers can change from one job to another.
Division of labour
Breaking production into separate tasks, with each worker concentrating on a particular task.

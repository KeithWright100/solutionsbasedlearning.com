10. Firms, mergers and economies of scale

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 3 Microeconomic decision-makers · Syllabus 3.4 · Part 3 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To classify firms, evaluate small and large firms and different types of merger, and explain how economies and diseconomies of scale affect costs.

Syllabus Statement

3.4 Firms: primary, secondary and tertiary sector firms; private and public sector firms; advantages and disadvantages of small and large firms; horizontal, vertical and conglomerate mergers; internal and external economies and diseconomies of scale, with average total cost diagrams.

Success criterion 1

Classifying firms

SectorWhat firms doExamples
Primary sectorExtracts raw materials from the earthFarming, fishing, mining, forestry, oil extraction
Secondary sectorManufactures goods and constructs buildingsCar factories, clothing makers, construction firms
Tertiary sectorProvides servicesShops, banks, schools, hairdressers, transport, insurance

The sectors are interdependent: a children’s clothes shop needs cotton (primary), machinists to make the clothes (secondary) and banking, transport and advertising (tertiary). This is the chain of production.

Private sector firms are owned by individuals and shareholders and usually aim for profit: sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies and public limited companies. Public sector organisations are owned by the government, funded by taxes and aim to provide a service (water supply, postal services, state broadcasters).

Firm size can be measured by the number of employees, market share, market capitalisation (share price × number of shares) or sales revenue.

Success criterion 2

Small and large firms

Advantages of small firmsDisadvantages of small firms
Easy and cheap to set up with few legal formalitiesLimited finance: hard to raise money to start up or expand
Easier to manage and control; avoid diseconomies of scaleHighest risk of business failure; intense competition
The owner keeps all the profit, which is a strong incentiveSuccess depends on the owner’s skills; heavy workload
Personal service; can adapt quickly and offer unique or tailored productsLack of continuity if the owner is ill or away
May serve remote or niche marketsCannot benefit from economies of scale, so unit costs and prices are higher

Many advantages of small firms are disadvantages of large firms, and vice versa. Large firms benefit from economies of scale, more finance, and wider choice (a supermarket offers one-stop shopping), but may suffer diseconomies of scale and give a less personal service.

Firms grow internally (organically, by selling more) or externally through mergers and takeovers. A merger is when two or more firms join to form one firm; a takeover is when one firm buys a controlling share of another, which may be hostile.

Success criterion 3

Types of merger

TypeMeaning and exampleAdvantagesDisadvantages
HorizontalFirms at the same stage of production in the same industry (Kraft and Heinz, 2015)Higher market share; fewer duplicated staff; economies of scaleJob losses; diseconomies of scale; clash of cultures; less competition for consumers
Vertical (backward)Firm merges with a supplier at an earlier stage (a nugget factory buying a chicken farm)Control over the quality and cost of suppliesExtra costs of running a different type of business
Vertical (forward)Firm merges with a business at a later stage, nearer the customer (Apple and IKEA own their shops)Control over how products are sold; retailer’s profit is keptNeeds new expertise; higher running costs
ConglomerateFirms in unrelated businesses (the Tata Group: hotels, steel, cars)Diversification spreads riskMay become too diverse and hard to manage

Success criterion 4

Economies and diseconomies of scale

Economies of scale are the cost advantages of large-scale production that reduce average total cost (ATC) as output rises.

Internal economy of scaleExplanation
Bulk-buying (purchasing)Discounts for buying raw materials in large quantities.
TechnicalLarge firms can afford advanced machinery and mass production.
FinancialBanks lend more cheaply to large, secure firms.
MarketingAdvertising costs are spread over more units; can afford national campaigns.
ManagerialCan employ specialist managers, raising efficiency.
Risk-bearingSelling many products in many markets spreads risk.

External economies of scale arise from the firm’s location and the growth of the whole industry: nearby suppliers (textile firms in Istanbul), a pool of skilled labour (ICT in Bangalore), the area’s reputation (Silicon Valley) and good transport networks.

Diseconomies of scale occur when a firm becomes too large and ATC rises: poor communication and slow decisions, difficulties of control and coordination, low staff morale and motivation, and clashes of culture after a merger. Some firms demerge as a result.

Key terms

Primary sector
Firms that extract raw materials, such as farming, fishing and mining.
Secondary sector
Firms that manufacture goods and construct buildings.
Tertiary sector
Firms that provide services.
Private sector
Firms owned by private individuals and shareholders, usually aiming for profit.
Public sector
Organisations owned by the government, usually aiming to provide a service.
Merger
When two or more firms join together to form one firm.
Horizontal merger
A merger of firms at the same stage of production in the same industry.
Vertical merger
A merger of firms at different stages of production in the same industry.
Conglomerate merger
A merger of firms in unrelated businesses.
Economies of scale
Cost advantages of large-scale production that reduce average total cost.
External economies of scale
Cost advantages from the location or growth of the whole industry.
Diseconomies of scale
Rising average total cost when a firm becomes too large.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain two internal economies of scale a large firm may benefit from. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for each economy of scale and 1 mark for explanation (× 2).
  • Bulk-buying (1): discounts for buying in large quantities reduce average costs (1).
  • Technical (1): can afford advanced machinery / mass production (1).
  • Financial (1): can borrow more cheaply (1).
  • Marketing (1): advertising costs spread over more units (1).
  • Managerial / risk-bearing (1) with explanation (1).
Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not consumers would benefit from more firms competing in the electric car market. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why consumers would benefit: better quality; lower prices as consumers can switch between producers; more choice and variety; cars to meet different needs; fewer petrol cars so less pollution; greater consumer power; less risk of diseconomies of scale.
  • Why they would not: smaller firms may not gain economies of scale, especially technical economies which are significant in car production; average costs and prices may be higher; fewer, larger firms may have more funds to invest in improving quality.

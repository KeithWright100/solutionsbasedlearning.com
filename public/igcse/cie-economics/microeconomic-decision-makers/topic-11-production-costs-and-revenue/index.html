11. Production, productivity, costs and revenue

Success criterion 1

The demand for factors of production

The demand for any factor of production is a derived demand: it depends on the demand for the product it makes (university economics lecturers are hired only if students want economics courses). In a recession, firms demand less labour. Demand for factors also depends on:

InfluenceExplanation
Demand for the productMore demand for the product means more demand for the factors that make it.
Price (cost) of the factorThe dearer a factor, the less it is demanded; if labour becomes expensive relative to capital, firms switch to machines. Higher interest rates make capital dearer to finance.
AvailabilityPlentiful factors are cheaper (the large workforces of India and China attract multinationals).
ProductivityMore productive (better-quality) factors are in higher demand, e.g. highly skilled surgeons and pilots; fertile land for rice.
Success criterion 2

Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

Labour-intensive production uses a high proportion of labour relative to other factors (teaching, hairdressing, tailoring, traditional farming, professional football). Capital-intensive production uses a high proportion of capital, i.e. machinery and technology (car manufacturing, soft drinks bottling, oil extraction, aircraft making).

Labour-intensiveCapital-intensive
Highly customised, personalised products and services, so higher prices can be chargedMass production: large output at low unit cost
Quality from skilled, experienced workersMachines are accurate, reducing human error
Flexible: workers can be hired for busy seasonsTechnical economies of scale in the long run
Disadvantage: expensive skilled labour means high costs and pricesDisadvantages: huge set-up, running and replacement costs; breakdowns stop production; high cost is a barrier to entry

The choice depends on the relative cost of labour and capital, the size of the market (mass markets suit capital-intensive methods) and the firm’s objectives (some firms protect jobs or offer a personal service).

Exam tip: Whether a firm is labour-intensive depends on the proportion of its costs spent on labour, not the amount. A firm spending $30,000 on labour and $15,000 on capital is more labour-intensive than one spending $60,000 on each.

Success criterion 3

Production and productivity

Production is the total output of goods and services. Productivity measures efficiency: output per unit of input, such as output per worker (labour productivity) or output per machine hour.

Worked example: labour productivity

Sharma Realty sells $3,950,000 of property with 8 sales staff: $3,950,000 ÷ 8 = $493,750 per worker.

Urvashi Realty sells $3,800,000 with 10 staff: $380,000 per worker.

Sharma Realty has higher sales per worker, but Urvashi sold more units (14 against 10), so the measure chosen matters.

Influence on productivityExplanation
InvestmentSpending on new machinery and technology helps workers produce more; higher interest rates discourage it.
InnovationNew ideas and products, e.g. AI and remote-working technology, raise output per worker.
Skills and experienceEducation and training improve the quality of labour (human capital).
Entrepreneurial spiritMotivated entrepreneurs organise resources well and exploit new opportunities.
CompetitionRivalry forces firms to become more efficient.

Effects of more investment on productivity: new capital equipment usually raises output per worker and per machine. A firm that buys better machines, trains some workers and makes others redundant will see both capital and labour productivity rise. Higher productivity lowers unit costs, raises profits and wages, improves competitiveness and contributes to economic growth.

Success criterion 4

Costs and revenue

TermMeaningFormula
Fixed costs (FC)Do not change with output; paid even if nothing is produced: rent, insurance, managers’ salaries, loan repayments.
Variable costs (VC)Change with output: raw materials, components, production workers’ wages.
Total cost (TC)All costs of production.TC = FC + VC
Average fixed cost (AFC)Fixed cost per unit; falls continuously as output rises.AFC = FC ÷ output
Average variable cost (AVC)Variable cost per unit.AVC = VC ÷ output
Average total cost (ATC)Cost per unit (unit cost).ATC = TC ÷ output (or AFC + AVC)
Total revenue (TR)Money received from sales.TR = price × quantity sold
Average revenue (AR)Revenue per unit sold (the average price).AR = TR ÷ quantity sold

Worked example: Bernice’s Beach Mats

Fixed costs = $4,000 a month; average variable cost = $7; output = 1,000 mats; price = $15.

VC = 1,000 × $7 = $7,000. TC = $4,000 + $7,000 = $11,000.

ATC = $11,000 ÷ 1,000 = $11. AFC = $4,000 ÷ 1,000 = $4.

TR = $15 × 1,000 = $15,000; AR = $15. Profit = TR − TC = $4,000.

Exam tip: Firms pay costs; customers pay the price. Do not mix up the two words. Revenue is not profit: profit = total revenue − total cost.

Key terms

Derived demand
Demand for a factor of production that comes from demand for the product it makes.
Labour-intensive production
Production using a high proportion of labour relative to other factors.
Capital-intensive production
Production using a high proportion of capital relative to other factors.
Production
The total output of goods and services.
Productivity
Output per unit of input, e.g. output per worker.
Fixed costs
Costs that do not change with output.
Variable costs
Costs that change with the level of output.
Total cost
Fixed costs plus variable costs.
Average total cost
Total cost divided by output; the cost per unit.
Average fixed cost
Fixed cost divided by output.
Average variable cost
Variable cost divided by output.
Total revenue
Price multiplied by quantity sold.
Average revenue
Total revenue divided by quantity sold.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Section A, part (c) style.

Explain one reason why productivity may increase in a country’s tertiary sector. [2]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for identification and 1 mark for explanation.
  • Improvements in education (1) increase workers’ skills (1).
  • Advances in technology (1) give workers better capital equipment (1).
  • A rise in wages (1) may increase workers’ motivation (1).
Calculation practice

A firm has fixed costs of $2,000 and variable costs of $3 per unit. It produces 500 units and sells them at $10 each. Calculate its total cost, average total cost, total revenue and profit. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • TC = $2,000 + (500 × $3) = $3,500 (1).
  • ATC = $3,500 ÷ 500 = $7 (1).
  • TR = 500 × $10 = $5,000 (1).
  • Profit = $5,000 − $3,500 = $1,500 (1).

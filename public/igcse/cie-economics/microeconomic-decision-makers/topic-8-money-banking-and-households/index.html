8. Money, banking and households

Success criterion 1

Money: forms, functions and characteristics

Money is anything that is widely accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services. Today it takes three main forms: cash (banknotes and coins, called legal tender), bank deposits (most of the money supply, held electronically and spent with cards and transfers) and central bank reserves (used by banks to make payments to each other).

Without money, people must barter (swap goods). Barter needs a double coincidence of wants (each person must want what the other has), and goods such as sheep are hard to divide and carry.

CharacteristicMeaning
DurabilityLasts a long time (polymer banknotes last longer than paper).
AcceptabilityWidely recognised and accepted as payment (the Zimbabwean dollar stopped being accepted in 2009 during hyperinflation).
DivisibilityCan be split into small units (a third of a goat is not useful!).
UniformityEach note or coin of the same value looks identical.
ScarcityLimited in supply so it keeps its value; the central bank controls the supply.
PortabilityEasy to carry: a banknote weighs about 1 gram.

Exam tip: Do not confuse functions (what money does: medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, standard of deferred payment) with characteristics (what makes something good money: durable, acceptable, divisible, uniform, scarce, portable).

Success criterion 2

Central banks and commercial banks

A central bank is the monetary authority that manages a country’s money supply and banking system, for example the Federal Reserve (USA), the European Central Bank, the People’s Bank of China and the Bank of Thailand. It is responsible for monetary policy, including setting interest rates (Topic 4).

Function of a central bankExplanation
Sole issuer of banknotes and coinsOnly it can print and mint legal tender, which builds confidence in the currency.
The government’s bankHolds government accounts, lends to government, manages public debt and can intervene in foreign exchange markets.
The bankers’ bankCommercial banks hold accounts with it, so payments between banks are settled through it.
Lender of last resortLends to commercial banks in financial difficulty, protecting savers and the banking system (e.g. bank bailouts after 2008).

A commercial bank is a retail bank that provides financial services to households and firms (e.g. HSBC, ICBC, Bangkok Bank). Its primary functions are:

  • Accepting deposits: sight deposits (withdraw on demand) and time deposits (fixed period, higher interest).
  • Making advances: loans, overdrafts and mortgages (long-term loans secured on property).
  • Credit creation: lending increases the money supply in the economy.

Secondary functions include money transfers, foreign exchange, credit cards, safety deposit boxes and online banking. Commercial banks matter because they keep savings safe, pay interest on them, and provide the finance households and firms need to spend and invest.

Success criterion 3

Influences on spending, saving and borrowing: income, interest and confidence

Disposable income is income after compulsory deductions such as income tax. It is the single biggest influence on household behaviour: higher disposable income usually means more spending and more saving. Higher income tax reduces disposable income.

Income groupSpendingSavingBorrowing
Low incomeSpend most of their income, mainly on necessities (food, clothing, housing)Little or nothing left to saveMay borrow for capital items; banks see them as higher risk
Middle incomeSpend a lower proportion on necessities, some on luxuriesCan save some incomeBorrow for cars, furniture and mortgages
High incomeSpend the smallest proportion of income on necessities; buy luxuriesSave a greater proportion of incomeBorrow with little risk; less need to borrow

Wealth (assets minus liabilities) also matters: rising property and share prices create a positive wealth effect, so people spend more.

Confidence: when consumers and firms are confident about the future (a boom), they spend and borrow more and save less. In a recession, people fear losing their jobs, so they save more and spend less.

Other influences on borrowing: availability of credit (credit cards, store cards, how much banks will lend) and wealth (banks lend more readily to people with assets to offer as security).

Success criterion 4

Influences on spending, saving and borrowing: age and culture

Age: spending and saving change over a person’s life.

  • Young people often earn low incomes and spend most of it; many borrow (for example student loans).
  • Middle-aged people usually earn the most, so they can save the largest proportion of their income (for a home, children’s education and retirement).
  • Retired people have no earned income, so they dissave: they spend their savings.

Culture and attitudes: in the USA and UK, many people borrow with credit cards and loans; in Japan, Germany and China people tend to be more cautious and save for a “rainy day”. China’s gross savings were around 46% of GDP in 2022, helped by rising incomes, smaller families and a preference for paying in cash rather than borrowing. The size of households also matters: a family with three children spends more than a single person.

Reason for savingExplanation
PrecautionMoney set aside for emergencies such as job loss or illness.
Future spendingSaving for a holiday, a home, children’s education or retirement.
Earning interestBanks pay interest and keep money secure.

Key terms

Money
Anything widely accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services.
Barter
Swapping goods or services directly without using money.
Double coincidence of wants
The need, under barter, for each trader to want what the other has.
Central bank
The monetary authority that manages a country’s money supply and banking system.
Lender of last resort
The central bank’s role of lending to banks in financial difficulty.
Commercial bank
A retail bank providing deposits, loans and other financial services to households and firms.
Mortgage
A long-term loan secured on property.
Disposable income
Income after compulsory deductions such as income tax.
Saving
Putting aside part of current income for future spending.
Borrowing
Taking out a loan that is repaid over time with interest.
Dissaving
Spending past savings, for example by retired people.

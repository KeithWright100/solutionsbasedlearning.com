19. Population and differences in economic development

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 5 Economic development · Syllabus 5.3–5.4 · Part 2 of 2 in this topic

Learning Intention

To explain what affects population growth, the effects of changes in population size and structure, and why economic development differs between countries.

Syllabus Statement

5.3 Population: birth rate, death rate, net migration, immigration and emigration; reasons for differences between countries; the optimum population; effects of changes in population size and in age and gender distribution. 5.4 Differences in economic development between countries: income, productivity, population growth, size of sectors, saving and investment, education, healthcare and natural resources.

Success criterion 1

What affects population growth

TermMeaningNotes
Birth rateLive births per 1,000 of the population per year.High in low-income countries (Niger 46.6); low in high-income countries (Japan, South Korea about 7).
Death rateDeaths per 1,000 of the population per year.Depends on healthcare, nutrition, sanitation and housing; also the age structure.
ImmigrationPeople entering a country to live.
EmigrationPeople leaving a country to live abroad.
Net migrationImmigration − emigration.Positive = net immigration; negative = net emigration.

Why rates differ between countries:

  • Birth rates are lower in high-income countries because of education, access to contraception, the high cost of raising children, and women choosing careers and having children later. They are higher in low-income countries where children may work or support parents in old age.
  • Death rates are lower where there is better healthcare, nutrition, sanitation and housing; famine, disease and poverty raise them.
  • Net migration depends on job opportunities and wages, taxes, and conflict or instability (many Filipinos and Indonesians work in Hong Kong and Singapore for higher pay).

The fertility rate is the average number of children per woman; about 2 is needed to keep the population stable (the replacement rate). South Korea’s is about 0.9.

Success criterion 2

The optimum population

A country is under-populated if it has too few workers to make the best use of its resources: more people would raise GDP per head (it may encourage immigration). It is over-populated if there are too many people for its resources, causing shortages of housing, food and energy, which lowers GDP per head. The optimum can change: new technology or more investment raises the population a country can support.

Malthus argued that population grows faster than food production, so living standards would fall. This has not happened, because population growth slowed in richer countries and food technology (machinery, irrigation, fertilisers) raised output rapidly.

Success criterion 3

Effects of changes in population size and structure

Age distribution: low-income countries often have a young population (about 56% of Ghanaians are under 25); high-income countries often have an ageing population (about 30% of Japanese people are 65 or over). The dependency ratio compares the dependent population (under 15, over 65) with the working population; a high ratio means a bigger burden on workers and taxpayers.

Gender distribution: most countries have roughly equal numbers of males and females; China’s one-child policy led to more males than females, which can cause labour and social problems.

Effect onIncrease in population / ageing populationConsequences and responses
ConsumersDemand changes: an ageing population buys more healthcare; young families buy housing, education and toys.Firms adapt products; some markets grow and others shrink.
Firms and workersRapid growth increases the future labour supply; an ageing or shrinking population means labour shortages.Higher wages; more automation; reliance on immigration.
GovernmentA larger workforce pays more tax, but more people need services. An ageing population needs more pensions and healthcare with fewer taxpayers.Raising the retirement age (France to 64); compulsory pension saving; higher taxes.
The economy and environmentMore pressure on scarce resources; possible inflation and more imports; pollution and congestion.Larger markets and more workers can also boost growth.

Success criterion 4

Differences in economic development between countries

Economic development is an improvement in living standards that includes both quantitative (income) and qualitative factors (less poverty and inequality, more freedom, gender equality and self-esteem).

FactorHow it causes differences in development
IncomeHigher real GDP per head usually means higher living standards, though the composition and distribution of GDP matter.
ProductivityAccess to technology, better resources and foreign direct investment raise output per worker.
Population growthRapid growth reduces GDP per head and strains resources (South Sudan, Niger).
Size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectorsLow-income countries rely on low-value primary output; as countries develop, employment shifts to manufacturing and then services.
Saving and investmentPoor households cannot save, so there are few funds for banks to lend for investment.
EducationMore years of schooling and higher literacy raise skills and productivity.
HealthcareHigher life expectancy and lower child mortality mean a healthier, more productive workforce.
Natural resourcesResources such as oil and minerals can bring export earnings, but over-dependence can cause unequal development and environmental damage.

Key terms

Birth rate
The number of live births per 1,000 of the population per year.
Death rate
The number of deaths per 1,000 of the population per year.
Net migration
Immigration minus emigration.
Immigration
People entering a country to live and work.
Emigration
People leaving a country to live and work abroad.
Fertility rate
The average number of children born per woman.
Optimum population
The population size at which output per head is maximised.
Ageing population
A rising proportion of older people in the population.
Dependency ratio
The dependent population (young and old) compared with the working population.
Economic development
An improvement in living standards, including both income and quality of life.
Paper 2-style question (e)

Section A, part (e) style: use data from a table.

A table shows life expectancy and net migration rates. Monaco: 87 years, +11.0; Australia: 84, +7.1; UK: 82, +3.2; Algeria: 78, −0.8; Tonga: 72, −10.9; Fiji: 68, −6.0. Analyse the relationship between life expectancy and the net migration rate. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • Expected relationship: a positive / direct relationship; high life expectancy goes with net immigration (1).
  • Supporting evidence: e.g. Monaco has the longest life expectancy and highest net immigration (1); the three countries over 80 years all have net immigration (1).
  • Analysis: long life expectancy suggests good healthcare / high living standards (1), which may attract migrants (1).
  • Exception: Fiji has a lower life expectancy than Tonga but less net emigration (1); other influences on migration, e.g. immigration controls (1).
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse the effects of an ageing population on a government’s budget. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • More older people need state pensions (1) and healthcare (1), increasing government spending (1).
  • A smaller working population (1) means less income tax revenue (1).
  • The budget deficit may increase / the government may need to raise taxes, borrow or raise the retirement age (1).
  • Older people may spend less, reducing indirect tax revenue (1).

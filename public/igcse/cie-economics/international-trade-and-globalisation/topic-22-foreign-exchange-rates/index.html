22. Foreign exchange rates

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 6 International trade and globalisation · Syllabus 6.3 · Part 3 of 4 in this topic

Learning Intention

To explain how floating exchange rates are determined, why they change, and the consequences of changes for prices, exports, imports and the economy.

Syllabus Statement

6.3 Foreign exchange rates: definition; reasons for buying and selling foreign currencies; floating exchange rate, appreciation and depreciation; demand for and supply of a currency and the equilibrium rate; causes of fluctuations (exports and imports, interest rates, speculation); consequences for prices and demand for exports and imports.

Success criterion 1

Exchange rates and calculations

A foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, e.g. MYR1 = PKR60.

Worked example: prices in another currency

A good made in the USA costs $10. The exchange rate is $1 = 20 South African rand (ZAR), so it costs ZAR200 in South Africa.

The rate changes to $1 = 19 ZAR. The good now costs 10 × 19 = ZAR190: the price falls by ZAR10 (specimen paper question 39).

Australian textbooks cost AUD65. If AUD1 = CNY6.5, they cost 65 × 6.5 = CNY422.50 in China.

Exam tip: To convert, multiply by the rate when going from the currency on the left of the “=” to the one on the right; divide when going the other way. Always check which currency the question asks for.

Success criterion 3

Floating exchange rates: determination and causes of change

In a floating exchange rate system, the rate is set by the market forces of demand for and supply of the currency. Appreciation is a rise in the value of a currency; depreciation is a fall.

ChangeEffect on demand or supplyEffect on the currency
Demand for exports risesMore demand for the currencyAppreciates
Demand for imports risesMore supply of the currency (to buy foreign currency)Depreciates
Interest rates riseForeign savers buy the currency for better returnsAppreciates
Domestic inflation is highExports less competitive, so less demand for the currencyDepreciates
Inward investment by MNCs (FDI)More demand for the currencyAppreciates
Speculators expect a riseThey buy the currencyAppreciates
Central bank sells its own currencyMore supplyDepreciates
Success criterion 4

Consequences of exchange rate changes

Remember SPICED: a Strong Pound (currency) means Imports are Cheaper and Exports are Dearer. A depreciation has the opposite effects.

Effect onAppreciationDepreciation
Export prices abroadRise, so demand for exports may fallFall, so demand for exports may rise
Import prices at homeFall, so imports become more attractiveRise, so imports become less attractive
Current accountMay worsenMay improve
Employment and growthExporters may cut jobs; growth may slowExport industries expand; growth and jobs may rise
InflationLower: cheaper imported goods and raw materialsHigher: dearer imports (imported inflation)

The size of the effects depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports: a depreciation reduces spending on imports only if demand for them is price elastic. Essential imports such as oil have inelastic demand, so a weaker currency raises import costs. Governments may want a lower exchange rate to boost exports, improve the current account, raise growth and reduce unemployment.

Activity: appreciate or depreciate?

Would each event make a country’s currency appreciate or depreciate?

  • Interest rates in the country rise
  • Its citizens buy far more imports
  • Foreign demand for its exports rises
  • It has much higher inflation than its trading partners
  • A foreign MNC builds a large factory there
  • Speculators expect the currency to fall and sell it

Key terms

Foreign exchange rate
The price of one currency in terms of another.
Floating exchange rate
An exchange rate determined by the demand for and supply of the currency.
Appreciation
A rise in the value of a currency in a floating system.
Depreciation
A fall in the value of a currency in a floating system.
Speculation
Buying and selling currencies to profit from changes in their value.
Remittances
Money sent home by people working abroad.
Imported inflation
Inflation caused by higher prices of imports, e.g. after a depreciation.
Paper 2-style question (a)

Identify two reasons why a government may want a lower foreign exchange rate. [2]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for each reason (up to 2).
  • To improve the current account / increase exports.
  • To increase economic growth.
  • To increase employment / lower unemployment.
  • A lack of foreign exchange reserves to maintain the current value.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse the likely effects of a currency appreciation on a country’s exports and imports. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • Appreciation makes exports more expensive in foreign currencies (1), so demand for exports may fall (1).
  • Imports become cheaper in the domestic currency (1), so demand for imports may rise (1).
  • The current account may worsen (1).
  • The effect depends on price elasticity of demand: if demand for exports is inelastic, export revenue may not fall much (1).

21. Trade restrictions (protection)

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 6 International trade and globalisation · Syllabus 6.2.4–6.2.6 · Part 2 of 4 in this topic

Learning Intention

To explain the methods of protection, the reasons governments restrict trade, and the consequences of trade restrictions for the home country and its trading partners.

Syllabus Statement

6.2.4 Types of trade restrictions: tariffs, import quotas, subsidies, embargoes. 6.2.5 Reasons for trade restrictions: infant (sunrise), declining (sunset) and strategic industries, dumping, current account deficit, tax revenue, demerit goods, environmental sustainability. 6.2.6 Consequences of trade restrictions for the home country and its trading partners; advantages and disadvantages.

Success criterion 1

Methods of protection

Protection (trade restriction) is the use of barriers to limit international trade and foreign competition.

MethodExplanation
Tariff (customs duty)A tax on imports (e.g. a 25% US tariff on tyres from China). Raises the price of imports.
Import quotaA limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported (e.g. Indonesian quotas on Thai fruit and vegetables).
SubsidyPayments to domestic producers that lower their costs so they can compete with imports (e.g. EU farm subsidies).
EmbargoA complete ban on trade with a particular country, often for political reasons (e.g. the US embargo on Cuba).
Rules and regulationsStrict safety, quality or environmental standards and paperwork that raise costs for foreign firms.
Success criterion 2

Reasons for trade restrictions

ReasonExplanation
Protect infant (sunrise) industriesNew industries need time to grow before they can compete (China allows only a few Hollywood films a year).
Protect declining (sunset) industriesGives time to restructure and avoids sudden mass unemployment (US steel tariffs, 2018).
Protect strategic industriesAvoid dependence on other countries for essentials such as food, energy and defence.
Avoid dumpingStop foreign firms selling goods below cost to drive out domestic producers.
Reduce a current account deficitFewer imports improve the balance of trade.
Raise tax revenueTariffs raise money for the government (India’s tariffs on gold).
Restrict demerit goodsHigh tariffs on tobacco and alcohol protect public health.
Promote environmental sustainabilityTaxes on carbon-intensive imports (the EU carbon border adjustment); restrictions on logging.

Success criterion 3

Consequences of trade restrictions

WhoPossible benefitsPossible costs
Home countryProtected industries keep jobs; more tax revenue; may improve the current account.Consumers pay higher prices and have less choice; protected firms may become inefficient; firms pay more for imported raw materials (cost-push inflation).
Trading partnersNone directly.Lower exports, so lower output and jobs; they may retaliate with their own tariffs, leading to a trade war (US–China tariffs, 2024).

Overall: restrictions distort market signals and can lead to a global misallocation of resources. Long trade disputes, such as the 20-year EU–Latin America “Banana Wars”, harm both sides. Free trade organisations such as the World Trade Organization (WTO) aim to reduce barriers.

Exam tip: A tariff raises the price of imports for consumers. It does not protect consumers from high prices charged by domestic monopolies; that is not a reason for trade restrictions (specimen paper question 38).

Advantages of restricting free tradeDisadvantages of restricting free trade

Key terms

Protection
Using trade barriers to restrict international trade and foreign competition.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods.
Import quota
A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported.
Embargo
A ban on trade with a particular country.
Subsidy
A payment to domestic producers that lowers their costs.
Infant (sunrise) industry
A new industry that may need protection while it grows.
Declining (sunset) industry
An industry in long-term decline.
Strategic industry
An industry important for national security, such as energy, food or defence.
Dumping
Selling exports abroad at a price below the cost of production.
Retaliation
When a trading partner responds to restrictions with its own barriers.

Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not a government should protect its infant industries. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why it should: new firms cannot yet compete with established foreign rivals that have economies of scale; protection lets them grow, gain economies of scale and become competitive; creates jobs; diversifies the economy; may become future exporters.
  • Why it should not: higher prices and less choice for consumers; protected firms may never become efficient; difficult to remove protection later; retaliation from trading partners; opportunity cost of subsidies.

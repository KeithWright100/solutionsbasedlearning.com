23. The current account of the balance of payments

Success criterion 1

The structure of the current account

The balance of payments records a country’s transactions with the rest of the world. Its largest part is the current account.

PartWhat it records
Trade in goods (visible balance)Exports and imports of physical goods, e.g. Japanese cars sold abroad (export) or German cars bought by Japanese residents (import).
Trade in services (invisible balance)Exports and imports of services: tourism, banking, insurance, shipping. Foreign tourists spending in France are an export for France.
Primary incomeIncome from investments abroad: profits of overseas subsidiaries, interest and dividends.
Secondary incomeTransfers: workers’ remittances, foreign aid, donations to charities, pensions paid abroad.

Worked example: current account balances

Exports of goods $57bn, services $28bn; imports of goods $88bn, services $15bn.

Visible balance = 57 − 88 = −$31bn; invisible balance = 28 − 15 = +$13bn.

Balance of trade = −31 + 13 = −$18bn (a deficit).

If exports are $120m (50% goods) and imports $100m (60% goods), exports of goods = $60m (specimen paper question 40).

Success criterion 2

Causes of deficits and surpluses

A current account deficit is when more money flows out than in (e.g. imports exceed exports). A current account surplus is when more flows in than out.

Deficit causeExplanationSurplus causeExplanation
Lower demand for exportsHigher costs and less competitive industries; recession in trading partners; a higher exchange rate.Higher demand for exportsHigher productivity and competitiveness; growth in trading partners; a lower exchange rate.
Higher demand for importsCheaper or better-quality foreign goods; a higher exchange rate; domestic inflation; rising domestic incomes.Lower demand for importsDearer imports (a lower exchange rate); inflation abroad; better domestic products.
Success criterion 3

Consequences of deficits and surpluses

Possible consequences of a deficitPossible consequences of a surplus
Lower total demand and GDP, possibly recessionMore jobs and higher GDP from export sales
Unemployment, especially in export and import-competing industriesHigher incomes and living standards
Lower living standards if incomes flow outDemand-pull inflation from strong export demand
More borrowing or selling assets to finance itA higher exchange rate, which may later reduce competitiveness
A lower exchange rate, causing imported inflationJob losses in trading partners’ economies; may cause tension

Exam tip: A small, short-term deficit is not necessarily bad: it may come from strong growth with consumers buying good-quality imports. A surplus is not always good: it can mean exports leaving the country that people at home could have consumed.

Success criterion 4

Policies for balance of payments stability

PolicyHow it improves the current accountDrawbacks
Contractionary fiscal policyHigher taxes and lower government spending reduce incomes and spending on imports.May cause unemployment and slower growth.
Monetary policyHigher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending on imports; a lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.Higher rates slow growth; a lower exchange rate causes imported inflation.
Supply-side policyEducation, training, infrastructure and incentives raise productivity and competitiveness, increasing exports.Takes a long time.
ProtectionTariffs and quotas reduce imports.Retaliation; higher prices; inefficiency.

Key terms

Balance of payments
A record of a country’s transactions with the rest of the world.
Current account
The part of the balance of payments recording trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income.
Visible balance
Exports of goods minus imports of goods.
Invisible balance
Exports of services minus imports of services.
Balance of trade
The visible balance plus the invisible balance.
Primary income
Income from investment abroad: profits, interest and dividends.
Secondary income
Transfers between countries, such as remittances and foreign aid.
Current account deficit
When more money flows out of the current account than flows in.
Current account surplus
When more money flows into the current account than flows out.
Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not an increase in a surplus on the current account of the balance of payments will increase living standards in a country. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why it will: increases demand and employment; higher employment may increase wages; households can consume more; more tax revenue to spend on education and healthcare; may raise the exchange rate, making imports more affordable.
  • Why it will not: more goods and services leave the country; opportunity cost of exports (could have been sold at home); resources may be depleted, reducing future living standards; higher demand may cause demand-pull inflation; some incomes may not rise in line with prices; a higher exchange rate may reduce competitiveness, lowering exports and incomes.
Calculation practice

Section A, part (a) style.

A country’s GDP is $208bn and its primary sector’s share of GDP is 15%. Calculate the value of its primary sector output. Show your working. [2]

Show mark guidance
  • Correct answer $31.2bn (2).
  • Correct method but wrong answer: $208bn × 15/100 (1).

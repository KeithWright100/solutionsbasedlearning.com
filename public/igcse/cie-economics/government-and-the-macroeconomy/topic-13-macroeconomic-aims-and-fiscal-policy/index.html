13. Macroeconomic aims and fiscal policy

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 4 Government and the macroeconomy · Syllabus 4.1–4.2 · Part 1 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To understand the government’s six macroeconomic aims, the conflicts between them, and how the budget, taxation and government spending are used as fiscal policy.

Syllabus Statement

4.1 Government macroeconomic intervention: the six macroeconomic aims, reasons for them and possible conflicts. 4.2 Fiscal policy: government budget, deficit and surplus; reasons for government spending; reasons for and classification of taxes; impact of taxation; fiscal policy measures and their effects on the aims.

Success criterion 1

The six macroeconomic aims

AimWhy it mattersPossible criterion
Economic growthA rise in real GDP over time; raises living standards (shown by an outward shift of the PPC).e.g. 2–3% a year
Full employment / low unemploymentEveryone willing and able to work has a job; uses resources efficiently and raises output and incomes.e.g. 3–5% unemployment
Stable prices / low inflationA low, steady rise in the general price level, measured by the CPI. Low inflation (2–3%) is not harmful.e.g. 2% inflation target
Balance of payments stabilityAvoiding a large, persistent current account deficit, which suggests the country is uncompetitive.current account close to balance
Redistribution of incomeGreater equality, e.g. through progressive taxes, benefits and pensions.a smaller gap between rich and poor
Environmental sustainabilityMeeting today’s needs without harming future generations: renewable energy, lower emissions (Paris Agreement, the UN SDGs).emissions targets

Conflicts between aims (a government usually cannot achieve them all at once):

  • Full employment and stable prices: as more people work and spend, demand-pull inflation can occur; labour shortages push up wages (cost-push inflation).
  • Economic growth and environmental sustainability: growth uses more resources and creates more pollution.
  • Full employment and balance of payments stability: people with jobs buy more imports, and rising wages make exports less competitive.
  • Economic growth and stable prices: cutting interest rates to boost growth can cause demand-pull inflation.
Success criterion 2

The government budget

The government budget is its plan of revenue (mainly taxes) and spending for a year.

Worked example: a budget deficit

Government spending = $550bn; government revenue = $400bn.

Budget deficit = 550 − 400 = $150bn. (Exports and imports are not part of the government budget.)

A deficit is common in a recession (tax revenue falls, benefit spending rises) and adds to the national debt; a surplus is more likely in a boom and can be used to repay debt.

Main areas of government spending: education, healthcare, defence, infrastructure (roads, railways), welfare benefits and state pensions, police and law and order, and interest on the national debt. Reasons: to provide essential, public and merit goods; to redistribute income; to correct market failure (e.g. subsidies for green technology); and to influence total demand.

Success criterion 3

Taxation

A tax is a compulsory payment to the government. Reasons for taxation: raising revenue; discouraging consumption of demerit goods (high taxes on cigarettes in Australia); reducing imports (tariffs); redistributing income (progressive income tax); influencing total demand (tax cuts in a recession); and encouraging environmental sustainability (carbon taxes).

TypeMeaningExamples
Direct taxPaid directly from income, wealth or profitIncome tax, corporation (profits) tax, inheritance tax, capital gains tax
Indirect taxA tax on spending on goods and servicesSales tax / VAT / GST, excise duties on alcohol, tobacco and petrol, import tariffs

Exam tip: A progressive tax is not simply one where the rich pay more tax: even a flat-rate tax makes the rich pay more dollars. What matters is the percentage of income paid.

Impact of taxation onEffect
ConsumersIndirect taxes raise prices and reduce quantity bought; direct taxes reduce disposable income.
WorkersHigh income tax may reduce the incentive to work; tax cuts increase it.
Producers / firmsHigher costs and lower profits; high corporation tax discourages investment and may drive firms abroad.
GovernmentTax revenue funds spending; very high rates encourage tax avoidance (legal) and tax evasion (illegal).
The economyTaxes reduce spending and inflationary pressure; tax cuts boost demand and growth; progressive taxes redistribute income.

Success criterion 4

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the use of taxation and government spending to influence the economy and achieve macroeconomic aims.

Fiscal stanceMeasuresEffects
Expansionary (loose)Lower taxes and / or higher government spendingIncreases total demand; used in a recession to raise growth and cut unemployment. May worsen the budget deficit and cause inflation.
Contractionary (tight)Higher taxes and / or lower government spendingReduces total demand; used to control demand-pull inflation. May slow growth and raise unemployment.
AimHow fiscal policy can help
Economic growthSpending on infrastructure, education and health; lower corporation tax to attract investment.
Low unemploymentHigher spending creates jobs; lower income tax increases incentives to work; subsidies for start-ups.
Low inflationHigher taxes or lower spending reduce excess demand.
Balance of paymentsLower taxes and subsidies help exporters compete.
RedistributionProgressive taxes plus spending on benefits, education and healthcare.
SustainabilityCarbon taxes; subsidies and tax breaks for renewable energy.

Limitations: time lags; tax rises are unpopular; deficits increase national debt; higher taxes may reduce incentives to work and invest.

Key terms

Economic growth
An increase in real GDP over time.
Inflation
A sustained rise in the general price level.
Balance of payments
A record of a country’s transactions with the rest of the world.
Redistribution of income
Making the distribution of income more equal.
Environmental sustainability
Meeting present needs without harming future generations’ ability to meet theirs.
Government budget
The government’s plan of revenue and spending for a year.
Budget deficit
When government spending is greater than revenue.
Budget surplus
When government revenue is greater than spending.
Direct tax
A tax on income, wealth or profit.
Indirect tax
A tax on spending on goods and services.
Progressive tax
A tax that takes a larger percentage of income as income rises.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a larger percentage of income from people on low incomes.
Proportional tax
A tax that takes the same percentage of income at every income level.
Fiscal policy
Using taxation and government spending to influence the economy.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse why there may be a conflict between full employment and stable prices. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • As unemployment falls, more people have incomes (1) so consumer spending / total demand rises (1).
  • If demand rises faster than output (1) there is demand-pull inflation (1).
  • Labour becomes scarce / firms compete for workers (1), wages rise, increasing costs of production (1).
  • Firms pass higher costs on as higher prices: cost-push inflation (1).
Paper 2-style question (d)

Section A, part (f) style (6 marks): up to 4 marks for reasons why and up to 4 for reasons why not.

Discuss whether or not a government should impose a carbon tax. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • Why it should: puts pressure on firms to cut CO₂ emissions (1), reduces external costs (1) and misallocation of resources (1); less extreme weather damage to buildings, crops and tourism (1); raises tax revenue (1); helps achieve environmental sustainability (1).
  • Why it should not: if other countries do not impose one (1), products become less competitive (1), exports fall (1), firms may move abroad (1) and global pollution may not fall (1); may cut jobs in oil and gas industries (1); likely to be regressive (1); complicated and costly to operate (1).

16. Employment and unemployment

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 4 Government and the macroeconomy · Syllabus 4.6 · Part 4 of 5 in this topic

Success criterion 1

Definitions and measurement

  • Employment is the use of labour as a factor of production (including the self-employed).
  • Unemployment is when people of working age are willing and able to work but cannot find a job.
  • Full employment is when everyone willing and able to work has a job (in practice, a low rate such as 3–4%, because some frictional unemployment always exists).

Unemployment is measured by the labour force survey, the International Labour Organization’s standard household survey. People count as unemployed if they are without work, have actively looked for work in the last 4 weeks and can start within 2 weeks.

Unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100

Worked example: the unemployment rate

The labour force (employed + unemployed) is 45 million and 5 million are unemployed.

Unemployment rate = 5 ÷ 45 × 100 = 11.1%. (Children and retired people are not in the labour force.)

If the labour force is 30 million and the unemployment rate is 8%, the number unemployed = 30m × 0.08 = 2.4 million.

Exam tip: The unemployment rate is a national average. It can hide much higher rates among young people, some regions or particular groups, and countries measure it in slightly different ways.

Success criterion 2

Types and causes of unemployment

TypeCause and examplesPossible solution
FrictionalPeople between jobs, e.g. school leavers searching for work or workers changing jobs. Always exists; usually short term.Better information on vacancies; job centres.
SeasonalDemand for some jobs varies with the season: ski instructors, lifeguards, harvest workers, tourism.Encourage other seasonal work; tourism all year round.
StructuralLong-term decline in demand for an industry’s products (e.g. foreign competition, new technology): UK coal mining, steel, shipbuilding; jobs replaced by AI.Retraining, education, regional policies.
Cyclical (demand-deficient)A lack of total demand in a recession; affects every industry. The most severe type.Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy.

Activity: which type of unemployment?

Identify the type of unemployment.

  • A school leaver spending time searching for a first job
  • Coal miners losing jobs as mines close for good
  • Workers in many industries losing jobs in a recession
  • A ski instructor out of work in summer
  • Bank clerks replaced by online banking
  • Falling total demand leads firms to cut staff

Success criterion 3

Consequences of unemployment

Consequences forEffect
The individualLoss of income and lower living standards; stress, poor health and low self-esteem; skills become out of date.
Producers / firmsLower consumer spending reduces sales and profits; more business failures. But a large pool of labour may keep wage costs down and make it easier to recruit.
The governmentLess income tax and sales tax revenue; more spending on unemployment benefits; a larger budget deficit.
The economyWasted resources: output is below its potential (inside the PPC); lower GDP; possible rise in crime and social problems; skilled workers may emigrate (brain drain).
Success criterion 4

Policies to reduce unemployment

PolicyHow it worksBest forDrawbacks
Expansionary fiscal policyTax cuts and more government spending raise total demand.Cyclical unemploymentBudget deficit; inflation; time lags.
Expansionary monetary policyLower interest rates boost spending and investment, raising the demand for labour.Cyclical unemploymentDepends on confidence; inflation risk.
Supply-side policyEducation and retraining; better job information; infrastructure for geographical mobility; lower benefits to increase the incentive to work; subsidies for hiring the long-term unemployed.Structural and frictional unemploymentSlow; cutting benefits may increase poverty.
ProtectionismTariffs and quotas protect domestic jobs from foreign competition (e.g. US tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles).Jobs threatened by importsRetaliation; higher prices; inefficiency.

The choice of policy depends on the cause of unemployment: demand-side policies for cyclical unemployment, supply-side policies for structural and frictional unemployment.

Key terms

Employment
The use of labour as a factor of production.
Unemployment
When people of working age are willing and able to work but cannot find a job.
Full employment
When everyone willing and able to work has a job.
Labour force survey
The ILO’s standard household survey used to measure unemployment.
Unemployment rate
The number unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
Frictional unemployment
Short-term unemployment while people move between jobs.
Seasonal unemployment
Unemployment caused by seasonal changes in demand.
Structural unemployment
Long-term unemployment caused by the decline of an industry.
Cyclical unemployment
Unemployment caused by a lack of total demand in a recession.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain two consequences for firms of a decrease in their country’s unemployment rate. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for identification and 1 mark for explanation (× 2).
  • Lower unemployment is likely to increase demand (1), raising firms’ revenue / profits (1).
  • Lower unemployment may increase wages (1) due to workers’ greater bargaining power / raising costs of production (1).
  • It may become harder to recruit workers (1), which may stop firms expanding (1).
Calculation practice

A country has a population of 75 million, of whom 40 million are employed and 5 million unemployed. Calculate the unemployment rate. [2]

Show mark guidance
  • Labour force = 40m + 5m = 45m (1).
  • Unemployment rate = 5 ÷ 45 × 100 = 11.1% (1).

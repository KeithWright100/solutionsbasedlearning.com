17. Inflation and deflation

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 4 Government and the macroeconomy · Syllabus 4.7 · Part 5 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To understand how inflation is measured, its causes and consequences for different groups, and the policies used to control it.

Syllabus Statement

4.7 Inflation: definitions of inflation and deflation; measurement using the Consumer Prices Index (CPI); demand-pull and cost-push inflation; consequences for savers, lenders, borrowers, consumers, workers, firms and the economy; policies to control inflation.

Success criterion 1

Inflation and deflation

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level over time. Not every price rises, but prices rise on average.
  • Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level (a negative inflation rate). It is worrying when caused by falling demand in a recession.
  • Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation is very high, out-of-control inflation (Zimbabwe reached 231 million per cent in 2008 and abandoned its currency).

Low, stable inflation of about 2–3% is not usually harmful. Governments often set an inflation target.

Success criterion 2

Measuring inflation: the CPI

The Consumer Prices Index (CPI) measures changes in the prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by a typical household. It is a measure of the cost of living.

  1. A survey finds what households buy; prices of the items in the basket are collected every month.
  2. Each item gets a weight showing its share of household spending (if 15% of spending is on food, food has a weight of 15 out of 100).
  3. A base year is given an index of 100. An index of 115.2 means prices are 15.2% higher than in the base year.

Worked example: inflation from a price index

The CPI rises from 123.0 to 129.15.

Inflation = (129.15 − 123.0) ÷ 123.0 × 100 = 5%.

If a basket costs $1,200 when the CPI is 125, it costs $1,200 × 135 ÷ 125 = $1,296 when the CPI is 135.

Exam tip: A weight of 200 for transport and 100 for clothing (out of 1,000) means households spend twice as much on transport as on clothing. It says nothing about how fast prices are rising (specimen paper question 33).

Success criterion 3

Causes of inflation

Demand-pull inflation is caused by total demand rising faster than the economy’s ability to supply: higher consumer and business confidence, lower interest rates, tax cuts, higher government spending, more exports, or growth in the money supply.

Cost-push inflation is caused by higher costs of production, which firms pass on as higher prices: higher wages (a wage-price spiral, where higher wages lead to higher prices and further wage claims), higher raw material and energy prices, higher rents, higher indirect taxes, and imported inflation from dearer imports or a fall in the exchange rate.

Classify each cause of inflation.

  • A sharp rise in world oil prices
  • A boom in consumer confidence
  • Workers win a 10% pay rise across industry
  • Lower interest rates encourage borrowing
  • A rise in the price of imported raw materials

Success criterion 4

Consequences of inflation and policies to control it

GroupEffect of inflation
SaversLose if the inflation rate is above the interest rate: the real value of savings falls (2% interest with 3% inflation is a real return of −1%).
LendersLose: money repaid is worth less than money lent.
BorrowersGain: the real value of their debt falls.
ConsumersLower purchasing power (real income); time spent shopping around (shoe leather costs). The poor are hit hardest.
WorkersReal wages fall unless pay rises keep up; people on fixed incomes and pensions lose most.
Producers / firmsMenu costs (changing price lists); higher wage demands; uncertainty discourages investment; exports become less competitive.
The economyLess international competitiveness, fewer exports, lower growth and possibly higher unemployment.
PolicyHow it reduces inflationLimitations
Contractionary fiscal policyRaise taxes and / or cut government spending to reduce total demand.Unpopular; may cause unemployment.
Contractionary (tight) monetary policyRaise interest rates and / or reduce the money supply, so borrowing and spending fall; a higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper.Time lags; may reduce growth and investment.
Supply-side policyRaise productivity and capacity (training, competition, lower corporation tax) so output can grow without prices rising.Slow to work.

Demand-side policies work best against demand-pull inflation; cost-push inflation is harder to control because cutting demand reduces output and jobs as well as prices.

Key terms

Inflation
A sustained rise in the general price level over time.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general price level over time.
Disinflation
A fall in the rate of inflation, with prices still rising.
Hyperinflation
Very high, out-of-control inflation.
Consumer Prices Index (CPI)
A weighted index of the prices of a typical household’s basket of goods and services.
Base year
The starting year of a price index, given the value 100.
Weights
Numbers showing each item’s share of household spending in the CPI.
Demand-pull inflation
Inflation caused by total demand rising faster than supply.
Cost-push inflation
Inflation caused by rising costs of production.
Wage-price spiral
Higher wages cause higher prices, which lead to further wage demands.
Imported inflation
Inflation caused by rising prices of imports.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse how inflation affects savers, lenders and borrowers. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • Savers lose if the interest rate is below the inflation rate (1): the real value / purchasing power of savings falls (1).
  • This may discourage saving (1) and reduce funds for investment (1).
  • Lenders lose (1) because money repaid is worth less than money lent (1).
  • Borrowers gain (1) because the real value of their debt falls (1).
Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not attracting more foreign MNCs will reduce inflation. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why it will: MNCs may use advanced technology with low costs of production; trained workers are more productive, lowering costs; lower costs reduce cost-push inflation; more competition with domestic firms restricts price rises; fewer expensive imports needed.
  • Why it will not: MNCs may drive out domestic producers and then raise prices; may pay higher wages, increasing total demand; more exports raise total demand; higher total demand causes demand-pull inflation; may deplete natural resources so imports are needed.

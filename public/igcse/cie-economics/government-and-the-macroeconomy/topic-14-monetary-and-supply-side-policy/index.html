14. Monetary policy and supply-side policy

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 4 Government and the macroeconomy · Syllabus 4.3–4.4 · Part 2 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To explain how monetary policy and supply-side policy can be used to achieve the government’s macroeconomic aims, and compare their strengths and limitations.

Syllabus Statement

4.3 Monetary policy: money supply and monetary policy; changes in interest rates, money supply and the foreign exchange rate; effects on macroeconomic aims. 4.4 Supply-side policy: education and training, infrastructure, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, incentives to work and invest, privatisation; effects on macroeconomic aims.

Success criterion 1

Monetary policy

The money supply is the amount of money in the economy at a point in time: notes, coins, bank deposits and central bank reserves. Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate to influence economic activity. It is usually carried out by the central bank. Most countries rely mainly on interest rates.

StanceMeasuresEffects
Expansionary (loose)Lower interest rates; increase the money supply (e.g. let banks lend more, or quantitative easing); lower exchange rateBoosts consumption and investment; raises growth and employment; helps exporters
Contractionary (tight)Higher interest rates; reduce the money supply; higher exchange rateReduces spending and borrowing; controls inflation; may slow growth and cost jobs
Success criterion 2

Monetary policy and the macroeconomic aims

AimHow monetary policy can help
Economic growthLower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing, encourage spending and investment, and reduce saving.
Low unemploymentMore spending and investment create jobs.
Low inflationHigher interest rates reduce consumption and investment, cutting demand-pull inflation.
Balance of paymentsA lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, improving the current account.

Limitations: time lags before changes take effect; spending also depends on confidence (in the COVID-19 pandemic, near-zero interest rates did not stop very low growth); rates cannot go much below zero; higher rates raise firms’ costs and may discourage investment, so fighting inflation can conflict with growth and employment.

Exam tip: Remember that a change in interest rates affects savers, borrowers with existing loans and mortgages, and new borrowers. Use all three in your analysis.

Success criterion 3

Supply-side policy

Supply-side policies are long-term measures that increase the productive capacity of the economy by improving the quantity and quality of factors of production. They shift the PPC outwards.

MeasureHow it increases productive capacityDrawbacks
Education and trainingBetter skills, productivity and occupational mobility; retraining for the unemployed.Expensive; takes many years.
Infrastructure spendingRoads, railways, broadband and ports cut firms’ costs and attract investment (UAE railway programme).Very costly; may need higher taxes or borrowing.
Labour market reformsReducing trade union power, unemployment benefits or minimum wages makes labour markets more flexible.May increase inequality and poverty.
Lower direct taxesLower income tax increases the incentive to work; lower corporation tax increases the incentive to invest.Reduces tax revenue; may benefit the rich most.
DeregulationRemoving barriers to entry increases competition, lowering prices and raising quality.Benefits take a long time; less protection for consumers.
Incentives to work and investTax rebates and subsidies, enterprise zones in high-unemployment areas.Opportunity cost for the government.
PrivatisationSelling state-owned firms so they are run more efficiently for profit (Air India sold to the Tata Group, 2021).May create private monopolies; job losses.
Success criterion 4

Supply-side policy and the macroeconomic aims

AimHow supply-side policy can help
Economic growthRaises productive capacity, so growth is sustainable.
Low unemploymentTraining reduces structural and frictional unemployment; more output creates jobs.
Low inflationMore capacity means output can grow without prices rising (unlike demand-side policies, which risk demand-pull inflation).
Balance of paymentsHigher productivity and lower costs make exports more competitive.
RedistributionEducation, training and incentives to work help low-income earners most.
SustainabilityInvestment and tax breaks for renewable energy and green technology.

The main limitation is time: it may take decades for better education or infrastructure to pay off, so supply-side policies are less useful for an immediate problem such as a sudden recession.

Activity: which type of policy?

Classify each measure.

  • The central bank cuts interest rates
  • The government raises spending on unemployment benefits
  • A new programme to retrain unemployed workers
  • The central bank increases the money supply
  • Removing regulations that stop new firms entering a market
  • A rise in the rate of sales tax to reduce total demand

Key terms

Money supply
The total amount of money in the economy at a point in time.
Monetary policy
Using interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate to influence the economy.
Expansionary (loose) monetary policy
Lower interest rates or more money supply to boost spending.
Contractionary (tight) monetary policy
Higher interest rates or less money supply to reduce spending and inflation.
Quantitative easing
A central bank creating money to buy bonds, increasing the money supply.
Supply-side policy
Long-term measures to increase the productive capacity of the economy.
Deregulation
Removing rules and barriers to entry to increase competition.
Labour market reforms
Changes that make the labour market more flexible, e.g. reducing trade union power.
Enterprise zone
An area where firms receive tax breaks and fewer regulations to encourage investment and jobs.
Paper 2-style question (c)

Analyse two policy measures a government could use to reduce unemployment. [6]

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks for identifying two relevant policies; remaining marks for analysis.
  • Increase government spending / expansionary fiscal policy (1), increasing total demand (1), reducing cyclical unemployment (1).
  • Reduce the rate of interest / expansionary monetary policy (1): lower cost of borrowing (1), less reward for saving (1), more consumer spending (1), more total demand (1).
  • Improve education, training or health / supply-side policy (1): more skills (1), more occupational mobility (1), less structural unemployment (1).
  • Infrastructure spending (1): more geographical mobility (1). Better job information (1): less frictional unemployment (1).
  • Do not reward the same point twice.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain how a rise in interest rates may reduce inflation. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • Borrowing becomes more expensive (1), so consumers and firms borrow and spend / invest less (1).
  • Saving becomes more attractive (1), so consumption falls (1).
  • Those with existing loans / mortgages have higher repayments and less disposable income (1).
  • Total demand falls, reducing demand-pull inflation (1).

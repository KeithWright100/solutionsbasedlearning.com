15. Economic growth and recession

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 4 Government and the macroeconomy · Syllabus 4.5 · Part 3 of 5 in this topic

Learning Intention

To understand how economic growth is measured, what causes growth and recession, their consequences, and the policies to promote growth.

Success criterion 1

Measuring economic growth

Economic growth is an increase in a country’s real GDP over time (the annual percentage change in real GDP). It is shown by an outward shift of the PPC.

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. It can be measured as total spending:

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

  • C: consumption by households; I: investment by firms (machinery, factories); G: government spending on goods and services (not benefits); X − M: net exports (export earnings minus import spending).
  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation, so it shows the true change in output. If GDP rises 5% but prices also rise 5%, real GDP has not changed.
  • GDP per head = GDP ÷ population.

Worked example: GDP per head

Australia: GDP $1,724bn, population 26.97 million → $1,724,000m ÷ 26.97m ≈ $63,900 per head.

Cambodia: GDP $31.77bn, population 16.94 million → ≈ $1,875 per head.

Success criterion 2

The business cycle and the causes of growth

Economic growth can be caused by:

  • an increase in total demand (C + I + G + X − M), e.g. higher consumer confidence, more government spending or more exports;
  • an increase in the quantity of resources, e.g. discovering natural resources (lithium in Chile), net immigration, more capital through investment;
  • an increase in the quality of resources, e.g. better education and training, higher labour productivity, new technology and AI.
Advantages of economic growthDisadvantages of economic growth
Higher living standards: people can buy more goods and servicesEnvironmental damage: pollution, congestion, climate change
More jobs and lower unemploymentRisk of demand-pull inflation if demand grows too fast
Higher tax revenue to fund public servicesInequality may widen: not everyone benefits equally
Less absolute povertyDepletion of non-renewable resources (overfishing, deforestation)
Success criterion 3

Recession

A recession is a fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters (six months).

Cause of recessionExamples
A decrease in total demandLower consumer and business confidence; higher interest rates; lower government spending; higher taxes; fewer exports; more spending on imports; a financial crisis or stock market crash.
A decrease in the quantity of resourcesWars and natural disasters destroy capital and labour; a falling population through emigration.
A decrease in the quality of resourcesLower productivity, illness (COVID-19), worse education or older equipment.
Consequences forEffect
ConsumersShops close; people save rather than spend; some must dissave.
WorkersHigher unemployment; little or no pay rises; less job security.
Producers / firmsLower demand and revenue; firms may make workers redundant, cut investment or close.
GovernmentLess tax revenue and more spending on benefits, so the budget deficit worsens.

Exam tip: Mining a new source of a useful metal would increase the quantity of resources, so it is not a cause of recession (specimen paper question 29).

Success criterion 4

Policies to promote economic growth

PolicyHow it promotes growthLimitations
Expansionary fiscal policyLower taxes and higher government spending raise total demand quickly.Increases the budget deficit; risk of inflation; time lags.
Expansionary monetary policyLower interest rates cut the cost of borrowing, boosting consumption and investment.Depends on confidence; may cause inflation; rates cannot fall much below zero.
Supply-side policiesEducation, training, infrastructure and lower corporation tax raise productive capacity.Take a long time; can be expensive.

Demand-side policies (fiscal and monetary) can help an economy recover quickly from recession; supply-side policies are needed for long-term, sustainable growth without inflation. Hong Kong used both in 2022: consumption vouchers to boost spending, and tax cuts for firms and individuals.

Key terms

Economic growth
An increase in real GDP over time.
Gross domestic product (GDP)
The total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
Real GDP
GDP adjusted for inflation.
GDP per head
GDP divided by the population.
Business cycle
Fluctuations in economic activity over time: boom, recession, slump and recovery.
Recession
A fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters.
Total demand
Total spending in the economy: C + I + G + (X − M).
Investment
Spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery and buildings.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain how investment helps to increase economic growth. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • Investment is a component of total demand (1), so more investment raises GDP directly (1).
  • Investment increases the quantity of capital (1), increasing productive capacity / shifting the PPC outwards (1).
  • New technology raises labour productivity (1), increasing output per worker (1).
Paper 2-style question (d)

Discuss whether or not economic growth always increases living standards. [8]

Show mark guidance
  • Level 3 (6–8): a reasoned discussion that accurately considers both sides of the argument, with clear, logical analysis and accurate use of terms; may point out uncertainties and reach a justified conclusion.
  • Level 2 (3–5): a reasoned discussion with some clear analysis, but it may lack depth or be one-sided.
  • Level 1 (1–2): relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Why it does: higher incomes and spending on goods and services; more jobs; more tax revenue for education and healthcare; less absolute poverty.
  • Why it may not: pollution and environmental damage; growth may be unequally shared, widening inequality; population may grow faster than GDP; inflation may erode real incomes; resource depletion harms future living standards; longer working hours.

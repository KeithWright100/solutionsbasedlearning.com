1. Scarcity, factors of production and opportunity cost

Cambridge IGCSE Economics · 1 The basic economic problem · Syllabus 1.1–1.3 · Part 1 of 2 in this topic

Learning Intention

To understand why scarcity forces every economic agent to make choices, what resources are used to make those choices real, and what each choice costs.

Syllabus Statement

1.1 The nature of the basic economic problem: finite resources and unlimited wants, the three basic economic questions, economic and free goods. 1.2 Factors of production and their rewards, and changes in their quantity and quality. 1.3 Opportunity cost and its influence on decision-making.

Success criterion 1

The basic economic problem and scarcity

In every economy resources are finite (limited) but people’s needs and wants are unlimited. The basic economic problem is how best to allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited needs and wants. Because of scarcity, choices must be made. Economics is the study of how scarce resources are allocated.

  • Needs are goods and services essential for survival (food, clean water, shelter, clothing, healthcare and education).
  • Wants are not needed for survival but are desired. Wants are unlimited: people are rarely satisfied with what they have.
  • Goods are physical items (tables, cars, toothpaste). Services are non-physical (haircuts, bus journeys, internet access).

The three main economic agents (decision-makers) are consumers (households), firms (private sector businesses) and the government (public sector). All of them face the basic economic problem:

Economic agentHow scarcity affects them
ConsumersLimited income, so buying one product means not buying another.
WorkersJobs are limited compared with the number of people looking for work, so workers may accept lower pay, move to another area or study further.
Producers / firmsLimited finance, labour and machinery, so they must choose what to make and how.
GovernmentsLimited tax revenue, so spending more on roads may mean less on schools or hospitals.
Success criterion 2

Resource allocation and economic and free goods

Scarcity means every economy must answer three basic economic questions:

  1. What to produce? Which goods and services, and how much of each (more roads and airports, or more schools and hospitals?).
  2. How to produce? Which combination of land, labour, capital and enterprise to use (for example many workers or more machines).
  3. For whom to produce? Who receives the output: everyone, or only those who can pay?
TypeMeaningExamples
Economic goodLimited in supply, so it is scarce and has an opportunity costOil, wheat, cotton, housing, cars
Free goodUnlimited in supply, so it has no opportunity costAir, seawater, rainwater, sunlight

A free good is not the same as a good that is free to the user. State education and healthcare have an opportunity cost: they are paid for by taxpayers and the money could have been used for something else, so they are economic goods.

Exam tip: In multiple-choice questions, “a good that has no opportunity cost” is the definition of a free good. A good provided by the state is still an economic good.

Success criterion 3

Factors of production and their rewards

Factors of production are the resources needed to produce a good or service. Remember them as CELL:

  • Land: natural resources such as oil, coal, water, wood, metal ores and agricultural products. Reward: rent.
  • Labour: human resources, skilled and unskilled. Reward: wages and salaries.
  • Capital: man-made resources used to make other goods, such as machinery, tools, equipment, factory buildings and delivery vehicles. Reward: interest.
  • Enterprise: the skills and risk-taking needed to combine and manage the other three factors. Reward: profit, what is left after all other costs are paid.

Causes of changes in the quantity and quality of factors of production include:

CauseExample
Costs of factorsA higher national minimum wage raises the cost of labour and may reduce the demand for it.
Government policyLower income tax can increase the incentive to work; subsidies cut costs; regulations raise costs.
New technologyAutomation and artificial intelligence increase output and productivity.
Net migrationMore immigrants than emigrants increases the quantity of labour.
Education, training and healthcareImprove the quality (skills and productivity) of labour.
Weather and climateDroughts and floods reduce the supply of agricultural land and output.
Success criterion 4

Opportunity cost and decision-making

Opportunity cost is the cost of the next best alternative forgone (given up) when making a decision. Every choice has an opportunity cost because resources have alternative uses.

  • Choosing IGCSE Economics means giving up another IGCSE subject.
  • A school buying 100 laptops may not be able to buy new science equipment.
  • Going to university means giving up the income from a full-time job during those years.
  • Building another airport terminal could mean less government money for public housing.
AgentOpportunity cost in their decisions
ConsumersLimited incomes: buying one product means giving up the benefits of another.
WorkersChoosing one career (for example accountancy) means giving up other careers.
Producers / firmsChoose between competing opportunities, for example a car maker deciding whether to spend research money on hybrid or electric vehicles.
GovernmentsMore spending on infrastructure means less available for education or healthcare.

In general, decision-makers choose the option that gives them the greatest benefit after considering what they give up.

Worked example: opportunity cost from a table

A farmer can produce 65 units of corn plus 30 units of wheat, or 55 units of corn plus 35 units of wheat.

Producing 5 more units of wheat (30 → 35) means giving up 10 units of corn (65 → 55).

So the opportunity cost of 1 unit of wheat = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 units of corn.

Exam tip: Opportunity cost is the next best alternative only, not every alternative and not the money price. If a $295 console is now worth $195 second-hand, the opportunity cost of keeping it is the $195 you could get by selling it.

Key terms

Basic economic problem
How best to allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited needs and wants.
Scarcity
When resources are limited relative to the needs and wants they could satisfy, so choices must be made.
Needs
Goods and services essential for survival.
Wants
Goods and services that are desired but not necessary for survival.
Economic agents
Decision-makers in an economy: consumers (households), firms and the government.
Economic good
A good that is limited in supply and so has an opportunity cost.
Free good
A good that is unlimited in supply and so has no opportunity cost.

The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and enterprise.
Capital
Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services, such as machinery and tools.
Enterprise
The skills and risk-taking needed to organise the other factors of production.
Opportunity cost
The cost of the next best alternative forgone when making a decision.
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain two causes of an increase in the quality of a country’s labour. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for identifying each cause and 1 mark for explaining it (× 2).
  • Improved education (1) increases workers’ skills and knowledge (1).
  • Better training (1) means workers can use new technology / complete tasks more productively (1).
  • Better healthcare (1) means workers are fitter, absent less often and more productive (1).
Paper 2-style question (b)

Explain how opportunity cost influences whether a school leaver decides to go to university. [4]

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks for understanding opportunity cost: the next best alternative (1) forgone / given up (1).
  • Up to 2 marks for application: going to university means giving up a full-time job (1); the student compares the lost earnings now with the higher earnings a degree may bring later (1); or compares the satisfaction of studying with that of working (1).

Model answer: Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. If a school leaver goes to university, the opportunity cost may be the wage they could have earned from a full-time job for three or four years. They will compare this lost income with the higher earnings they may receive later with a degree. If the expected future gain is greater than the earnings given up, they are more likely to go to university.

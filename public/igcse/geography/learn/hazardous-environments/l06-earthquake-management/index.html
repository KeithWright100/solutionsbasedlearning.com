6. Earthquake management

Success criterion 1

Preparing for earthquakes

Earthquakes cannot yet be predicted accurately, so management focuses on preparation and protection:

  • Monitoring — seismometers detect the first P-waves; early warning systems (e.g. Japan’s) give seconds of warning to stop trains and alert people.
  • Earthquake-resistant buildings — deep foundations, base isolators (rubber pads that absorb movement), steel frames that bend, cross-bracing, counterweights on the roof, and shatterproof glass. Older buildings can be retrofitted.
  • Building codes and land-use planning — laws on how buildings are constructed; keeping hospitals and schools off unstable ground.
  • Education and drills — ‘Drop, Cover and Hold On’; emergency kits with water, food, a torch and a radio.
  • Emergency planning — trained rescue teams, evacuation routes, tsunami sea walls and warning sirens.
Success criterion 2

Short-term and long-term responses

Short-term (relief: hours to weeks)Long-term (recovery: months to years)
Search and rescue for trapped peopleRebuilding homes, schools and hospitals to higher standards
Emergency medical care and field hospitalsRepairing roads, bridges, water and power supplies
Food, clean water, tents and blanketsCreating jobs and rebuilding the economy
Restoring communications; burying the deadNew building codes, warning systems and education
International emergency aidLong-term development aid and loans

Richer countries usually respond faster and rely less on foreign aid; poorer countries often depend on international help, and recovery can take many years.

Success criterion 3

Why do people live in areas at risk?

  • Fertile soils — volcanic soils are rich in minerals.
  • Jobs and cities — many major cities (Tokyo, Los Angeles, Istanbul) are in earthquake zones and offer work and services.
  • Family and culture — people have lived there for generations.
  • Poverty — people cannot afford to move elsewhere.
  • Confidence in technology — good buildings and warnings make people feel safe.
  • Low perceived risk — big earthquakes are rare, so people think ‘it won’t happen to me’.
Success criterion 4

Applying it to case studies

In the next two lessons you will compare Japan 2011 (a high-income country) with Haiti 2010 (a low-income country). For each, use these headings: cause, primary and secondary effects, short- and long-term responses, and management before and after.

Key terms

Base isolator
Rubber or spring pads under a building that absorb ground movement.
Retrofitting
Strengthening existing buildings to resist earthquakes.
Building code
Laws setting the standard to which buildings must be built.
Short-term response
Immediate relief such as rescue, food and shelter.
Long-term response
Recovery such as rebuilding and new building codes.

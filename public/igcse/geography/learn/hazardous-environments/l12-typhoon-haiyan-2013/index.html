12. Tropical storm case study: Typhoon Haiyan 2013

Short and long-term impacts of a tropical storm.

Success criterion 1

Cause and track

  • Typhoon Haiyan (known in the Philippines as Yolanda) formed in early November 2013 over the warm western Pacific, south-east of Micronesia.
  • It moved west over very warm water (over 30°C), with low wind shear, and intensified rapidly into a category 5 super typhoon.
  • It made landfall at Guiuan, Eastern Samar, in the central Philippines, at 04:40 on 8 November 2013, with sustained winds of up to about 280 km/h and gusts of 315 km/h — one of the strongest tropical cyclones ever to hit land.
  • It crossed the Visayas islands, including Leyte and the city of Tacloban, then continued west across the South China Sea to Vietnam.
Success criterion 2

Short-term and long-term impacts

Short-termLong-term
About 6,300 people killed, most by a storm surge over 5 m high in TaclobanAbout 4.1 million people displaced; many lived in tents and temporary shelters for months or years
About 16 million people affected across 44 provincesDamage of around US$2 billion to infrastructure and farming
Over 1.1 million houses damaged, about half of them totally destroyedMillions of coconut trees destroyed — they take years to regrow, so farmers lost their income
Tacloban airport, roads, power and water supplies destroyed; looting and shortages of food and waterFishing boats and rice crops lost; jobs and schooling disrupted

Responses: the government declared a state of national calamity on 11 November and the UN declared its highest-level emergency. Countries including the USA and UK sent ships, aircraft and aid; the Philippine Red Cross and international charities supplied food, water and shelter. Long-term, the government planned to ‘build back better’, including stronger homes and a no-build zone along parts of the coast, and new shelters and storm-surge warnings.

Success criterion 3

Why do people still live in areas at risk?

  • Livelihoods — fishing and farming depend on living near the coast.
  • Poverty — people cannot afford land in safer areas; moving inland means losing their jobs.
  • Family and community — people have lived there for generations.
  • Frequent but usually smaller storms — around 20 typhoons enter Philippine waters each year, so people are used to them and may underestimate the risk (in 2013 many did not understand the term ‘storm surge’).
  • Cities and services — Tacloban is a regional centre with jobs, schools and hospitals.

Key terms

Typhoon
A tropical cyclone in the north-west Pacific.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level caused by a storm’s low pressure and strong winds.
Landfall
When the centre of a storm crosses the coast.
Build back better
Rebuilding to make communities stronger than before the disaster.

Success criterion 1

How tsunamis form

  1. An earthquake at a destructive boundary under the sea makes the sea floor suddenly jerk upwards.
  2. This lifts the whole column of water above it, creating a series of waves.
  3. In deep ocean the waves are low (often under 1 m) but travel very fast — up to 700–900 km/h.
  4. Near the coast the water gets shallower, the waves slow down and grow much taller, sometimes over 30 m.
  5. Often the sea first retreats (drawback) — a natural warning sign — before the waves surge inland.

Examples: the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries; the 2011 Japan tsunami reached up to about 40 m high.

Success criterion 2

Factors affecting the impact of an earthquake

  • Magnitude and depth — bigger, shallower earthquakes cause more damage.
  • Distance from the epicentre and population density — an earthquake near a big city is far more deadly.
  • Geology — soft sediments and reclaimed land shake more and can liquefy.
  • Time of day — at night people are asleep indoors; in the day, schools and offices are full.
  • Building quality — earthquake-resistant buildings save lives; poorly built concrete buildings collapse.
  • Level of development — richer countries can afford warning systems, building codes and fast rescue.
  • Preparation — drills and education help people react correctly.

Key terms

Primary hazard
A hazard caused directly by the earthquake shaking.
Secondary hazard
A hazard that results from the primary effects, e.g. a tsunami.
Tsunami
A series of large waves caused by the sudden movement of the sea floor.
Liquefaction
When shaking makes wet soil behave like a liquid.
Drawback
The sea retreating just before a tsunami arrives.
Exam-style question

Explain how an earthquake can cause a tsunami. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: an earthquake on the sea bed (often at a subduction zone) suddenly lifts or drops the ocean floor.
  • 1 mark: this displaces a huge volume of water, creating waves that travel across the ocean at high speed.
  • 1 mark: as the waves reach shallow water near the coast they slow down and increase in height, flooding the land.

Model answer: When an earthquake occurs under the sea, the sea bed is suddenly pushed up or dropped down. This displaces a large volume of water above it, creating a series of waves that travel across the ocean very quickly. As the waves reach shallower water near the coast, they slow down and grow much taller, so they flood low-lying coastal areas.

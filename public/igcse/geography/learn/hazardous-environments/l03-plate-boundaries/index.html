Types of plate boundary

BoundaryWhat happensHazardsExample
Constructive (divergent)Plates move apart; magma rises to fill the gap, forming new crust and ocean ridgesFrequent gentle (effusive) eruptions; small, shallow earthquakesMid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland); East African Rift
Destructive (convergent) — oceanic and continentalThe denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the continental plate and meltsExplosive volcanoes; very powerful earthquakes; tsunamisNazca and South American plates (Andes); Pacific plate under Japan
Collision — two continental platesNeither plate subducts, so crust is crumpled upwards into fold mountainsPowerful earthquakes; no volcanoesIndo-Australian and Eurasian plates (Himalayas, Nepal 2015)
Conservative (transform)Plates slide past each other; friction makes them stick, then suddenly slipEarthquakes; no volcanoesSan Andreas Fault (USA); Caribbean and North American plates (Haiti)
Success criterion 2

Why do earthquakes and volcanoes happen where they do?

  • Earthquakes happen at all types of plate boundary, because stress builds up wherever plates move against each other. The most powerful happen at destructive boundaries, where huge areas of plate lock together.
  • Volcanoes need magma, so they form at constructive boundaries (where the mantle melts as pressure falls) and destructive boundaries (where the subducting plate melts). They do not form at conservative or collision boundaries.
  • Hotspots are the exception: a plume of very hot mantle rises under the middle of a plate, e.g. Hawai‘i.

Key terms

Constructive boundary
Where plates move apart and new crust forms.
Destructive boundary
Where plates move towards each other and one subducts.
Conservative boundary
Where plates slide past each other.
Subduction
When a denser plate sinks beneath another into the mantle.
Hotspot
A plume of hot mantle that creates volcanoes away from plate boundaries.
Exam-style question

Explain why volcanoes form at destructive plate boundaries. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: oceanic and continental plates move towards each other.
  • 1 mark: the denser oceanic plate is subducted beneath the continental plate.
  • 1 mark: friction and heat melt the plate in the mantle, forming magma.
  • 1 mark: the less dense magma rises through weaknesses in the crust and erupts, forming composite volcanoes (e.g. the Andes).

Model answer: At a destructive boundary an oceanic plate and a continental plate move towards each other. The denser oceanic plate is forced down beneath the continental plate in the subduction zone. As it sinks into the mantle, heat and friction cause it to melt, forming magma. This magma is less dense than the surrounding rock, so it rises through cracks in the crust and erupts, forming steep composite volcanoes such as those in the Andes.

Success criterion 1

The Earth's layers

  • Crust — the thin, rocky outer layer. Oceanic crust is thin (5–10 km), dense, young and made of basalt. Continental crust is thicker (25–70 km), lighter and older, made mainly of granite.
  • Mantle — about 2,900 km thick; mostly solid but able to flow very slowly. Its upper part, the asthenosphere, is partly molten.
  • Outer core — liquid iron and nickel; its movement creates the Earth’s magnetic field.
  • Inner core — solid iron and nickel at about 5,500°C, kept solid by enormous pressure.
  • The crust and the rigid top of the mantle form the lithosphere, which is broken into tectonic plates.
Success criterion 2

Tectonic plates and why they move

There are seven major plates — the Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian and Antarctic — and many smaller ones, such as the Nazca, Philippine, Caribbean and Cocos plates. They move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

  • Convection currents — heat from the core makes mantle rock rise, spread and sink, dragging the plates.
  • Ridge push — new, hot crust at ocean ridges is higher, so plates slide away from the ridge under gravity.
  • Slab pull — at subduction zones the cold, dense edge of a plate sinks, pulling the rest of the plate with it. Scientists now think this is the most important force.
Success criterion 3

Evidence for continental drift

In 1912 Alfred Wegener suggested that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent, Pangaea, which split up about 200 million years ago. His evidence:

  • Jigsaw fit — the coasts of South America and Africa fit together.
  • Fossils — the same fossils, e.g. the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris, are found on continents now separated by oceans.
  • Rocks and mountains — matching rock types and mountain ranges, e.g. the Appalachians in North America and the mountains of Scotland and Norway.
  • Climate evidence — glacial deposits in hot countries such as India and coal (formed from tropical forests) in Antarctica.

Wegener could not explain how continents moved, so his idea was rejected. In the 1950s–60s, sea-floor spreading and magnetic stripes in ocean rocks proved the plates move, leading to the theory of plate tectonics.

Key terms

Crust
The thin, solid outer layer of the Earth.
Mantle
The thick layer of hot, slowly flowing rock below the crust.
Lithosphere
The crust and rigid upper mantle, broken into plates.
Convection current
The circulation of heat in the mantle that helps move plates.
Slab pull
A sinking plate edge pulling the rest of the plate.
Pangaea
The supercontinent that existed about 200 million years ago.

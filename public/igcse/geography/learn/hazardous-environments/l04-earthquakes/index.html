What is an earthquake?

  • An earthquake is a sudden, violent shaking of the ground. Plates do not move smoothly: friction makes them stick, stress builds up, and the rocks eventually break or slip along a fault.
  • The point underground where this happens is the focus (hypocentre); the point on the surface directly above is the epicentre.
  • Energy travels out as seismic waves: fast P-waves (primary) arrive first; slower S-waves (secondary) and surface waves cause most of the damage.
  • Shallow-focus earthquakes (less than 70 km deep) usually cause more damage at the surface than deep ones. Smaller aftershocks often follow for days or weeks.

Measuring earthquakes

ScaleWhat it measuresHow
Moment magnitude scale (Mw)The energy released (magnitude)From seismometer readings and the size of the fault slip. Each whole number is about 32 times more energy than the one below. It has replaced the older Richter scale.
Modified Mercalli scale (I–XII)The intensity — the effects people feel and the damage causedFrom observations and reports: I = not felt; VI = felt by all, some damage; XII = total destruction. Intensity varies from place to place.

Earthquakes are recorded by seismometers, which produce a trace called a seismogram. The biggest earthquake ever recorded was magnitude 9.5, in Chile in 1960.

Key terms

Focus
The point underground where an earthquake starts.
Epicentre
The point on the surface directly above the focus.
Seismic waves
Waves of energy released by an earthquake.
Moment magnitude scale
Measures the energy released by an earthquake.
Mercalli scale
Measures the effects and damage of an earthquake (I–XII).
Aftershock
A smaller earthquake following the main one.
Seismometer
An instrument that records ground shaking.

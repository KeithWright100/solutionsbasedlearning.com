1. What is a hazard?

Success criterion 1

Hazards and disasters

A natural hazard is a natural event that threatens people, property or the environment. It only becomes a disaster when it causes serious damage and loss of life that a community cannot cope with on its own. A volcano erupting on an uninhabited island is a hazard event, but not a disaster.

TypeCauseExamples
Tectonic (geological)Movement of the Earth’s platesEarthquakes, volcanoes, tsunamis
Atmospheric (climatic)Weather and climateTropical cyclones, droughts, heatwaves, tornadoes
HydrologicalWater on or near the landRiver and coastal floods
GeomorphologicalMovement of land and slopesLandslides, avalanches
BiologicalLiving thingsDisease epidemics, locust swarms, wildfires

The risk from a hazard depends on the hazard itself, how vulnerable people are, and how well they can cope: risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity to cope.

Success criterion 2

Where do hazards occur?

  • Earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries. The Pacific Ring of Fire has about three-quarters of the world’s active volcanoes and around 90% of its earthquakes (e.g. Japan, Indonesia, Chile, the west coast of the USA). Others lie along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the Himalayas and the Mediterranean.
  • A few volcanoes form away from plate boundaries over hotspots, such as Hawai‘i.
  • Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans between about 5° and 30° north and south of the Equator, and affect coasts such as the Caribbean, the south-east USA, the Philippines, southern China, Bangladesh and northern Australia.
Success criterion 3

What makes a hazard more severe?

Physical factorsHuman factors
Magnitude (size) and duration of the eventPopulation density near the hazard
Depth of an earthquake and distance from the epicentreLevel of development and wealth
Type of rock and ground (soft ground shakes more)Quality of buildings and building codes
Secondary hazards (tsunamis, landslides, storm surges)Warning systems, education and preparation
Time of day (people asleep or at work/school)Speed and quality of the emergency response; corruption

For example, the 2010 Haiti earthquake (magnitude 7.0) killed far more people than the much stronger 2011 Japan earthquake (magnitude 9.0–9.1) because Haiti was poorer, had no building codes and was less prepared.

Key terms

Natural hazard
A natural event that threatens people, property or the environment.
Disaster
A hazard event causing serious damage and loss of life that a community cannot cope with alone.
Vulnerability
How likely people are to be harmed by a hazard.
Ring of Fire
The zone of earthquakes and volcanoes around the edge of the Pacific Ocean.
Risk
The chance of a hazard causing loss or harm.
Exam-style question

Explain one factor that affects the severity of a natural hazard. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: identify a factor, e.g. magnitude of the event; population density; level of development; preparedness; time of day; building quality.
  • 1 mark: explain how it affects the impact.
  • 1 mark: development or example, e.g. Haiti 2010 vs Japan 2011.

Model answer: The level of development of a country affects how severe a hazard is. Richer countries can afford earthquake-resistant buildings, warning systems and emergency services, so fewer people die. For example, the Haiti earthquake in 2010 killed over 200,000 people, partly because buildings were poorly constructed, whereas Japan has strict building codes.

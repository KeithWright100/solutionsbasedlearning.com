11. Tropical storms

Success criterion 1

Distribution and characteristics

  • Tropical cyclones are called hurricanes in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, typhoons in the north-west Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
  • They form over warm oceans between about 5° and 30° north and south of the Equator, usually in late summer and autumn (e.g. June–November in the North Atlantic).
  • They usually move westwards with the trade winds, then curve towards the poles. They weaken over land or cold water because they lose their supply of heat and moisture.
  • Characteristics: a huge spinning storm, often 200–700 km across; very low air pressure at the centre; sustained winds of at least 119 km/h; a calm eye surrounded by the eyewall with the strongest winds and heaviest rain; they can last for days or weeks.
Success criterion 2

Measuring tropical cyclones

The Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale rates storms from 1 to 5 by their sustained wind speed:

CategoryWind speedTypical damage
1119–153 km/hSome damage to roofs, trees and power lines
2154–177 km/hExtensive damage; trees uprooted
3178–208 km/hDevastating; major damage to homes
4209–251 km/hCatastrophic; areas uninhabitable for weeks
5252 km/h or moreCatastrophic; most homes destroyed

Storms are also measured by their central air pressure (lower = stronger) and tracked with satellites, aircraft and radar.

Success criterion 3

What is needed for a tropical cyclone to form?

  1. Warm ocean — sea surface temperature of at least 26.5°C to a depth of about 50–60 m.
  2. Distance from the Equator — at least 5°, so that the Coriolis effect (caused by the Earth’s rotation) is strong enough to make the storm spin.
  3. Moist, unstable air that rises rapidly, cools and condenses, releasing heat (latent heat) that powers the storm.
  4. Low wind shear — winds at different heights blowing at similar speeds, so the storm is not torn apart.
  5. An existing area of low pressure or disturbance to start it off.
Success criterion 4

Why are some countries more vulnerable?

  • Hazards: strong winds, torrential rain and flooding, landslides, and the storm surge — a rise in sea level caused by low pressure and winds pushing water onshore, which causes most deaths.
  • Physical vulnerability: low-lying coasts and deltas (e.g. Bangladesh), islands in storm paths (e.g. the Philippines, the Caribbean), and removal of mangroves.
  • Human vulnerability: poverty and poorly built homes, high population density on the coast, weak warning systems and communication, little money for shelters and recovery.
  • Climate change may make the strongest storms more intense and sea-level rise makes storm surges higher.

Key terms

Tropical cyclone
A large, intense low-pressure storm forming over warm tropical oceans.
Eye
The calm centre of a tropical cyclone.
Eyewall
The ring of tall clouds around the eye with the strongest winds.
Saffir–Simpson scale
Measures tropical cyclones from category 1 to 5 by wind speed.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level caused by a storm’s low pressure and strong winds.
Coriolis effect
The deflection of winds caused by the Earth’s rotation.

7. How are rural areas changing in developed countries? UK case study (2)

Success criterion 1

Accessible and remote tourist areas

Accessible tourist areas such as the Lake District and Peak District are within easy reach of large cities by motorway, so they attract huge numbers of day visitors and second-home owners. Remote areas such as the Scottish Highlands attract fewer visitors but rely heavily on tourism as other jobs decline. Across both, farm buildings are converted to holiday lets, outdoor activity businesses grow, and some villages become dominated by tourism.

Success criterion 2

Tourism in the Lake District

  • Location: Cumbria, north-west England; England’s largest National Park (2,362 km²), designated in 1951 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2017. It includes Scafell Pike (978 m), England’s highest mountain, and Windermere, its largest lake.
  • Why tourism has grown: spectacular glaciated scenery; walking, climbing, water sports; literary heritage (Wordsworth, Beatrix Potter); good access via the M6 and West Coast railway; more leisure time and car ownership.
  • Scale: about 17.5 million visitors a year (2025), spending about £1.8 billion, compared with only about 39,000 residents.

Success criterion 3

Decline in farm employment

Farming in the UK is very different from 200 years ago, when it was a major part of the economy. Today agriculture accounts for only about 1% of the UK’s GDP and 1% of employment, and the UK produces only around 60% of the food it eats.

  • Mechanisation and technology — machines replaced farm workers, so output rose while jobs fell.
  • Specialisation and larger farms — small farms were merged into bigger, more efficient businesses.
  • Low prices — supermarkets want cheap food all year round and often import or buy from large agribusinesses, so small farms struggle to make a profit.
  • Young people leave for better-paid jobs in towns.
Success criterion 4

Rural isolation and the negative multiplier effect

In remote parts of Cumbria (e.g. upland valleys away from the tourist centres), the loss of farm jobs starts a negative multiplier effect:

  1. Farm jobs are lost and young people move away.
  2. Fewer people use local shops, pubs, post offices, buses and schools, so these close.
  3. With fewer services and poor broadband and transport, the area becomes more isolated and less attractive for new businesses or families.
  4. The population becomes older and declines further, and the cycle continues.

Natural hazards add to the problems: storms such as Desmond (2015) caused severe flooding in Cumbria, damaging homes, farms, roads and bridges and cutting off communities.

Key terms

Honeypot site
A popular place that attracts large numbers of visitors.
National Park
A protected area managed for conservation and recreation.
Negative multiplier effect
A downward spiral where job losses lead to more job losses and closing services.
Rural isolation
Being cut off from services and opportunities because of distance and poor access.
Mechanisation
Replacing human labour with machines.

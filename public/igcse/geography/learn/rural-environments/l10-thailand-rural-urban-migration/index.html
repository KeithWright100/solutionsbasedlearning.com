10. How are rural areas changing in developing/emerging countries? Thailand case study (1)

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Rural Environments · Rural change in an emerging country (Thailand) · Lesson 10 of 11

Learning Intention

To apply knowledge and understanding to a case study.

Syllabus Statement

Factors leading to rural changes in a named developing country or emerging economy: population growth, changing farm economy and landholdings, natural hazards and rural–urban migration.

Success criterion 1

Key terms

Activity: match the key word to its definition

Choose the key word for each definition.

  • A process where an increasing proportion of the population lives in towns and cities
  • A form of internal migration from rural areas to cities, usually for work
  • Circumstances driving people away from rural areas, e.g. low wages
  • Attractions enticing people towards the city, e.g. job opportunities
  • An area that contains few people per square kilometre
  • An area that is crowded and contains many people per square kilometre

Population density is the average number of people per km²; population distribution is how people are spread out (even or uneven, clusters, patterns and anomalies).

Success criterion 2

Thailand: key population facts

Income groupUpper-middle income (an emerging economy)
Total populationAbout 66 million
Population growthVery slow and now close to zero; low fertility and an ageing population
Life expectancyAbout 79–80 years
Population densityAbout 130 people per km²
Largest citiesBangkok (about 10 million in the metropolitan area), then much smaller cities such as Nonthaburi, Nakhon Ratchasima (Korat) and Chiang Mai

Approximate recent figures; check the latest World Bank data.

Distribution: the population is uneven. It is most dense in Bangkok and the surrounding Central Plain (fertile, flat river lowlands, industry and services) and in parts of the North-East (Isan) around cities such as Khon Kaen and Udon Thani. It is most sparse in the mountainous north and west along the Myanmar border and in parts of the south.

Rice and the farm economy: Thailand produced only about 4% of the world’s rice in 2019/20, yet it was the world’s second-largest exporter (about 21% of exports), after India (USDA).

Success criterion 3

Push and pull factors

Activity: push or pull?

Is each factor pushing people out of the countryside or pulling them into the city?

  • High risk of famine or drought in the countryside
  • Lack of jobs, mainly in farming: very hard work for low pay
  • Lack of access to basic medical services
  • Very few entertainment services or venues
  • High birth rates put pressure on limited rural resources
  • Perception of more employment opportunities in the cities
  • Secure supply of food and water and better transport links
  • Better housing with piped water and electricity
  • A wider variety of entertainment and medical services
  • Access to education in the city

Success criterion 4

Impacts on rural Thailand

Activity: cause or consequence?

Is each statement a cause of rural–urban migration or a consequence of it?

  • Most migrants in Bangkok come from very poor households in the North-East (Isan)
  • Rural schools and universities lack funding, so young people move for education
  • Rising costs of land, fertiliser and pesticides while crop prices (rubber, sugar cane, cassava) fluctuate
  • Rural populations are ageing as young adults leave, reducing farm productivity
  • About 3 million children (24%) are raised by grandparents in “skip-generation households”
  • Migrants send money home (remittances) that supports rural economies
  • Fewer farm workers may threaten food security in a major rice producer
  • Less pressure on natural resources allows some land to be rewilded or protected

Key terms

Urbanisation
An increasing proportion of people living in towns and cities.
Rural–urban migration
Moving from the countryside to cities, usually for work.
Push factor
Something that drives people away from an area.
Pull factor
Something that attracts people to an area.
Remittances
Money migrants send back to their families.
Skip-generation household
Children living with grandparents while parents work elsewhere.

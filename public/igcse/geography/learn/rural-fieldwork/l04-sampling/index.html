4. Sampling: why and how we sample

Success criterion 1

What is sampling and why do we sample?

Sampling is a shortcut method for investigating a whole population. Data is gathered on a small part of the whole parent population or sampling frame and used to show what the whole picture is like.

Why sample? In reality there is not enough time, energy, money, labour, equipment or access to suitable sites to measure every single item or site in the whole sampling frame. So an appropriate sampling strategy is used to get a representative and statistically valid sample of the whole.

Success criterion 2

Four sampling methods

MethodHow it works
RandomSelecting a person to interview or site to measure at random. It is unbiased because particular people or places are not specifically chosen. Random point sampling: draw a grid over a map of the study area, use random number tables to get grid references, then sample as close to those points as possible.
SystematicSamples are chosen in a regular way: evenly spaced in space (e.g. every 2 m along a transect), at regular time intervals (e.g. every half hour), or regularly numbered (e.g. every 10th house or person).
StratifiedDividing the sample into groups, e.g. three sites from each section of coastline, or five people from each age range. It can be combined: stratified random (random samples within each category) or stratified systematic (regular samples within each category).
ConvenienceA non-random (non-probability) method where people or sites are chosen because they are the easiest to reach — because they are nearby, available at that time or willing to take part. Sometimes called accidental sampling.

Activity: which sampling method?

Identify the sampling method.

  • Using random number tables to pick grid references on a village map
  • Interviewing every 10th person who walks past
  • A 2-minute traffic count every 30 minutes
  • Interviewing two shopkeepers and two restaurant owners in each village
  • Interviewing whichever shopkeepers were free and willing to talk
  • Counting traffic at the spot nearest to where the bus stopped

Success criterion 3

Sampling in our rural enquiry

  • Land use survey and services tally — we walked around each village and recorded every building we could reach, so this was close to a full survey rather than a sample.
  • Traffic and pedestrian counts — a systematic time sample: counts lasted the same length of time (e.g. 2 minutes) and were repeated.
  • Interviews — at least two people working in each village (e.g. shopkeepers and restaurant owners). This was mainly convenience sampling (who was available and willing), with a stratified element because we chose people from each village.

Limitation: convenience sampling may be biased — the people who agreed to talk may not represent all business owners.

Key terms

Sampling
Investigating part of a population to represent the whole.
Sampling frame
The whole population or area from which a sample is taken.
Random sampling
Every person or site has an equal chance of being chosen.
Systematic sampling
Sampling at regular intervals in space, time or number.
Stratified sampling
Dividing the sample into groups and sampling each one.
Convenience sampling
Choosing whoever or whatever is easiest to access.
Representative
A sample that fairly reflects the whole population.

5. Rural enquiry methods: how we collected the data

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Rural Fieldwork · Collecting the data · Lesson 5 of 7

Learning Intention

To plan, organise and carry out fieldwork data collection.

Syllabus Statement

Paper 2, Section B fieldwork: investigating rural environments &mdash; the relationship between settlement size and the provision of services.

Success criterion 1

Land use survey

How: we walked around each village and recorded our findings on a blank base map. We drew in the individual buildings and noted their land use, e.g. residential, commercial, public building. Each map had a key, using letters or a different colour for each land use.

How it helped: it showed the different types of services in each settlement and whether they were high- or low-order. We used it alongside the services tally to compare the villages.

Success criterion 1

Types of services survey (tally)

How: while completing the land use survey we kept a tally of the types of services in each village: the number of high-order services, low-order services and any other services that did not fit either category. We also noted additional observations.

How it helped: a quantitative method that gave numerical data on the number and type of services in each village, so we could plot graphs and make comparisons.

Success criterion 1

Traffic count and pedestrian count

How: we chose a suitable time frame (e.g. 2 minutes) and gave each group member a role: one person on the timer, one counting traffic in one direction, another counting traffic in the other direction. We tallied cars, commercial vehicles, taxis, buses and motorcycles in both directions, and counted pedestrians twice.

How it helped: both are quantitative. They show how busy each settlement is so the villages can be compared. We would expect larger settlements to have more pedestrians and traffic, more services and more high-order services, because they have a larger sphere of influence.

Success criterion 2

Interviews

How: before the trip we planned our interviews in groups and designed 3 questions to ask each person (each group’s questions were different). In each village we spoke to at least 2 people working locally, e.g. shopkeepers and restaurant owners.

How it helped: this depends on your questions. For example, “Where do most of your customers come from?” tests the sphere of influence; “Where do you go for a bank or hospital?” shows where high-order services are found.

Success criterion 2

Annotated photographs

How: at each village we took lots of photographs, especially of the different types of services. We made notes to explain each photo and recorded the weather and time of day. Back in class we annotated the photographs.

How it helped: a qualitative method that gave a bigger picture of each area and supported the quantitative data from the services tally, traffic and pedestrian counts and land use survey.

Key terms

Land use survey
Mapping what each building or area is used for.
Base map
A blank map used to record fieldwork data.
Tally
A quick way of counting using marks in groups of five.
Traffic count
Counting vehicles passing a point in a set time.
Pedestrian count
Counting people walking past a point in a set time.
Interview
Asking people planned questions to collect their views.
Exam-style question

Explain how a services survey helps to test the hypothesis that larger settlements have more services. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: a services survey records the number and type of services in each settlement.
  • 1 mark: services can be classified as high-order or low-order.
  • 1 mark: the number of services can be compared with settlement population to see whether there is a positive relationship (e.g. on a scatter graph).

Model answer: A services survey counts and records the type of every service, such as shops, schools and doctors, in each settlement. The services can be classified as high or low order. Comparing the number of services with the population of each settlement, for example on a scatter graph, shows whether larger settlements do have more services, so the hypothesis can be accepted or rejected.

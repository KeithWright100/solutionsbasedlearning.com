3. Planning: types of data, secondary research, equipment and risk

ExamplesServices tallies, traffic and pedestrian counts, land use maps, photographs, interviewsGovernment websites and census data, newspapers, historical photographs, tourism websites
StrengthsYou know exactly how it was collected; specific to your questionQuick and cheap; can cover large areas or long time periods
LimitationsTime-consuming; only a snapshot of one dayMay be out of date, biased or collected for a different purpose
Success criterion 2

Quantitative and qualitative data

  • Quantitative data is countable or measurable, relating to numbers. It tells us how many, how much or how often. It can be presented in graphs and analysed statistically. Example: our traffic count.
  • Qualitative data is descriptive, relating to words or language. It describes attributes and helps us understand why or how. Example: our annotated photos and interviews.

Activity: classify our data

Is each one quantitative or qualitative? (Hint: all of these are primary.)

  • Traffic count
  • Pedestrian count
  • Tally of high- and low-order services
  • Number of buildings of each land use
  • Annotated photographs
  • Shopkeeper interview answers
  • Notes on our observations

Success criterion 3

Secondary data in our study

  • Census data for each settlement from a government website gave the total population of each village and some details of its population structure. We used it to compare the size of each settlement with its number of services, and to create our initial hypothesis.
  • The Thai national parks website and the Tourism Authority of Thailand website helped us research the characteristics of the area and the main functions of each settlement (e.g. fishing, farming, tourism) before we visited, which also helped us form our hypothesis.
Success criterion 4

Equipment and risk assessment

EquipmentUsed for
Clipboard and penRecording the land use survey, tallies and interview answers
Clicker (tally counter)Counting pedestrians and vehicles accurately
TimerKeeping each count to the same time period (e.g. 2 minutes)
CameraPhotographs of services and land use to annotate later
Base mapPlotting buildings and land use in each village

Risks: traffic accidents; injury from slipping, falling or falling/flying objects; a student going missing; bites from stray dogs; stranger danger; heat stroke and sunburn.

Activity: match each risk to its mitigation

Choose the risk that each control measure reduces.

  • High-vis jackets, safe crossing points and avoiding fast or dangerous junctions
  • Route checked beforehand for uneven surfaces and holes; suitable footwear
  • Walking in pairs with a 12:1 student-to-adult ratio
  • Sun cream, hats and regular breaks in the shade or air conditioning
  • Keep away from animals and stay with the group
  • Stay in pairs, only interview people working in shops, with an adult nearby

Key terms

Primary data
Information collected first-hand.
Secondary data
Information previously collected by someone else.
Quantitative data
Numerical data that can be counted or measured.
Qualitative data
Descriptive data in words or images.
Census
An official count of the population.
Risk assessment
Identifying hazards and how to reduce them.
Exam-style question

Explain one advantage of using secondary data in a rural enquiry. (2 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: advantage identified, e.g. provides background/context; covers a larger area or longer time period; quick and cheap to obtain.
  • 1 mark: development, e.g. census data on population can be compared with our service counts to support the hypothesis.

Model answer: Secondary data, such as census data on village populations, is quick and cheap to obtain and covers a larger area than we could survey ourselves. This allows us to compare our primary data on services with the size of each settlement to test the hypothesis.

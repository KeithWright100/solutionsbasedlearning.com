9. Case studies: Development projects in Nigeria and Kenya

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Development and Human Welfare · Reducing the development gap · Lesson 9 of 9

Learning Intention

Syllabus Statement

Success criterion 2

Top-down: the Lagos–Calabar coastal railway, Nigeria

  • Location — a planned 1,402 km standard-gauge line along Nigeria’s southern coast from Lagos to Calabar near the Cameroon border, through about 10 states and cities such as Benin City, Warri, Port Harcourt and Uyo, with 22 stations and branches to seaports.
  • Aims — link Nigeria’s coastal cities, oil region and seaports; move goods and people faster and more cheaply than by road; boost trade and the coastal economy.
  • How it works — a top-down project planned by the federal government and built by a large Chinese company, the China Civil Engineering Construction Corporation (CCECC); the contract was first signed in 2014.
  • Funding — about $11–12 billion, mainly through foreign loans. China’s Export-Import Bank was expected to lend but withdrew in 2017; the government later arranged other finance, including with Standard Chartered Bank.
  • Who benefits — businesses, ports and the oil industry; commuters and traders; construction workers; the Chinese contractor.
  • Strengths — could cut road congestion and accidents, create jobs and connect major ports and cities.
  • Weaknesses — repeated delays and funding problems (little progress by 2021); huge debt, much of it owed to China; corruption concerns; land taken from local people; benefits may go mainly to businesses rather than the poorest.

Linked project: the Kano–Maradi railway

In March 2024 Nigeria secured $1.3 billion to complete a railway from Kano in northern Nigeria to Maradi in neighbouring Niger. A CCECC-led group provides 85% of the funding, with the Nigerian government, the African Export-Import Bank and the African Development Bank providing the rest. It aims to boost trade with Niger and help rebuild the poor north, which has suffered from an Islamist insurgency.

Success criterion 3

Bottom-up: WaterAid in Kenya

  • Location — Kenya, East Africa. Water is scarcest in the arid and semi-arid north and east, in remote villages and in urban slums such as those in Nairobi. WaterAid’s school health clubs include Kangemi School in Nairobi.
  • The need — over 3 in 10 people in Kenya lack clean water and 7 in 10 lack a decent toilet; more than 5,000 children die each year from diseases caused by dirty water and poor sanitation; up to a third of water pumps are broken at any one time.
  • Aims — give people clean water, decent toilets and good hygiene, and persuade governments and service providers to reach the poorest communities.
  • How it works — WaterAid works with local partners and communities, who help plan and look after projects. It uses appropriate technology such as hand-dug wells, hand pumps, rainwater harvesting and community latrines, and runs school health clubs that teach hygiene through drama, games and workshops (including managing periods safely).
  • Funding — WaterAid is a UK-based charity (founded in 1981) funded by donations (e.g. £2 a month), fundraising events, grants and partnerships.
  • Who benefits — the poorest communities, especially women and girls, who spend less time collecting water and can attend school and work; children, through less diarrhoea and disease.
  • Strengths — low cost; local people can repair it; community ownership makes it sustainable; directly improves health and education.
  • Weaknesses — small scale, reaching relatively few people; depends on donations; WaterAid describes its Kenya programme as still at the pilot stage; pumps can break if maintenance is not funded.

Key terms

Top-down development
Large projects planned by governments or big organisations.
Bottom-up development
Small projects planned with and run by local communities.
Appropriate technology
Technology suited to local skills, money and resources.
Standard gauge
The international standard width of railway track (1,435 mm).
WaterAid
A UK charity working on clean water, toilets and hygiene.
Exam-style question

Compare a top-down and a bottom-up development project. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: top-down projects are planned by governments or large organisations, are usually large-scale and expensive, e.g. the Lagos–Calabar coastal railway in Nigeria.
  • 1 mark: bottom-up projects involve local communities, are small-scale and use appropriate technology, e.g. WaterAid in Kenya.
  • 1 mark: comparison of benefits, e.g. top-down can transform national infrastructure and trade; bottom-up meets local needs directly.
  • 1 mark: comparison of drawbacks, e.g. top-down may displace people and lead to debt; bottom-up has a limited scale.

Model answer: A top-down project, such as the coastal railway in Nigeria, is planned by the government and funded by large loans; it can boost trade and connect cities, but it is expensive, may increase debt and does not always benefit local people. A bottom-up project, such as WaterAid in Kenya, works with local communities to build wells and hand pumps using simple technology, so it directly improves health and is sustainable, but it only helps a small area.

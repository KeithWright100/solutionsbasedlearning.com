4. Demographic data and the demographic transition model

Success criterion 1

Key demographic terms

  • Birth rate — live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Death rate — deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase — birth rate minus death rate (usually given as a %). If the death rate is higher, there is natural decrease.
  • Fertility rate — average number of children born to each woman. About 2.1 is ‘replacement level’, where the population stays stable.
  • Infant mortality rate — babies dying before their first birthday per 1,000 live births.
  • Maternal mortality rate — women dying from pregnancy or childbirth per 100,000 live births.
Success criterion 1

The demographic transition model (DTM)

StageBirth and death ratesWhy?Example
1 High fluctuatingBoth high; population stable and smallLittle health care, poor sanitation, famine and disease; children needed for farm workSome isolated tribes today
2 Early expandingBirth rate high; death rate falls rapidlyBetter health care, clean water, vaccinations and food supplyNiger, Chad
3 Late expandingBirth rate falls; death rate keeps fallingContraception, education of women, fewer children needed as infant mortality falls, urbanisationKenya, India
4 Low fluctuatingBoth low; population stableWomen in careers, cost of raising children, good health careUK, USA
5 DecliningBirth rate below death rateVery low fertility and an ageing populationJapan, Italy

The DTM shows that as countries develop, their population moves from high birth and death rates to low ones. The population structure (shown by a pyramid) changes too: a wide base at stage 2, straighter sides at stage 4, and a narrowing base at stage 5.

Success criterion 2

Reading a population pyramid

A population pyramid shows the population divided into age groups (usually 5 years) with males on the left and females on the right. When you interpret one, comment on:

  1. the base — a wide base shows a high birth rate; a narrowing base shows a falling birth rate;
  2. the sides — steep, concave sides show a high death rate at all ages; straight sides show low death rates;
  3. the top — a wide top shows high life expectancy and an ageing population;
  4. bulges or indents — e.g. a baby boom, a war, migration of young workers or a one-child policy;
  5. the dependency — how many young (0–14) and old (65+) people depend on the working population (15–64).

Less developed countries such as Niger have high fertility, high infant mortality, low life expectancy and a very young population. More developed countries such as the UK and Japan have low fertility, very low infant mortality, long life expectancy and an ageing population. Emerging countries such as India sit in between.

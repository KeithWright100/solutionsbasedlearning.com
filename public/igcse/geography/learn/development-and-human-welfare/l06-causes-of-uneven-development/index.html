6. Causes of uneven development

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Development and Human Welfare · Uneven development · Lesson 6 of 9

Learning Intention

To understand human patterns and processes.

Syllabus Statement

The global pattern of the uneven development between and within countries and the factors (social, historical and economic) that have led to these spatial variations.

Success criterion 1

An unequal world

  • Income — GNI per capita ranges from over $80,000 in countries such as Norway and Switzerland to under $1,000 in countries such as Niger and Burundi.
  • Health — life expectancy is over 80 in Japan, Europe and Australia, but under 60 in several African countries.
  • Education — almost all adults can read in high-income countries; in some Sahel countries fewer than half can.
  • Wealth — the richest 10% of the world’s people hold most of its wealth, while the poorest half hold only a tiny share.
  • Within countries — inequality also exists between regions (e.g. North and South Italy), between cities and rural areas, and between rich and poor neighbourhoods of the same city.

This gap between the richest and poorest countries is called the development gap. It is often described as a divide between the Global North (richer countries) and the Global South (poorer countries), although many emerging economies such as China, Brazil and Thailand are closing the gap.

Success criterion 2

Why is development uneven?

FactorExamples
PhysicalLandlocked countries (e.g. Niger, Chad) find trade harder and more expensive. Extreme climates (drought, desertification) make farming difficult. Natural hazards (earthquakes, tropical storms) destroy homes and infrastructure. Tropical diseases such as malaria reduce people’s ability to work. Few natural resources — or resources that lead to conflict.
HistoricalColonialism: European powers took raw materials and labour from Africa, Asia and South America and drew borders that later caused conflict. The slave trade removed millions of people from Africa. Former colonies were left exporting primary products.
EconomicReliance on low-value primary products (e.g. coffee, cocoa) whose prices go up and down; unfair terms of trade and trade barriers; large debts to richer countries and banks; little investment in industry.
Social and politicalPoor education and health care; gender inequality; conflict and civil war (e.g. South Sudan, Yemen); corruption and weak government that waste money meant for development.

These factors often combine into a cycle of poverty: low income → little tax and investment → poor schools and hospitals → low-skilled, unhealthy workforce → low income.

Success criterion 3

Dependency theory

Dependency theory was developed in the 1960s by economists such as Andre Gunder Frank. It argues that poorer countries are poor because of the way they have been linked to richer countries, not simply because they are ‘behind’.

  • The world is divided into a rich core (e.g. Europe and North America) and a poorer periphery (former colonies).
  • The periphery supplies cheap raw materials and labour; the core turns them into valuable manufactured goods and sells them back at a higher price.
  • Profits flow from the periphery to the core, so the core grows richer and the periphery stays dependent — through colonialism in the past, and through debt, trade rules and transnational corporations today.
  • Example: Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana grow most of the world’s cocoa, but most of the value of a chocolate bar is earned by the companies that process, brand and sell it in richer countries.

Criticism: some former colonies, such as South Korea and Singapore, have become rich, which suggests that dependency can be broken through education, investment and industrialisation.

Key terms

Development gap
The difference in wealth and well-being between the richest and poorest countries.
Colonialism
When a powerful country takes control of another country and its resources.
Primary products
Raw materials such as crops, timber and minerals.
Terms of trade
The value of a country’s exports compared with the value of its imports.
Landlocked
A country with no coastline.
Dependency theory
The idea that poorer countries stay poor because they depend on richer countries.
Core and periphery
The rich centre of the world economy and the poorer areas that supply it.
Exam-style question

Explain two causes of uneven development between countries. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark for each cause (max 2), e.g. colonialism and dependency; physical factors (landlocked, climate, natural hazards, disease); trade (exporting low-value primary products); debt; conflict and corruption.
  • 1 mark for each explanation.

Model answer: One cause is colonialism. Many former colonies were set up to export raw materials to European countries, so they still depend on low-value primary exports and have weak manufacturing industries. A second cause is physical geography: landlocked countries such as Chad have high transport costs for trade, and tropical diseases such as malaria reduce productivity.

8. International strategies to reduce the development gap

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Development and Human Welfare · Reducing the development gap · Lesson 8 of 9

Learning Intention

To understand the need for sustainable development when planning the future.

Syllabus Statement

The range of international strategies (international aid and intergovernmental agreements) that attempt to reduce uneven development.

Differing views held by individuals, organisations and governments on tackling the development gap.

Success criterion 1

International aid

Aid is help given by one country or organisation to another, as money, goods, expertise or loans.

  • Bilateral aid — from one government to another (e.g. the UK to Kenya).
  • Multilateral aid — from many governments through international organisations such as the World Bank, the UN or the EU.
  • NGO / voluntary aid — from charities such as WaterAid, Oxfam or the Red Cross, funded by donations.
  • Short-term (emergency) aid — food, water, tents and medicine after a disaster or conflict.
  • Long-term (development) aid — projects such as schools, wells or training that help people support themselves.
  • Tied aid — aid given on condition that the receiving country buys goods or services from the donor.

The UN target is for rich countries to give 0.7% of their GNI as aid; only a few (e.g. Norway, Sweden) reach it. The UK cut its aid from 0.7% to 0.5% in 2021 and plans to reduce it to 0.3% by 2027.

Success criterion 1

Trade, fair trade, debt relief and agreements

  • Trade — selling goods abroad earns money and creates jobs. Trade blocs and agreements that cut tariffs (taxes on imports) help poorer countries sell more, especially if they can export processed and manufactured goods rather than raw materials.
  • Fair trade — certified products (e.g. bananas, coffee, cocoa) pay farmers a guaranteed minimum price plus a Fairtrade Premium that the community spends on projects such as schools or clean water.
  • Debt relief — cancelling or reducing countries’ debts so that money spent on repayments can go on health and education. The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative (1996) and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) cancelled debts for over 30 countries, mostly in Africa.
  • Microfinance — small loans to people who cannot borrow from banks, so they can start businesses.
  • Intergovernmental agreements — the UN Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) and the Sustainable Development Goals (17 goals, 2015–2030), e.g. ending poverty and hunger and providing clean water for all.

Success criterion 2

Differing views

  • Individuals — some donate to charities and buy fair trade; others believe charity should begin at home.
  • NGOs (e.g. Oxfam, WaterAid) — usually favour small-scale, community-led projects and campaign for debt relief and fairer trade.
  • Governments of donor countries — may see aid as a moral duty and a way to gain influence and trade, but may cut aid when budgets are tight (e.g. the UK).
  • Governments of receiving countries — want investment and fairer trade rules, and may resist conditions attached to aid or loans.
  • International organisations (World Bank, IMF) — tend to favour economic growth, trade and large infrastructure loans.

Key terms

Bilateral aid
Aid from one government to another.
Multilateral aid
Aid given through international organisations such as the World Bank.
Tied aid
Aid that must be spent on goods or services from the donor.
NGO
Non-governmental organisation, e.g. a charity.
Fair trade
Trade that guarantees producers a fair minimum price and a social premium.
Debt relief
Cancelling or reducing a country’s debts.
Sustainable Development Goals
17 UN goals agreed in 2015 to be met by 2030.

4. Coastal enquiry methods: how to collect the data

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Coastal Fieldwork · Collecting the data · Lesson 4 of 7

Learning Intention

To plan, organise and carry out fieldwork data collection.

Syllabus Statement

Paper 1, Section B fieldwork: investigating the impact of human intervention on coastal processes.

Success criterion 1

Beach width and gradient

Beach width

  1. Decide where the transect line will be.
  2. Place the end of the tape measure (0 m) where the waves (swash) break on the beach.
  3. Run the tape in a straight line to the back of the beach (e.g. the vegetation line or sea wall), keeping it flat, and record the width in metres.

Beach gradient

  1. Two people hold ranging poles 5 m apart along the transect, starting at the water’s edge.
  2. Using a clinometer, sight from one pole to the same height on the other pole up the beach (foresight), then back down (backsight). Record both angles.
  3. Move both poles 5 m up the beach and repeat until you reach the back of the beach.

Why? Width and gradient test hypotheses 1 and 2: if defences such as breakwaters reduce wave energy, more sediment is deposited, making the beach wider and changing its profile.

Success criterion 1

Beach transect (vegetation and sediment)

  1. Keep the tape where it is along the transect.
  2. At 0 m, drop a quadrat (with 100 small squares) over your shoulder onto the beach.
  3. Count the squares covered by each type of material (e.g. sand, shingle, shells, vegetation, litter). Each square = 1%.
  4. Record on the data sheet and repeat every 10 m.

Why? Shows how the make-up of the beach changes up the profile. More vegetation suggests a more stable, sheltered beach where sediment is not being removed.

Success criterion 1

Longshore drift and wave frequency

Longshore drift (LSD)

  1. Work out which way LSD is moving (look at the angle of the waves and any build-up of sediment against groynes).
  2. Place the first ranging pole at 0 m at the water’s edge.
  3. Stand about 3 m into the sea and drop a float (e.g. a tennis ball or dog biscuit) in line with the pole.
  4. Time one minute, place the second ranging pole level with the float and measure the distance and direction it travelled. Repeat three times and calculate a mean.

Wave frequency

  1. Stand at the edge of the sea with a stopwatch.
  2. Count how many waves break in one minute.
  3. Repeat three times and calculate the mean.

Why? Tests hypothesis 3 and shows whether defences such as reefs and breakwaters reduce wave energy and slow LSD. Fewer waves per minute usually means more constructive waves, which build beaches up.

Optional: wind speed with an anemometer at head height, and wind direction using a compass and light material.

Success criterion 2

Field sketches and photos

  • At each site, draw a detailed field sketch showing the full width of the beach and its surroundings.
  • Label natural features (sand, vegetation, river mouth, headland) and all human management (breakwaters, groynes, sea walls, boats, buildings).
  • Add annotations that explain what you can see, e.g. “sand built up on the south side of the groyne”.
  • Take lots of photos from the same viewpoint at each site so they can be compared.
Success criterion 3

Justifying your methods

Activity: which method tests which idea?

Choose the method that best provides evidence for each statement.

  • The beach is wider where there is an offshore reef
  • The beach profile is steeper where there are no defences
  • Defences reduce the number of destructive waves
  • Groynes slow the movement of sediment along the coast
  • Sheltered beaches have more vegetation
  • Recording the type and position of coastal defences at each site

In the exam you must justify each method: say what it measures, how it was done, and how it helps answer the hypothesis or enquiry question.

Key terms

Clinometer
An instrument used to measure angles, e.g. beach gradient.
Ranging pole
A pole used to mark points and sight along.
Quadrat
A square frame used to estimate % cover.
Foresight/backsight
Gradient readings taken up and down the beach.
Wave frequency
The number of waves breaking per minute.
Field sketch
An annotated drawing of a site.
Exam-style question

Describe how beach gradient can be measured along a transect. (3 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: two ranging poles are placed a set distance apart (or at breaks of slope) along a transect from the sea to the back of the beach.
  • 1 mark: a clinometer is used to measure the angle between the poles (sighting from one pole to the same height on the other).
  • 1 mark: the process is repeated along the whole transect and results recorded; repeat at several transects for reliability.

Model answer: Two ranging poles are placed at each change of slope along a transect running from the sea to the back of the beach. One person looks through a clinometer from a mark on the first pole to the same height on the second pole and reads off the angle. This is repeated up the whole beach, and at several transects, to build up a profile of the beach gradient.

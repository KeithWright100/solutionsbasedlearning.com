6. Rising energy demand

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Economic Activity and Energy · Energy resources · Lesson 6 of 10

Learning Intention

To understand that energy resources are unevenly distributed and that demand is rising.

Syllabus Statement

Global energy demand is increasing and energy resources are unevenly distributed.

Reasons for the rising global demand for energy: population growth, economic development and rising living standards, and technological change.

Different views on the relationship between population and resources.

Success criterion 1

The global trend

Global energy use has risen almost every year, with brief falls during the 2009 financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic. Fossil fuels (oil, coal and natural gas) still provide around four-fifths of the world’s primary energy, although renewables are growing fastest.

Success criterion 2

Why is demand rising?

  • Population growth — the world population passed 8 billion in 2022; more people need energy for cooking, heating, lighting and transport.
  • Economic development — industrialising countries such as China and India use large amounts of energy in factories, construction and transport.
  • Rising living standards — a growing middle class buys cars, air conditioners, fridges, computers and phones, and travels by air.
  • Technology — more electrical devices, data centres and the internet; electric vehicles increase electricity demand (even though they reduce oil demand).
  • Urbanisation — city dwellers usually use more commercial energy than rural people who rely on wood.

Activity: which reason?

Classify each cause of rising energy demand.

  • More people need food cooked and homes heated
  • Fast-growing cities in Africa and Asia
  • New steel works and cement factories in India
  • China builds new roads and railways
  • Families buy their first car or air conditioner
  • More people take holiday flights
  • Data centres for cloud computing and AI
  • More electric vehicles to charge

Success criterion 3

Uneven consumption

  • High-income countries use far more energy per person than low-income countries. An average person in the USA uses many times more energy than an average person in Ethiopia or Bangladesh.
  • China is now the world’s largest energy consumer in total, but its use per person is still lower than the USA’s.
  • Hundreds of millions of people, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, still have no access to electricity and rely on wood, charcoal and kerosene.
  • Most of the growth in demand now comes from emerging and developing countries, while demand in many HICs is stable or falling thanks to efficiency.

Production versus consumption: the energy gap

Maps of energy production and consumption per person (in kg of coal equivalent) show that the countries that produce the most energy are not always the ones that consume the most. Canada, Norway, Saudi Arabia and Russia produce more than they use and can export the surplus. Japan, South Korea and much of Europe consume far more than they produce, so they have a wide energy gap and depend on imports.

Model ideas

a) Canada has energy security: it is not affected by supply disruptions or price rises abroad, and it earns money from exports. Japan must buy oil, gas and coal on world markets, so it is vulnerable to price shocks and conflicts. b) Japan could restart or build nuclear power, invest in offshore wind, solar and geothermal (it has many volcanoes), improve efficiency, and sign long-term supply deals with several countries.

Energy security

Energy security means having reliable, affordable access to enough energy. Ways a country can improve it:

Diversify energy sources (nuclear, renewables)
Improve energy efficiency and conservation in homes
Explore and develop domestic reserves
Improve infrastructure links, e.g. electricity pylons and interconnectors
Invest in research and development, e.g. solar power
International collaboration on energy projects
Success criterion 4

Population and resources: Malthus and Boserup

Malthus (1798) – pessimisticBoserup (1965) – optimistic
Population grows faster than food supply. Food demand rises, there is less food for everyone, so mortality rises (famine, disease, war) until population falls back.“Necessity is the mother of invention.” Rising demand for food and resources leads to innovation and invention, so farming produces more or more food is imported.

The Club of Rome (1972) “Limits to Growth” report warned that rising demand for food and resources would lead to resources running out and population decline unless policies regulated growth; industrialisation and lower fertility could reduce population growth.

Link to energy: Malthusians warn fossil fuels will run out and climate change will limit growth; Boserup’s view is supported by fracking, renewables and efficiency improvements that have increased energy supply.

Key terms

Energy demand
The amount of energy people and businesses want to use.
Energy consumption
The amount of energy actually used.
Primary energy
Energy in its raw form, e.g. coal, oil, wind.
Energy per capita
Energy used per person.
Energy poverty
Lack of access to modern energy such as electricity.

7. Renewable versus non-renewable energy

Success criterion 1

Classifying energy resources

Non-renewable resources are finite: they took millions of years to form and will run out (coal, oil, natural gas, and uranium for nuclear power). Renewable resources are naturally replaced and will not run out (solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass).

Success criterion 3

The energy mix

To ensure energy security and avoid relying on imports, countries try to have a diverse energy mix. This is especially important for countries without large energy resources of their own.

The UK’s changing mix: in the 1960s coal supplied well over half of the UK’s primary energy. North Sea gas from the 1970s and the “dash for gas” in the 1990s replaced most coal; nuclear has supplied a steady share; since about 2010 wind, solar and biofuels have grown quickly. Oil (mainly for transport) has stayed at roughly 35–40%.

What factors affect which energy a country uses? Climate, wealth, natural resources, government policies, population and technology.

Success criterion 4

Which is best for which country?

The best energy mix depends on a country’s physical geography (sunshine, wind, rivers, volcanoes, fossil fuel reserves), its wealth and technology, and its government policies on climate change and energy security.

  • Iceland — almost all electricity from hydro and geothermal.
  • UK — coal power ended in 2024; wind now one of the largest sources of electricity.
  • Norway — mostly hydroelectric power from mountain rivers.
  • Remote rural communities in LICs — small-scale solar or micro-hydro (see Lesson 10).

Key terms

Renewable energy
Energy from sources that are naturally replaced, e.g. wind, solar.
Non-renewable energy
Energy from finite sources that will run out, e.g. coal.
Fossil fuel
Coal, oil or gas formed from ancient plants and animals.
Intermittent
Not available all of the time, e.g. wind and solar.
Energy mix
The combination of energy sources a country uses.
Energy security
Having reliable, affordable access to enough energy.

Exam-style question

Assess the advantages and disadvantages of wind power as an energy source. (6 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Up to 2 marks for advantages, e.g. renewable; no greenhouse gas emissions when operating; low running costs; can be built offshore.
  • Up to 2 marks for disadvantages, e.g. unreliable/intermittent; visual and noise pollution; high initial cost; can harm birds.
  • Up to 2 marks for a judgement, e.g. valuable as part of a diverse energy mix with storage or backup.

Model answer: Wind power is renewable and produces no greenhouse gases when operating, and once built its running costs are low. However, it is intermittent because it only generates electricity when the wind blows, and some people object to the noise and visual impact of turbines. Overall, wind power is an important part of a sustainable energy mix, but it needs to be combined with storage or other sources to provide a reliable supply.

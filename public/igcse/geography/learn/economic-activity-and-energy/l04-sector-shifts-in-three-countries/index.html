4. Sector shifts in three countries

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Economic Activity and Energy · Changing employment · Lesson 4 of 10

Syllabus Statement

The growth and decline of different economic sectors has resulted in a range of impacts and possible resource issues.

Positive and negative impacts of economic sector shifts in a named developed country (UK), a named emerging country (China) and a developing country (Ethiopia).

Skills: use numerical economic data to profile a chosen country.

Ethiopia – a pre-industrial country

  • One of the least developed countries in the world. Around three-quarters of jobs are in the primary sector, mainly subsistence farming, but farming produces under half of GDP because much of it is not sold.
  • Primary changes: growth of commercial crops for export — coffee, oil seeds and cut flowers — and of gold mining, which earns foreign currency.
  • Secondary changes: food processing, textiles and leather goods are growing; the government is investing in roads and energy; cheap labour attracts TNCs.
  • Tertiary changes: still small, but growing in Addis Ababa, where the African Union and the UN Economic Commission for Africa have their headquarters. Over three-quarters of people still live in rural areas, but rural–urban migration is increasing.

China – a rapidly emerging country

  • Now one of the world’s two largest economies, but still a middle-income country per person.
  • A government-led shift from the primary to the secondary sector, based on cheap labour and energy, made China the “workshop of the world”.
  • Farming still employs many people but its share of GDP is shrinking, and a widening gap has opened between rural and urban living standards.
  • Cities such as Shanghai and Beijing have modern architecture and a growing middle class, but heavy industry and traffic cause serious air pollution.

The UK – a post-industrial country

  • The world’s first industrial nation. About 50 years ago manufacturing produced roughly 40% of the UK’s wealth; today it is under 20%.
  • Deindustrialisation — a global shift of manufacturing to China, India and other lower-cost countries — closed many factories, e.g. the pottery industry of Stoke-on-Trent (the Potteries).
  • Services now provide around 80% of jobs; the quaternary sector may be 10–15% of the economy.
  • Farming employs about 1% but produces much of the UK’s food thanks to high mechanisation.

Task: for each country, describe one change to its employment sectors and explain what it does or might lead to. Example: Ethiopia — more commercial crops such as oil seeds → more export income, but more land is needed, which may push out subsistence farmers and cause food shortages.

Discuss: has the deindustrialisation of the UK all been bad news?

Key terms

Pre-industrial
A stage when most people work in farming.
Industrial
A stage when manufacturing employs many people.
Post-industrial
A stage when most people work in services.
Industrialisation
The growth of manufacturing.
Regional inequality
Differences in wealth and opportunity between regions.
Exam-style question

Describe the differences in employment structure between a pre-industrial and a post-industrial country. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: pre-industrial countries have most workers in the primary sector (e.g. over 60%).
  • 1 mark: they have small secondary and tertiary sectors.
  • 1 mark: post-industrial countries have most workers in the tertiary sector (e.g. over 75%) and a growing quaternary sector.
  • 1 mark: very small primary sector (e.g. under 2%); use of data/example, e.g. Chad vs UK.

Model answer: In a pre-industrial country such as Chad, most people work in the primary sector, often more than 60% in farming, and the secondary and tertiary sectors are small. In a post-industrial country such as the UK, more than 80% work in the tertiary sector, with a growing quaternary sector, while fewer than 2% work in the primary sector.

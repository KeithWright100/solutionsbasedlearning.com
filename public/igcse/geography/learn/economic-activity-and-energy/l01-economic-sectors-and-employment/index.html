1. Economic sectors and employment

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Economic Activity and Energy · Economic sectors and location · Lesson 1 of 10

Learning Intention

To describe and explain human patterns and processes.

Syllabus Statement

The relative importance of different economic sectors; classification of employment by economic sector (primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary) and the reasons for the differences in employment structures in countries at different levels of development (Clark–Fisher model).

Skills: draw and interpret triangular graphs to show the proportion of people employed in the primary, secondary and tertiary/quaternary sectors.

Success criterion 1

The four economic sectors

SectorWhat it involvesExamples
PrimaryExtracting raw materials from the land or seaFarming, forestry, fishing, mining
SecondaryProcessing raw materials and manufacturing finished goodsFood processing, manufacturing, assembling, building
TertiaryProviding services to people and businessesTransport, retail, medicine, catering, teaching
QuaternaryProviding specialist information, research and expertiseResearch, design engineering, computer programming, financial management

The sectors are linked: primary products feed manufacturing, goods are delivered and sold through services, and quaternary research improves every sector.

Success criterion 2

Triangular graphs

A triangular graph shows three percentages that add up to 100%. Each side is a scale from 0 to 100 for one sector. To read a point, follow the grid lines parallel to each side to the scale. To plot a country, find its value on two scales and the lines will meet at the point; check the third value adds up to 100%.

Example: Japan — about 3% primary, 24% secondary and 73% tertiary — plots near the bottom-left (services) corner.

Success criterion 3

Employment structure and development: the Clark–Fisher model

  • Pre-industrial (e.g. Ethiopia today, the UK in 1800) — most people work in the primary sector, often subsistence farming; few jobs in industry or services.
  • Industrial (e.g. China) — farming is mechanised so fewer farm workers are needed; people move to cities to work in factories; the secondary sector peaks.
  • Post-industrial (e.g. the UK, Japan) — manufacturing moves abroad or is automated; most people work in tertiary services; the quaternary sector grows.

So a high percentage of primary workers usually indicates a low-income country, and a high percentage of tertiary and quaternary workers indicates a high-income country.

Key terms

Primary sector
Extracting raw materials, e.g. farming, mining.
Secondary sector
Manufacturing and processing goods.
Tertiary sector
Providing services.
Quaternary sector
Research, information and specialist expertise.
Employment structure
The percentage of workers in each economic sector.
Clark–Fisher model
A model showing how employment structure changes as a country develops.

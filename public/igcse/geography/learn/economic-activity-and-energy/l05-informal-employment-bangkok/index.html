5. Informal employment

Success criterion 1

What is informal employment?

Informal employment is work that is not officially registered, taxed or protected by employment laws, so workers have no contract, sick pay, pension or job security.

  • Examples: street food and market vendors, motorbike-taxi drivers, tuk-tuk drivers, shoe shiners, domestic workers, construction day labourers, rubbish pickers and recyclers, home-based garment workers.
  • Typical characteristics: easy to start with little money or skills; small-scale and family-run; long hours; irregular, often low income; no taxes paid; no health and safety protection.

  • Income often unreported

  • Hired on the spot with no interview
  • Works within a legal framework
  • Pays income tax
  • Written contract and records
  • Access to healthcare and unemployment benefits
  • Counted fully in GDP

Success criterion 2

The global pattern of informal employment

An estimated global map of informal employment (as a % of non-farm jobs) shows:

  • Highest (over 70%) in parts of sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Mali, Uganda, Tanzania, Zambia, Madagascar) and South Asia (e.g. India, Pakistan, Bangladesh).
  • High (25–70%) across much of Central and South America and South-East Asia.
  • Lower (10–25%) in parts of Eastern Europe, Central Asia, Turkey and South Africa.
  • Many countries — including most HICs, China and Russia — show “data not available”. Informal work is by definition unrecorded and hard to survey; some governments do not collect or publish the data; in HICs the informal sector is small and not measured in the same way.
Success criterion 2

Why is informal employment growing?

  • Rural–urban migration — large numbers of migrants arrive in cities faster than formal jobs are created.
  • Economic development — cities grow and create demand for cheap services (food, transport, cleaning), but formal jobs need qualifications many migrants do not have.
  • Lack of education and skills, and few welfare benefits, so people must find any work to survive.
  • Flexibility — informal work can fit around family or seasonal farm work.
Success criterion 3

Advantages and disadvantages

Activity: advantage or disadvantage?

Decide whether each point is an advantage or disadvantage of informal employment.

  • Easy to start with little money or qualifications
  • Provides cheap goods and services for city residents
  • Absorbs workers where unemployment is high
  • Income for people who cannot find formal jobs
  • Close-knit community support
  • Close relationship with the employer makes time off easy to arrange
  • Hired on the spot without long interviews or tests
  • No contract, sick pay, pension or health insurance
  • Low, irregular income makes saving difficult
  • Unprotected by labour laws, so workers can be exploited
  • Unpredictable working hours
  • A short illness or injury can mean no income or losing the job
  • No taxes paid, so less money for public services
  • Unsafe conditions and risk of police fines or eviction

Success criterion 4

The informal sector in Bangkok

  • Bangkok is Thailand’s megacity, with around 10 million people in the metropolitan region. Many of its workers are migrants from the North-East (Isan).
  • Over half of Thailand’s workforce is in informal employment, including many street vendors, motorbike-taxi riders and domestic and construction workers in Bangkok.
  • Street food is famous and supports tourism, but the city authorities have at times cleared vendors from pavements in areas such as Sukhumvit and Siam to reduce congestion, damaging vendors’ incomes.
  • Motorbike taxis must now register and wear numbered vests, which brings some formal recognition.
  • The government has encouraged informal workers to join social security schemes (e.g. Section 40 of the Social Security Act) to gain some protection.

Skills: use photographs and newspaper articles about Bangkok’s vendors to describe what you see and explain the issues.

Key terms

Informal employment
Work that is not registered, taxed or protected by law.
Formal employment
Registered work with a contract, taxes and legal protection.
Megacity
A city with over 10 million people.
Street vendor
A person selling goods or food from a stall or cart in the street.

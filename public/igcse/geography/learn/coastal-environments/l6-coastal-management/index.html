Pressures and conflicts on the coast

Coasts are some of the most crowded and valuable places on Earth. Many different groups want to use them:

  • Residents want their homes protected from erosion and flooding.
  • Tourism businesses want attractive beaches and good access.
  • Farmers want to protect farmland.
  • Industry and ports need flat land and deep water.
  • Conservationists want to protect ecosystems such as salt marshes, dunes and reefs.

These interests often conflict. Protecting one stretch of coast can stop sediment reaching another, increasing erosion further along. Climate change adds to the pressure: sea level rise and more intense storms increase the risk of flooding and erosion.

Hard and soft engineering

StrategyHow it worksAdvantagesDisadvantages
Sea wall (hard)Concrete wall reflects wave energyVery effective; protects towns; promenades for touristsVery expensive (several thousand £ per metre); can erode the beach in front
Groynes (hard)Wooden or rock barriers trap longshore driftBuild a wider beach that absorbs wave energyStarve beaches further down the coast of sediment
Rock armour (hard)Large boulders absorb wave energyCheaper than sea walls; quick to buildLook unnatural; can be dangerous to climb
Beach nourishment (soft)Sand is added to the beachLooks natural; helps tourismNeeds repeating regularly, so costs add up
Dune regeneration (soft)Planting marram grass and fencing off dunesCheap; protects habitatsRestricts access; takes time
Managed retreat (soft)Allowing low-value land to flood, creating salt marshCheap; new habitats absorb wave energyFarmers and landowners lose land

Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM) plans a whole stretch of coast together, so protecting one place does not cause problems elsewhere. In the UK, Shoreline Management Plans decide whether to hold the line, advance the line, carry out managed realignment or take no active intervention.

Key terms

Hard engineering
Building artificial structures to control natural processes.
Soft engineering
Working with natural processes to manage the coast.
Managed retreat
Allowing an area to flood or erode in a controlled way.
ICZM
Integrated Coastal Zone Management: planning a whole coastline together.
Sea level rise
The increase in average sea level, caused by thermal expansion and melting ice.
Case study

The Holderness coast, Yorkshire

Holderness is one of the fastest-eroding coastlines in Europe, retreating by an average of about 1.8 m a year, because its cliffs are made of soft boulder clay and it faces powerful North Sea waves with a long fetch.

  • Mappleton: in 1991 about £2 million was spent on two rock groynes and rock armour to protect the village and its road. The beach widened and Mappleton was protected.
  • Knock-on effect: the groynes trapped sediment, so the coast to the south, around Cowden, was starved of sand and erosion increased there. Farmers lost land.
  • Hornsea and Withernsea are protected by sea walls and groynes, but many isolated farms and caravan parks are not protected, as the cost is greater than the value of the land.
  • The sediment also feeds Spurn Head, so protecting Holderness could affect the spit further south.

This shows the conflict at the coast: protecting one place can increase erosion somewhere else.

Exam practice

Evaluate the effectiveness of hard engineering strategies in managing coastal erosion. Use a named example. (8 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Explain how one or more hard engineering strategies reduce erosion (e.g. groynes trap longshore drift, rock armour absorbs wave energy).
  • Use specific evidence from a named coast, e.g. Mappleton on the Holderness coast: £2 million rock groynes in 1991 protected the village.
  • Consider negative impacts: sediment starvation and increased erosion downdrift at Cowden; high cost; visual impact.
  • Compare with soft engineering or managed retreat where relevant.
  • Reach a supported judgement, e.g. effective locally but not sustainable for the whole coastline.

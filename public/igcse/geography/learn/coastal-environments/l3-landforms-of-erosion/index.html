Landforms of coastal erosion

Geology and the shape of the coast

The shape of the coastline depends on its geology (rock type and structure).

  • Discordant coast — bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast. The soft rock erodes faster to form bays; the hard rock is left sticking out as headlands. Example: Swanage Bay in Dorset, between the chalk of Ballard Point and the limestone of Durlston Head.
  • Concordant coast — the rock bands run parallel to the coast. A hard outer band protects the softer rock behind it. If the hard rock is breached, the sea erodes the soft rock behind to form a cove. Example: Lulworth Cove in Dorset.

Headlands and bays

On a discordant coast, the soft rock (e.g. clay) is eroded quickly by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a bay. The hard rock (e.g. chalk) erodes more slowly and is left as a headland.

Once formed, the headland sticks out into the sea and receives more wave energy, so it is attacked by erosion. The bay is now sheltered, so waves lose energy and deposit sand, forming a beach. Over time this can make the coast straighter again.

Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

  1. Waves erode the base of a cliff between high and low tide by hydraulic action and abrasion, creating a wave-cut notch.
  2. The notch gets deeper until the cliff above is unsupported and collapses (mass movement).
  3. The cliff retreats inland. Repeated many times, this leaves a gently sloping, rocky wave-cut platform that is exposed at low tide.

Caves, arches, stacks and stumps

Headlands are often eroded into a sequence of landforms:

  1. Hydraulic action and abrasion widen a crack or weakness in the headland.
  2. The crack grows into a cave.
  3. The cave is eroded right through the headland to form an arch (e.g. Durdle Door, Dorset).
  4. The arch is widened until its roof is unsupported and collapses, leaving a stack (e.g. Old Harry Rocks).
  5. The stack is eroded at its base and collapses into a low stump, often covered at high tide.

Key terms

Discordant coast
A coast where rock bands run at right angles to the sea, forming headlands and bays.
Concordant coast
A coast where rock bands run parallel to the sea.
Wave-cut notch
An indent at the base of a cliff eroded between high and low tide.
Wave-cut platform
A flat rocky area left behind as a cliff retreats.
Stack
An isolated pillar of rock left when an arch collapses.
Case study

The Dorset coast (Jurassic Coast)

The Dorset coast is a World Heritage Site with a remarkable range of erosional landforms in a short distance:

  • Swanage Bay — a discordant coast. Soft clays and sands have been eroded into the bay between two headlands of more resistant rock.
  • Old Harry Rocks — chalk stacks and a stump at the end of the Handfast Point headland.
  • Durdle Door — a limestone arch.
  • Lulworth Cove — a concordant coast where the sea broke through a band of resistant limestone and eroded the softer clays behind into an almost circular cove.

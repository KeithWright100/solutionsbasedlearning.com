15. Coastal management case study: the Holderness coast, UK

Success criterion 1

Location

The Holderness coast is in the East Riding of Yorkshire, north-east England. It runs for about 61 km from Flamborough Head in the north to Spurn Head at the mouth of the Humber Estuary in the south, facing the North Sea. Settlements along it include Bridlington, Hornsea, Mappleton and Withernsea.

Success criterion 2

Physical geography

  • Lithology — most of the cliffs are made of soft boulder clay (till), deposited by glaciers at the end of the last ice age. It is easily eroded and prone to slumping when wet. In the north, chalk forms the resistant headland of Flamborough Head.
  • Climate — a cool temperate climate; winter storms and heavy rain saturate the clay cliffs, causing slumping.
  • Coastal conditions — powerful waves from the north-east have a long fetch across the North Sea; beaches are narrow because the eroded clay is mostly fine material carried away in suspension, giving the cliffs little protection; longshore drift moves sediment south towards Spurn Head.

As a result, Holderness is one of the fastest-eroding coastlines in Europe, retreating by an average of about 1.8 m a year, and more in places after storms. Around 29 villages have been lost to the sea since Roman times.

Success criterion 3

Why is management needed?

  • Towns and villages (Hornsea, Mappleton, Withernsea) and roads such as the B1242 are at risk.
  • Farmland is lost every year; farmers lose their livelihood.
  • Important infrastructure — the Easington gas terminal handles a significant share of the UK’s gas supply.
  • Caravan parks and tourism depend on the cliff tops.
  • Spurn Head and its habitats depend on sediment eroded from the cliffs, so protecting the cliffs affects the spit.
Success criterion 4

Decisions made

PlaceManagement
HornseaSea wall, groynes and rock armour protect the town and its seafront.
MappletonIn 1991, about £2 million was spent on two rock groynes and rock revetment to protect the village and the B1242 road.
WithernseaSea wall, groynes and rock armour.
EasingtonRock revetment to protect the gas terminal.
Much of the restNo active intervention: isolated farms and caravan parks are not protected because the cost would be greater than the value of the land.

Your decision: with a limited budget, which places would you protect, and why?

Success criterion 5

How effective? Views of different stakeholders

StakeholderView
Mappleton residentsPositive: the village and road have been protected and the beach has widened.
Farmers at Cowden (south of Mappleton)Negative: the groynes trapped sediment, so less reached Cowden and erosion increased there; farmland and homes were lost.
Caravan park ownersNegative where unprotected: land and pitches are lost each year.
Gas companies / national governmentPositive: protecting Easington secures energy supplies.
ConservationistsMixed: defences reduce the sediment that feeds Spurn Head and its habitats; natural processes are interrupted.
Local councilMust balance limited money; defences protect towns but cannot protect the whole coast.

Overall: the schemes are effective locally, but they move the problem further down the coast. The Shoreline Management Plan therefore holds the line at the towns and allows the rest to erode naturally.

Key terms

Boulder clay (till)
Soft material deposited by glaciers, easily eroded.
Lithology
The type and characteristics of a rock.
Terminal groyne effect
Increased erosion just beyond the last groyne, because sediment is trapped.
No active intervention
A policy of allowing natural processes to continue.
Stakeholder
A person or group with an interest in how an area is managed.

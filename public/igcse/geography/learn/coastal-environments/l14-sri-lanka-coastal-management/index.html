Success criterion 1

Hard and soft engineering in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka uses a mix of strategies along its coastline:

  • Hard engineering — rock revetments and riprap, groynes and offshore breakwaters protect key stretches of the busy west and south-west coasts, including the coastal road and railway, towns and tourist resorts.
  • Soft engineering — beach nourishment at important tourist beaches; replanting mangroves and protecting coral reefs and sand dunes as natural barriers (especially after the 2004 tsunami showed that areas behind healthy mangroves and dunes often suffered less damage).
  • Planning — setback areas, where building close to the shoreline is restricted, and permits for any development in the coastal zone.
Success criterion 2

The CZMP: Sri Lanka's Coastal Zone Management Plan

  • The Coast Conservation Act of 1981 created a government department responsible for managing the coastal zone (today the Coast Conservation and Coastal Resource Management Department).
  • The first Coastal Zone Management Plan was published in 1990 and has been revised several times since, most recently as the Coastal Zone and Coastal Resource Management Plan (2018).
  • Key aims: reduce coastal erosion; protect habitats such as coral reefs, mangroves, lagoons and estuaries; control development through permits and setback areas; reduce pollution; and protect sites of cultural and scenic value.
  • Special Area Management (SAM) — at selected sites, local communities, government and other stakeholders work together to manage resources such as lagoons and reefs. This links to the idea of Integrated Coastal Zone Management.

Check these details against the CZMP notes you are given in class.

Success criterion 3

Why does Sri Lanka need coastal management?

  • A coastline of roughly 1,600 km, with a large share of the population, most tourist hotels, the main fishing harbours and much industry located in the coastal zone, especially around Colombo.
  • Coastal erosion — worsened by coral mining, sand mining from beaches and rivers, and poorly planned structures that interrupt longshore drift.
  • Hazards — the 2004 tsunami killed around 35,000 people in Sri Lanka; storm surges, monsoon storms and rising sea levels add to the risk.
  • Valuable ecosystems — coral reefs (e.g. Hikkaduwa, Pigeon Island, the Bar Reef), mangroves and lagoons support fishing and tourism but are under threat.
  • Competing users — tourism, fishing, ports, industry and housing all compete for the same narrow strip of land (Lesson 11).

The CZMP concept is to manage all of these issues together, rather than protecting one place at a time.

Success criterion 4

Critical thinking

Consider these questions and note your views:

  • Is it fair to stop fishing families building near the beach to protect hotels or ecosystems?
  • Should Sri Lanka spend more on hard defences, or on protecting mangroves and reefs?
  • How can laws such as the ban on coral mining be enforced when people depend on it for income?
  • How does Sri Lanka’s management compare with Holderness in the UK (Lesson 15)?

Key terms

CZMP
Coastal Zone Management Plan: Sri Lanka’s national plan for managing its coast.
Setback area
A zone near the shoreline where development is restricted.
Special Area Management
Community-based management of resources at selected coastal sites.
ICZM
Integrated Coastal Zone Management: managing the whole coast together.
Coral mining
Removing coral, e.g. to make lime for building.

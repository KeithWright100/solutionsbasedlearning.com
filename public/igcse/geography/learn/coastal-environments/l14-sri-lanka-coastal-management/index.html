Success criterion 3

Sri Lanka: background

Sri Lanka is a tropical island country in the Indian Ocean, just south-east of India, between about 6°N and 10°N. It has a coastline of roughly 1,600 km and a population of about 22 million. The capital and largest city, Colombo, is on the west coast.

  • An emerging economy — Sri Lanka is a lower-middle-income country. Its economy grew quickly after the civil war ended in 2009, based on clothing exports, tea, tourism and money sent home by workers abroad, though it suffered a severe economic crisis in 2022.
  • Income and life expectancy — life expectancy is high (around 77 years) for a country with its level of income, reflecting good basic health care and education. On Gapminder it sits above most countries with a similar income.
  • Climate — two monsoons bring heavy rain: the south-west monsoon (May–September) and the north-east monsoon (December–February).

Activity: write a good description of Sri Lanka using the PPT and the information above. Then use Gapminder to find another graph (for example GDP per person over time) that shows why Sri Lanka is an emerging economy.

Success criterion 3

Hazards and sea level rise

Sri Lanka faces several natural hazards. In disaster records, floods (mostly caused by monsoon rains) are by far the most common, followed by landslides, droughts, cyclones and storms. The rarest but deadliest was the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed around 35,000 people in Sri Lanka.

For coastal management we need to know where flooding happens, how often, who and what is exposed (homes, roads, hotels, harbours), and how the risk will change in future.

  • Sea level rise — as the climate warms, sea level is rising. Low-lying land around lagoons, estuaries and flat coastal plains is most at risk of permanent flooding and saltwater getting into farmland and wells.
  • Areas at risk — on sea level rise maps, the darker red areas are the most at risk. These are typically the flat Jaffna peninsula in the north, the lagoons of the east coast (e.g. around Batticaloa), the north-west around Puttalam, and the densely built-up west coast around Colombo and Negombo.

Activity: using the maps in class, identify the locations most at risk of coastal flooding, state the level of risk from the key, and give reasons why people still live there (jobs in fishing and tourism, flat land for farming and building, ports, transport links).

Success criterion 3

Where do people live?

Sri Lanka’s population is unevenly distributed. Use GCSE A to describe it:

  • General comment — the population is concentrated in the west and south-west, especially along the coast.
  • Compass points, Specific Examples and data — e.g. the Western Province around Colombo has the highest population density (use the figure from the key); the south-west coast towards Galle is also densely populated; the dry zone of the north-central and east is much less densely populated.
  • Anomaly — the Jaffna peninsula in the far north is densely populated even though the surrounding area is not.

This matters because the most crowded areas are also on the coast, so many people, homes and jobs are exposed to coastal hazards.

Success criterion 3

Why is there a need for coastal management?

Activity: social, economic or environmental?

Sort each reason for coastal management. Click a category; it turns green if correct.

  • The 2004 tsunami killed around 35,000 people in Sri Lanka and left many families homeless.
  • Fishing families often live in low-lying homes right next to the beach, at risk from flooding and erosion.
  • Untreated sewage from coastal towns pollutes inshore waters, a risk to people’s health.
  • A large share of the population lives in the coastal zone, especially around Colombo.
  • Beach hotels on the west and south coasts earn tourist income and jobs.
  • Fishing harbours and the fishing industry depend on a stable, healthy coast.
  • The coastal road and railway between Colombo and Galle are threatened by erosion.
  • Colombo port and much of the country’s industry are located on the coast.
  • Coral reefs are degraded by pollution, coral mining, unsustainable fishing and diving.
  • Mangroves have been cut down to make room for tourism and urban development.
  • Sand mining from beaches and rivers reduces the sediment that keeps beaches stable.
  • Deforestation inland increases surface runoff, so the risk of flooding rises.

Model paragraph: the need for coastal management

There is a severe need for coastal management in Sri Lanka because it is an island country, so a lot of the land is vulnerable to coastal erosion and flooding. Sri Lanka is prone to storm surges and monsoon rains, which increase the risk of coastal flooding and erosion. This will worsen over the next century with sea level rise. It is also being made worse by deforestation: with less vegetation, surface runoff increases and so does the risk of flooding.

Sri Lanka suffered a lot of damage from the 2004 tsunami. Areas that still had mangroves suffered less, as the mangrove forests absorbed a lot of the energy. However, many mangroves have been cut down to make room for tourism and urban development, which reduces the protection from the sea.

Water quality has also fallen because untreated sewage is discharged into inshore waters from coastal towns, cities and tourist developments. Coral reefs have been degraded by pollution, the ‘mining’ of coral and sand for building materials, and unsustainable fishing and recreational diving.

Highlight the social, economic and environmental reasons in three different colours.

Success criterion 1

Hard and soft engineering in Sri Lanka

Activity: match the technique

Choose the coastal management technique that matches each description, then check your answers.

  • A concrete wall along the seafront that reflects wave energy back out to sea.
  • Large boulders piled up in front of a cliff or seafront to absorb wave energy.
  • Wire cages filled with rocks, placed at the foot of a cliff or the back of a beach.
  • Barriers of wood or rock built out into the sea to trap sediment moved by longshore drift.
  • Adding sand or shingle to a beach to make it wider and higher.
  • Moving sediment from the lower beach back up to the upper beach after storms.
  • Planting marram grass and fencing off dunes so that they can grow.
  • Allowing an area of low-value land to flood or erode, creating new salt marsh or wetland.
  • Draining, terracing or pinning a cliff to reduce slumping and mass movement.

Sri Lanka uses a mix of these strategies:

  • Hard engineering — rock revetments (rock armour), groynes and offshore breakwaters protect key stretches of the busy west and south-west coasts, including the coastal road and railway, towns, harbours and tourist resorts.
  • Soft engineering — beach nourishment at important beaches; replanting mangroves; protecting coral reefs, salt marshes and sand dunes as natural barriers.
  • Planning — setback areas, where building close to the shoreline is restricted, and permits for any development in the coastal zone.
Success criterion 2

The CZMP: Sri Lanka's Coastal Zone Management Plan

  • The Coast Conservation Act No. 57 of 1981 (in force from 1983) made the Coast Conservation Department responsible for the coastal zone. Any development there needs a permit.
  • The coastal zone is defined as the land up to 300 m inland from the high water line and the sea up to 2 km offshore from the low water line.
  • The first Coastal Zone Management Plan (CZMP) was published in 1990 and revised in 1997, 2004 (after the tsunami) and 2018.
  • The 2018 plan has six objectives:
    1. tackle coastal erosion with a mix of hard and soft measures;
    2. make development fit with conserving the natural shoreline;
    3. make beaches more stable by controlling sand mining and ending coral mining;
    4. prepare for the impacts of climate change;
    5. build coastal communities’ resilience to erosion and extreme hazards;
    6. conserve salt marshes for their ecological and economic value.
  • Setback areas — the plan sets how far back from the sea houses, businesses and hotels must be built, varying from place to place.
  • Special Area Management (SAM) — at selected sites, local communities, government and other groups manage resources such as lagoons and reefs together. This is an example of Integrated Coastal Zone Management.

Activity: for each paragraph of the CZMP document, highlight up to 10 words, give the paragraph a title and summarise its main points. Then write at least 10 lines on the need for the plan and at least 10 lines summarising the plan itself.

Key terms

Emerging economy
A country whose economy is growing quickly and becoming more industrial.
CZMP
Coastal Zone Management Plan: Sri Lanka’s national plan for managing its coast.
Coastal zone
In Sri Lanka, land up to 300 m inland and sea up to 2 km offshore.
Setback area
A zone near the shoreline where development is restricted.
Special Area Management
Community-based management of resources at selected coastal sites.
ICZM
Integrated Coastal Zone Management: managing the whole coast together.
Coral mining
Removing coral, e.g. to burn for lime used in building.

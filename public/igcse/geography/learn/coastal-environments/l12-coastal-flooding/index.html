12. Coastal flooding: causes, prediction and prevention

Success criterion 1

Causes of coastal flooding

  • Storm surges — in a deep low-pressure system or tropical cyclone, low air pressure lets the sea surface rise, and strong onshore winds pile water up against the coast. If this coincides with high tide, the sea can rise several metres above normal. Examples: the 1953 North Sea flood, which killed over 2,000 people in the Netherlands and eastern England; Cyclone Nargis, Myanmar, 2008.
  • Tsunamis — giant waves caused by undersea earthquakes, volcanic eruptions or landslides that displace a huge volume of water. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami killed around 230,000 people in 14 countries, including about 35,000 in Sri Lanka.
  • Climate change — rising sea levels (from thermal expansion and melting ice) mean surges reach further inland, and warmer oceans may make tropical storms more intense.

The risk is greatest where coasts are low-lying, densely populated and poorly protected, e.g. Bangladesh.

Success criterion 2

Predicting and preventing flooding

StrategyHow it worksExamples
Forecasting and warningsSatellites, weather models and tide gauges predict storm surges days ahead; seismometers and ocean buoys detect tsunamis; warnings sent by phone, sirens, TV and radio.Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System (set up after 2004); UK Flood Forecasting Centre.
Building designRaising buildings on stilts or platforms; flood-resistant materials; electrics placed high up; cyclone and tsunami shelters on raised ground.Raised cyclone shelters in Bangladesh; stilt houses in South-East Asia.
PlanningLand-use zoning that keeps homes and key services out of the highest-risk areas; buffer zones along the coast; protecting mangroves and dunes as natural barriers.Coastal buffer zone in Sri Lanka after the 2004 tsunami.
EducationTeaching people warning signs (e.g. the sea suddenly retreating before a tsunami), evacuation routes and drills; community flood plans.School drills and evacuation signs in coastal Japan and Indonesia.
Hard defencesSea walls, embankments and barriers hold back the water.Thames Barrier, London (opened 1982).
Success criterion 3

Making decisions: which strategies matter most?

Rank the strategies for one place, such as a low-income coastal village in Bangladesh or a wealthy coastal city. Consider:

  • Cost — can the country or community afford it?
  • Lives versus property — warnings and education save lives cheaply; barriers protect property but cost a great deal.
  • Speed — a tsunami may arrive within minutes, so education and instant warnings matter most.
  • Sustainability — will it still work as sea levels rise?

Key terms

Storm surge
A rise in sea level caused by low pressure and strong winds pushing water onshore.
Tsunami
A series of giant waves caused by the sudden displacement of sea water.
Forecasting
Predicting events such as storms using scientific data.
Buffer zone
A strip of land along the coast kept free of development.
Evacuation
Moving people away from danger to a safe place.

4. Geology, vegetation, people and sea level rise

Success criterion 1

What is geology?

Geology is the study of rocks: their type (lithology), their structure, and how they were formed. On the coast, geology controls how quickly the land is eroded and what landforms develop.

Success criterion 2

Rock type and structure

  • Rock hardness — hard, resistant rocks such as granite and limestone erode slowly, forming steep cliffs and headlands. Soft rocks such as clay and sand erode quickly, forming bays and gentle slopes. The Holderness coast, made of soft boulder clay, erodes at about 1.8 m a year; granite cliffs may retreat only a few millimetres a year.
  • Joints and faults — cracks and lines of weakness let waves erode by hydraulic action, forming caves, arches and stacks.
  • Dip (angle of the rock layers) — layers dipping towards the sea can slide along bedding planes; layers dipping inland form steeper, more stable cliffs.
  • Arrangement of rock bands — produces concordant or discordant coasts (Lesson 5).
Success criterion 3

Vegetation

Vegetation can both protect and weaken the coast:

  • Protects — roots bind soil and sand together, reducing erosion and slumping. Marram grass stabilises sand dunes; mangroves and salt-marsh plants trap sediment and absorb wave energy. Plant cover also intercepts rain, so less water soaks into cliffs.
  • Weakens — roots grow into cracks and widen them (biological weathering), and decaying plants release acids that help chemical weathering.

Removing vegetation — for example clearing mangroves or trampling dunes — usually increases erosion.

Success criterion 4

The impact of people

As the number of people living on and visiting coasts grows, the pressure on the natural coastline increases:

  • Building houses, hotels and roads on cliff tops adds weight and increases run-off, making mass movement more likely.
  • Tourism — trampling damages dunes; boats and divers damage coral reefs.
  • Coastal defences protect one place but can starve beaches further along of sediment.
  • Removing sand, gravel or coral for building reduces natural protection.
  • Clearing mangroves for shrimp farms, agriculture or development removes a natural barrier.
Success criterion 5

Changes in sea level

Sea level changes in two main ways: eustatic change (the volume of water in the oceans changes, e.g. ice melting or water expanding as it warms) and isostatic change (the land itself rises or sinks).

Global sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1900 and 2018, and the rate is increasing. A rise in sea level means:

  • more frequent and severe coastal flooding, especially in low-lying areas such as Bangladesh, the Netherlands and small island nations like the Maldives;
  • waves reach further up the coast, increasing erosion of cliffs and beaches;
  • salt water enters farmland and fresh water supplies;
  • coastal ecosystems such as salt marshes and mangroves may be “squeezed” and lost.

Where sea level has fallen (or the land has risen), old beaches and cliffs can be left above the reach of the waves as raised beaches.

Key terms

Geology
The study of rocks: their type, structure and formation.
Lithology
The type and characteristics of a rock.
Eustatic change
A global change in sea level caused by a change in the volume of ocean water.
Isostatic change
A local change in sea level caused by the land rising or sinking.
Raised beach
An old beach left above the current sea level.

13. Coastal management strategies: hard and soft engineering

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Coastal Environments · 2.3 People and coastal management · Lesson 13 of 15

Learning Intention

To describe and explain the interactions of human and physical geography

Syllabus Statement

Coastal environments are of great importance to people and need to be sustainably managed

There are different coastal management strategies, including soft engineering (beach replenishment, cliff regrading, ecosystem rehabilitation and revegetation, managed retreat), hard engineering (groynes, revetments, sea walls, gabions, riprap) and shoreline management plans.

Success criterion 1

Hard and soft engineering

Hard engineering

Building artificial structures to control or resist natural processes. Usually expensive, effective in the short term and visually intrusive.

Soft engineering

Working with natural processes to reduce erosion and flooding. Usually cheaper, more sustainable and better for the environment, but may protect less.

Success criterion 2

How the schemes work

SchemeTypeHow it works
Sea wallHardA concrete wall, often curved, that reflects wave energy back out to sea.
GroynesHardWooden or rock barriers built out to sea that trap sediment moving by longshore drift, building a wider beach.
RevetmentsHardSloping wooden, concrete or rock structures at the back of the beach that break up and absorb wave energy.
GabionsHardWire cages filled with rocks, stacked at the base of a cliff to absorb wave energy.
Riprap (rock armour)HardLarge boulders piled at the foot of a cliff or in front of a sea wall to absorb wave energy.
Beach replenishmentSoftSand or shingle dredged from offshore is added to the beach, making it wider so it absorbs more wave energy.
Cliff regradingSoftThe cliff is reshaped to a gentler slope and drained, reducing mass movement.
Ecosystem rehabilitation and revegetationSoftReplanting mangroves, marram grass on dunes or salt-marsh plants so natural ecosystems absorb wave energy and trap sediment.
Managed retreatSoftAllowing low-value land to flood or erode, often creating new salt marsh that acts as a natural defence.

Shoreline Management Plans (SMPs) divide the coast into sections and choose one of four policies for each: hold the line (keep defences where they are), advance the line (build defences further out to sea), managed realignment (allow the shoreline to move in a controlled way) or no active intervention (do nothing).

SchemeAdvantagesDisadvantagesRelative cost
Sea wallVery effective; protects towns; often has a promenadeExpensive; reflected waves can scour the beach in front; uglyVery high
GroynesWider beach for tourists; reduces erosion locallyStarve beaches further along the coast of sedimentMedium
RevetmentsCheaper than sea walls; absorb energyWooden ones need regular repair; restrict beach accessMedium
GabionsCheap and quick to buildWire rusts; look unattractive; short lifespanLow
RiprapEffective and relatively cheapRocks are not local and look out of place; dangerous to climbMedium
Beach replenishmentNatural appearance; benefits tourismMust be repeated; dredging can damage sea-bed habitatsMedium, ongoing
Cliff regradingReduces landslidesCliff top land is lost; does not stop wave erosion at the baseLow–medium

When you evaluate, consider: effectiveness, cost, impact on other places, the environment, and who benefits or loses.

Key terms

Hard engineering
Building artificial structures to control natural processes.
Soft engineering
Working with natural processes to manage the coast.
Gabion
A wire cage filled with rocks used to absorb wave energy.
Riprap
Large boulders placed to absorb wave energy (rock armour).
Shoreline Management Plan
A plan setting the policy for each section of coast: hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment or no active intervention.

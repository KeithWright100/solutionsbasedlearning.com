7. Role of erosion and deposition in creating coastal landforms – Part 3

Success criterion 1

Beaches

A beach is an accumulation of sand and shingle (pebbles) deposited between the low tide mark and the highest point reached by storm waves. Beaches form where constructive waves deposit material, often in sheltered bays.

  • Sandy beaches — wide and gently sloping, because fine sand holds water and the backwash flows back over the surface.
  • Shingle (pebble) beaches — narrower and steeper, because water drains quickly through the gaps between pebbles, so the backwash is weak.
  • Bayhead beaches — form at the back of a bay between two headlands.

Beach profiles change with the seasons: constructive waves build berms (ridges) in summer; destructive storm waves drag material offshore in winter.

Success criterion 2

What is a spit?

A spit is a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle that is attached to the land at one end and extends out into the sea or across a river mouth.

Success criterion 3

Conditions needed for a spit

  • A good supply of sediment (e.g. from eroding cliffs).
  • Longshore drift to move the sediment along the coast.
  • A place where the coastline changes direction, such as a river mouth or estuary.
  • Shallow, sheltered water where waves lose energy and deposit material.
  • A river current or tide that is not strong enough to wash all the sediment away.
Success criterion 4

How a spit is formed

  1. Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast.
  2. Where the coastline changes direction, the water is calmer and deeper, so the sediment is deposited.
  3. Over time, more deposition extends the ridge out into the sea.
  4. Changes in the wind and wave direction curve the end of the spit, forming a recurved end (hook).
  5. The area behind the spit is sheltered, so mud is deposited and a salt marsh develops.
Success criterion 5

Bars and tombolos

  • A bar forms when a spit grows right across a bay, joining two headlands. The water trapped behind becomes a lagoon. Example: Slapton Ley, Devon.
  • A tombolo is a ridge of sand or shingle that joins the mainland to an island. Example: Chesil Beach, Dorset, about 29 km long, links the mainland to the Isle of Portland.

Key terms

Beach
An accumulation of sand and shingle between low tide and the reach of storm waves.
Spit
A narrow ridge of sediment attached to the land at one end.
Recurved end
The curved tip of a spit caused by changes in wind direction.
Bar
A ridge of sediment across a bay, trapping a lagoon.
Tombolo
A ridge of sediment joining the mainland to an island.
Case study

Spurn Head, Holderness

Spurn Head is a spit about 5 km long at the mouth of the Humber Estuary, East Yorkshire.

  • Its sediment comes from the rapidly eroding boulder-clay cliffs of the Holderness coast, carried south by longshore drift.
  • Where the coastline changes direction at the Humber, the sediment is deposited.
  • It has a recurved end and salt marshes in the sheltered water behind it.
  • Storms have breached it several times, most recently in December 2013.
Exam-style question

Explain the formation of a spit. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: longshore drift transports sand and shingle along the coast.
  • 1 mark: where the coastline changes direction (e.g. at a river mouth), material continues to be deposited into the sea.
  • 1 mark: the spit grows out into the sea; a change in wind/wave direction can curve the end (recurved tip).
  • 1 mark: salt marsh forms in the sheltered area behind the spit.

Model answer: Longshore drift transports sand and shingle along the coast. Where the coastline changes direction, for example at an estuary, the material continues to be carried in the same direction and is deposited in the sea, gradually building a ridge of sand that extends out from the land. Changes in the wind direction cause the end to curve, forming a recurved tip, and the sheltered water behind the spit allows a salt marsh to develop.

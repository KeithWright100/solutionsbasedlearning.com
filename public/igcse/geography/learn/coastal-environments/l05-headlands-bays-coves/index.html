5. Role of erosion and deposition in creating coastal landforms – Part 1

Success criterion 1

Headlands and bays

Headlands and bays form on coasts where bands of hard and soft rock meet the sea.

  1. The soft rock (e.g. clay or sand) is eroded quickly by hydraulic action and abrasion, and is worn back to form a bay.
  2. The hard rock (e.g. chalk or limestone) is more resistant, so it is eroded more slowly and is left sticking out into the sea as a headland.
  3. The headland is now exposed to more wave energy, so it is attacked by erosion and may develop caves, arches and stacks (Lesson 6).
  4. The bay is sheltered, so waves lose energy and deposit sand, forming a beach.
Success criterion 2

How coves are formed

A cove is a small, almost circular bay with a narrow entrance. Coves form on concordant coasts:

  1. A band of hard rock runs parallel to the coast, protecting softer rock behind it.
  2. Waves exploit a weakness such as a joint or fault in the hard rock and eventually break through it.
  3. The sea then rapidly erodes the soft rock behind, scooping out a wide, rounded bay.
  4. Further back, another band of more resistant rock slows erosion, limiting the size of the cove.

Example: Lulworth Cove, Dorset. The sea broke through a band of resistant Portland limestone and eroded the soft clays and sands behind, until it reached harder chalk at the back of the cove.

Success criterion 3

Discordant and concordant coastlines

Discordant coastline

  • Rock bands run at right angles to the coast
  • Hard and soft rocks are eroded at different rates
  • Creates an irregular coastline of headlands and bays
  • Example: Swanage Bay, Dorset

Concordant coastline

  • Rock bands run parallel to the coast
  • A hard outer band protects the softer rock behind
  • Creates a fairly straight coastline, with coves where the hard rock is breached
  • Example: Lulworth Cove, Dorset

Key terms

Headland
A section of hard rock sticking out into the sea.
Bay
An inlet of the sea where soft rock has been eroded.
Cove
A small, rounded bay with a narrow entrance.
Discordant coast
Rock bands at right angles to the coast.
Concordant coast
Rock bands parallel to the coast.
Case study

The Dorset coast

The Dorset coast (part of the Jurassic Coast World Heritage Site) shows both types of coastline within a few kilometres:

  • Swanage Bay is on a discordant stretch, where soft clays and sands have been eroded between two headlands of more resistant rock: Ballard Point (chalk) and Durlston Head (limestone).
  • Lulworth Cove is on a concordant stretch, where the sea has broken through a narrow band of limestone and hollowed out the softer rocks behind.
Exam-style question

Explain how headlands and bays are formed. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: they form on a discordant coastline where bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast.
  • 1 mark: the softer rock (e.g. clay) is eroded more quickly, forming a bay.
  • 1 mark: the harder rock (e.g. chalk) is more resistant and is left jutting out as a headland.
  • 1 mark: named erosion process, e.g. hydraulic action and abrasion erode the soft rock; deposition in the sheltered bay may form a beach.

Model answer: Headlands and bays form on discordant coastlines, where bands of resistant and less resistant rock meet the sea at right angles. Processes such as hydraulic action and abrasion erode the softer rock more quickly, forming a bay. The harder rock is eroded more slowly, so it is left sticking out into the sea as a headland. Waves are weaker in the sheltered bay, so sand is often deposited to form a beach.

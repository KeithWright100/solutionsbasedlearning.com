Success criterion 1

Why are coasts important?

The coast is the narrow zone where the land meets the sea. It is one of the most important environments on Earth:

  • People — around 40% of the world’s population lives within about 100 km of the coast, and many of the largest cities, such as Mumbai, Shanghai, New York and Bangkok, are on or near the coast.
  • Economy — ports handle most world trade; fishing, tourism, oil and gas, and industry all depend on the coast.
  • Ecosystems — coral reefs, mangroves, salt marshes and sand dunes are rich in biodiversity and act as nurseries for fish.
  • Protection — beaches, dunes, reefs and mangroves absorb wave energy and protect the land from storms and flooding.
  • Recreation and culture — coasts are popular for leisure, and many coastal landscapes are protected heritage sites.

Because so many people and activities depend on the coast, understanding how it works — and how to manage it — is vital.

Success criterion 2

Constructive and destructive waves

Geographers describe waves using four features: height, frequency (number of waves per minute), wavelength (distance between crests) and the strength of the swash (water rushing up the beach) and backwash (water draining back).

FeatureConstructive wavesDestructive waves
HeightLow (usually under 1 m)High and steep
FrequencyLow: about 6–8 per minuteHigh: about 10–14 per minute
WavelengthLongShort
Swash and backwashStrong swash, weak backwashWeak swash, strong backwash
EffectDeposit material, building wide, gently sloping beachesErode material and drag it offshore, leaving steeper beaches
When?Calm weather, often summerStorms, often winter
Success criterion 3

How do waves break?

In deep water, the water in a wave moves in a circular (orbital) motion — the water itself does not travel forward, only the energy does.

  1. As the wave approaches the shore, the water becomes shallower.
  2. Friction with the sea bed slows down the base of the wave, so the circular motion becomes elliptical.
  3. The wavelength shortens and the wave becomes taller and steeper.
  4. The top (crest) of the wave is moving faster than the base, so it topples forward and the wave breaks.
  5. The water rushes up the beach as swash and returns as backwash.
Success criterion 4

Wind speed, fetch and the size of waves

Waves are formed by wind blowing over the sea. Friction between the wind and the water surface transfers energy to the water. The size and energy of a wave depend on:

  • Wind speed — stronger winds transfer more energy, producing bigger waves.
  • Duration — the longer the wind blows, the more energy is transferred.
  • Fetch — the distance of open water the wind blows over. A long fetch gives the wind more time and distance to build up waves. For example, south-west England faces the Atlantic, with a fetch of several thousand kilometres, so it receives very powerful waves; a sheltered bay or small lake has a short fetch and only small waves.

Key terms

Coast
The zone where the land meets the sea.
Swash
Water rushing up the beach after a wave breaks.
Backwash
Water draining back down the beach towards the sea.
Wavelength
The distance between two wave crests.
Fetch
The distance of open water over which the wind blows.

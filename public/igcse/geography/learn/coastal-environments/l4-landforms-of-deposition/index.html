Landforms of coastal deposition

Beaches

A beach is an accumulation of sand and shingle (pebbles) deposited between the low tide mark and the highest point reached by storm waves. Beaches form in sheltered areas such as bays, where constructive waves deposit material.

  • Sandy beaches are usually wide and gently sloping, because sand is fine and the backwash drains away slowly.
  • Shingle beaches are steeper, because water drains quickly through the larger pebbles so the backwash is weaker.

Beach profiles change with the seasons: in summer, constructive waves build the beach up with ridges called berms; in winter, destructive storm waves drag material offshore and the beach becomes narrower and steeper.

Spits

A spit is a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle that sticks out from the land into the sea, attached to the land at one end.

  1. Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast.
  2. Where the coastline changes direction (e.g. at a river mouth or estuary), the sea becomes deeper and calmer, so the sediment is deposited.
  3. Over time, more deposition makes the ridge grow out into the sea.
  4. Changes in wind and wave direction can curve the end of the spit, forming a recurved end (hook).
  5. The area behind the spit is sheltered, so mud is deposited and salt marshes develop.

A spit cannot grow right across a river mouth because the river current carries the sediment away.

Bars and tombolos

  • A bar forms when a spit grows right across a bay, joining two headlands. Water trapped behind it forms a lagoon. Example: Slapton Ley in Devon.
  • A tombolo is a ridge of sand or shingle that joins the mainland to an island. Example: Chesil Beach in Dorset, about 29 km long, links the mainland to the Isle of Portland.

Key terms

Beach
An accumulation of sand and shingle between low tide and the reach of storm waves.
Berm
A ridge on a beach marking the high tide line.
Spit
A long narrow ridge of sediment attached to the land at one end.
Bar
A ridge of sediment that grows across a bay, trapping a lagoon.
Tombolo
A ridge of sediment joining the mainland to an island.
Case study

Spurn Head, Holderness

Spurn Head is a spit at the mouth of the Humber Estuary on England’s east coast, about 5 km long.

  • It is made of sand and shingle eroded from the soft boulder-clay cliffs of the Holderness coast to the north and carried south by longshore drift.
  • Where the coast changes direction at the Humber, the material is deposited, building the spit out across the estuary.
  • It has a recurved end, and salt marshes have formed in the sheltered water behind it.
  • Storms have breached it several times, most recently in 2013, when it was cut off at high tide.
Exam practice

Explain the formation of a spit. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind (1).
  • Where the coastline changes direction, e.g. at an estuary, the water is calmer and deeper, so sediment is deposited (1).
  • Continued deposition extends the ridge out to sea (1).
  • A change in wind/wave direction can form a recurved end; salt marsh forms in the sheltered area behind (1).

Coastal ecosystems: dunes, salt marshes, mangroves and coral reefs

Sand dunes and salt marshes

In temperate areas such as the UK, two important coastal ecosystems develop where sediment is deposited.

Sand dunes form when dry sand is blown inland from the beach and trapped by obstacles. Pioneer plants such as marram grass are adapted to the harsh, dry, salty conditions: long roots reach water, and rolled leaves reduce water loss. As plants die they add organic matter, soil develops, and a succession of plants follows inland, from yellow dunes to grey dunes and finally heath or woodland.

Salt marshes form in sheltered, calm water behind spits or in estuaries, where fine mud is deposited. Salt-tolerant plants (halophytes) such as glasswort and cordgrass trap more mud, so the marsh slowly builds up.

Mangroves

Mangroves are salt-tolerant trees and shrubs that grow on muddy tropical and subtropical coasts, mostly between about 30°N and 30°S, for example in Indonesia, Brazil, Nigeria and the Sundarbans of Bangladesh and India.

  • Adaptations: prop and stilt roots support trees in soft mud; breathing roots (pneumatophores) poke up out of the water; leaves can get rid of excess salt.
  • Value: roots trap sediment and reduce wave energy, protecting coasts from storm surges; they are nursery areas for fish; and they provide wood, honey and income for local people.
  • Threats: clearance for shrimp farms, rice paddies, tourism and urban development; pollution; and sea level rise.

Coral reefs

Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called polyps, which live in a partnership with algae. Reefs need very specific conditions:

  • warm water, about 18–29°C, so they are found roughly between 30°N and 30°S;
  • clear, shallow water (usually less than 50 m deep) so sunlight reaches the algae;
  • salt water, away from river mouths that bring fresh water and sediment.

Reefs are among the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth. They protect coasts by breaking up waves and support fishing and tourism.

Threats: rising sea temperatures cause coral bleaching (the coral expels its algae and can die); ocean acidification; pollution and sediment from farmland; overfishing and destructive fishing; and damage from tourists and boats.

Key terms

Ecosystem
A community of plants and animals and the environment they live in.
Succession
The gradual change in plant communities over time as conditions change.
Pioneer plant
The first plant to colonise a new area, e.g. marram grass on dunes.
Halophyte
A plant adapted to grow in salty conditions.
Coral bleaching
When stressed coral expels its algae, turns white and may die.
Case study

The Sundarbans mangrove forest

The Sundarbans on the delta of the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers is the largest mangrove forest in the world, covering about 10,000 km² across Bangladesh and India.

  • It is home to the endangered Bengal tiger and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • It acts as a natural barrier against cyclones and storm surges — for example, it reduced the impact of Cyclone Sidr in 2007.
  • Millions of people depend on it for fishing, honey and wood.
  • It is threatened by clearance for shrimp farming and agriculture, reduced fresh water from upstream dams, rising salinity and sea level rise.
Exam practice

Explain why mangroves are important for coastal communities. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Roots trap sediment and reduce wave energy, so they protect against erosion and storm surges (1+1 developed).
  • They are nursery grounds for fish and shellfish, supporting local fishing (1).
  • They provide resources and income such as wood, honey and tourism (1).

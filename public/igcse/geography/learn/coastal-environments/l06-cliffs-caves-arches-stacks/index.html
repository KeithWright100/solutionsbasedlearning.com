6. Role of erosion and deposition in creating coastal landforms – Part 2

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Coastal Environments · 2.1 Coastal processes and landforms · Lesson 6 of 15

Role of erosional and depositional processes in the development of landforms: headlands and bays, cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks and stumps, beaches, spits and bars.

Success criterion 1

Using the language of erosion

To explain landforms you must use the correct terms for erosion — examiners reward this.

  • Hydraulic action — waves force air into cracks; the pressure widens them.
  • Abrasion — waves hurl sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition — rocks carried by waves collide and become smaller and rounder.
  • Solution (corrosion) — slightly acidic sea water dissolves rocks such as chalk and limestone.

Tip: instead of writing “the sea wears the cliff away”, write “the base of the cliff is eroded by hydraulic action and abrasion”.

Success criterion 2

Wave-cut notches and wave-cut platforms

  1. Waves erode the base of a cliff between high and low tide by hydraulic action and abrasion, creating a wave-cut notch.
  2. The notch gets deeper, so the cliff above is undercut and left unsupported.
  3. Eventually the overhanging cliff collapses under gravity. The waves remove the fallen rock (helped by attrition).
  4. The process repeats and the cliff retreats inland, leaving behind a gently sloping, rocky wave-cut platform that is exposed at low tide.

As the platform gets wider, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so the rate of retreat slows down.

Success criterion 3

Cracks, caves, arches, stacks and stumps

  1. Waves attack a crack or joint in a headland by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  2. The crack is widened into a cave.
  3. The cave is eroded through the headland, often meeting a cave on the other side, to form an arch (e.g. Durdle Door, Dorset).
  4. The arch widens and its roof is weakened by erosion below and weathering above, until it collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock: a stack (e.g. Old Harry Rocks).
  5. The stack is undercut at its base and collapses into a stump, which may only be visible at low tide.

Key terms

Wave-cut notch
An indent at the base of a cliff eroded between high and low tide.
Wave-cut platform
A gently sloping rocky area left as a cliff retreats.
Arch
A natural opening eroded through a headland.
Stack
An isolated pillar of rock left when an arch collapses.
Stump
The low remains of a collapsed stack.
Case study

Old Harry Rocks and Durdle Door, Dorset

  • Old Harry Rocks are chalk stacks and a stump at Handfast Point, near Swanage. The headland has been eroded along joints in the chalk.
  • Durdle Door is a famous limestone arch near Lulworth Cove.
Exam-style question

Explain the formation of a stack. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • 1 mark: waves erode a crack or joint in a headland by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a cave.
  • 1 mark: the cave is eroded right through the headland to form an arch.
  • 1 mark: the arch is widened by erosion at the base and weathering of the roof.
  • 1 mark: the roof collapses under gravity, leaving an isolated pillar of rock: a stack (which is later eroded to a stump).

Model answer: Waves attack a weakness, such as a joint, in a headland. Hydraulic action and abrasion enlarge the crack into a cave. Over time the cave is eroded right through the headland to form an arch. Erosion at the base and weathering of the top widen the arch until the roof can no longer be supported and collapses, leaving an isolated column of rock called a stack.

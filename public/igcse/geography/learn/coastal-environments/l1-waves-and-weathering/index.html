Coastal processes: waves, weathering and mass movement

Edexcel IGCSE Geography · Paper 1: Physical Geography · Coastal Environments · Lesson 1 of 6

SpecificationPAPER 1 · SECTION A
Skill FocusDescribe and explain processes
Reading Time30–40 minutes

In this lesson you will learn to…

  • Explain how waves form and what affects their energy (wind, fetch, duration).
  • Compare constructive and destructive waves.
  • Describe types of weathering and mass movement on cliffs.

How do waves form?

Waves are created by wind blowing over the surface of the sea. Friction between the wind and the water makes the water move in a circular motion. As a wave reaches shallow water near the coast, the bottom of the wave is slowed by friction with the sea bed, the top carries on, and the wave breaks.

The energy of a wave depends on three things:

  • Wind speed — stronger winds create bigger waves.
  • Duration — how long the wind has been blowing.
  • Fetch — the distance of open water the wind blows over. The south-west coast of the UK has a fetch of thousands of kilometres across the Atlantic, so its waves are very powerful.

When a wave breaks, the water that rushes up the beach is the swash; the water that drains back down is the backwash.

Constructive and destructive waves

Geographers group waves into two types, depending on whether the swash or the backwash is stronger.

Constructive waves

  • Low height, long wavelength
  • 6–8 waves per minute
  • Strong swash, weak backwash
  • Deposit material and build up wide, gently sloping beaches
  • Common in calm summer weather

Destructive waves

  • High and steep, short wavelength
  • 10–14 waves per minute
  • Weak swash, strong backwash
  • Erode material and drag it offshore, leaving steeper beaches
  • Common during winter storms

Weathering

Weathering is the breakdown of rock in situ (where it is) by the weather, plants or chemical reactions. It weakens cliffs so they are easier to erode.

  • Mechanical weathering — for example freeze–thaw: water enters cracks, freezes and expands by about 9%, widening the crack. Repeated many times, pieces of rock break off. Salt weathering happens when sea water evaporates in cracks and salt crystals grow.
  • Chemical weathering — for example rainwater is a weak carbonic acid, which slowly dissolves rocks such as chalk and limestone.
  • Biological weathering — plant roots grow into cracks and burrowing animals loosen rock.

Mass movement

Mass movement is the downhill movement of material under the influence of gravity. It is common on coasts made of soft rock such as clay, especially after heavy rain.

  • Rock fall — pieces of rock break off a steep cliff face and fall to the base. Common on hard-rock cliffs affected by freeze–thaw.
  • Slumping (rotational slip) — saturated soft rock such as clay slides down a curved slip surface. Rain soaks into the cliff, adds weight and reduces friction.
  • Landslide — large blocks of rock slide downhill along a flat slip plane.

Waves then remove the fallen material from the cliff base, so the cliff stays steep and the cycle repeats.

Key terms

Fetch
The distance of open water over which the wind blows.
Swash
Water rushing up the beach after a wave breaks.
Backwash
Water draining back down the beach towards the sea.
Weathering
The breakdown of rock in situ by weather, plants or chemical reactions.
Mass movement
The downhill movement of material under gravity, e.g. slumping or rock fall.
Exam practice

Explain how destructive waves can lead to cliff retreat. (4 marks)

Show mark guidance
  • Destructive waves have a strong backwash, so they remove more material than they deposit (1).
  • They erode the base of the cliff by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a wave-cut notch (1).
  • The cliff above is undercut and becomes unstable, so it collapses (1).
  • Waves remove the fallen material, so the process repeats and the cliff retreats inland (1).

3. Local responses to global interactions

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 5: Human Development and Diversity · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand how civil society and governments resist, restrict or promote global interactions.

Syllabus Statement

Campaigns against TNCs, local sourcing and anti-immigration movements; travel bans, internet controls, protectionism and resource nationalism; civil society and international-mindedness (Hong Kong and the Malala Fund).

Resistance to global interactions: Campaigns against TNCs

Civil society means organisations and groups outside government and business: NGOs, charities, unions, community and protest groups.

Success criterion 1

Resistance to global interactions: Buying local: Thailand’s “Eat Local” movement

Movements to buy local food and goods reject globalised production. In Thailand, “Eat Local” and similar campaigns promote local farmers’ markets, organic produce and traditional Thai ingredients, supported by social media and chefs. Reasons are economic (supporting local farmers and small businesses), social and cultural (protecting traditional food culture), health (fewer processed foods and chemicals) and environmental (fewer food miles and less packaging).

Success criterion 1

Resistance to global interactions: Anti-immigration movements

Some resistance targets migration. The Minuteman Project (from 2005) organised armed civilian volunteers to watch the US–Mexico border in Arizona and report migrants to the authorities, claiming the government had failed to control the border. It gained media attention and influenced political debate, but was widely criticised as vigilantism, caught few migrants, and split into rival groups. Anti-immigration parties and movements have also grown in Europe.

Evaluation: civil society movements can raise awareness and change corporate and government behaviour, but their impact varies, they may be dominated by particular groups, and some promote exclusion rather than international-mindedness.

Success criterion 1

Resistance to global interactions: Civil society, anti-globalisation and nationalism in the USA and France

The UN describes three sectors of society: government, business and civil society, the groups and organisations that act in the public interest outside government and business. Anti-globalisation movements oppose the economic, cultural or political effects of global interactions. People may oppose global interactions because of job losses to cheaper countries, the power of TNCs, environmental damage, loss of local culture and language, rising immigration, inequality, or a feeling that decisions are taken far away.

USA: anti-immigration measures under President Trump (from 2017) included building more of a border wall with Mexico, the 2017 travel ban, reducing refugee admissions, the “Remain in Mexico” policy for asylum seekers, separating families at the border (later reversed), and attempting to end protection for people brought to the USA as children (DACA).

France: the National Front (renamed National Rally in 2018), led by Marine Le Pen, gained support by opposing immigration, the EU and globalisation, claiming they threatened French jobs, culture and security. Support grew after deindustrialisation, the 2008 financial crisis, the 2015 migrant crisis and terrorist attacks; Le Pen reached the presidential run-off in 2017 and 2022.

Are the objections valid? Evaluate each: some concerns (job losses in particular places, environmental damage, TNC power) are backed by evidence, while others (claims that migrants increase crime or take most jobs) are often not supported by the data. A strong answer separates the two and considers who benefits and who loses from globalisation.

Success criterion 2

Geopolitical constraints: Controls on personal freedoms

  • Travel and migration bans: US Executive Order 13769 (January 2017) suspended entry for citizens of seven Muslim-majority countries (Iran, Iraq, Libya, Somalia, Sudan, Syria and Yemen) and paused refugee admissions. It was challenged in the courts and replaced by revised versions, one of which the Supreme Court upheld in 2018.
  • Internet controls: China’s “Great Firewall” blocks Google, Facebook, YouTube and many foreign news sites; North Korea allows almost no internet access; Iran and Russia block or throttle platforms, especially during protests. Countries such as Iceland and Estonia have very open internet.
  • Militia and conflict controls: armed groups may restrict movement, education (especially for girls) and communication in areas they control.
Success criterion 2

Geopolitical constraints: Protectionism

Protectionism is the use of trade barriers to protect domestic industries from foreign competition.

MeasureExample
TariffsTaxes on imports, such as US tariffs on Chinese goods since 2018 and much wider US tariffs from 2025
QuotasLimits on the quantity of imports
SubsidiesGovernment support for domestic producers, such as EU farm subsidies
Embargoes and sanctionsBans on trade with particular countries
RegulationsStandards and paperwork that make imports harder

Governments use protectionism to protect jobs and strategic industries and respond to political pressure, but it can raise prices, provoke retaliation and reduce efficiency.

Success criterion 2

Geopolitical constraints: Resource nationalism

Resource nationalism is when governments take greater control of natural resources, through nationalisation, higher royalties, export bans or limits on foreign ownership. Examples: Bolivia nationalised its gas industry in 2006; Indonesia banned exports of raw nickel ore in 2020 to encourage processing at home; Mexico nationalised lithium in 2022. It can keep more wealth in the country but can deter foreign investment.

Success criterion 2

Geopolitical constraints: Censorship and restrictions on travel

In a Committee to Protect Journalists ranking, the five most censored countries were Eritrea, North Korea, Saudi Arabia, Ethiopia and Azerbaijan, based on repressive laws, internet restrictions and the imprisonment of journalists. In Eritrea, fewer than 1% of people went online. Governments restrict global interactions to maintain political control and stop the spread of ideas that might challenge them; even in countries with open internet access, governments may monitor social media.

Success criterion 3

Hong Kong and the Malala Fund: Civil society and international-mindedness

International-mindedness is openness to and engagement with people, cultures and issues beyond your own country. Civil society promotes it through global campaigns (for example on climate change, human rights and education), international NGOs, volunteering and, increasingly, social media, which lets movements share information instantly, organise without leaders and reach global audiences, including campaigns for internet freedom.

Success criterion 3

Hong Kong and the Malala Fund: Hong Kong pro-democracy demonstrations

  • 2014 Umbrella Movement: students and citizens occupied central Hong Kong for 79 days, demanding genuine universal suffrage.
  • 2019–20 protests: began against a bill allowing extradition to mainland China and grew into a wider pro-democracy movement, with some of the largest marches in Hong Kong’s history.
  • Use of technology: encrypted messaging apps (such as Telegram), online forums and AirDrop to organise; “be water” tactics; global solidarity rallies and international media coverage.
  • Outcome: the extradition bill was withdrawn, but the National Security Law (June 2020) led to arrests, the closure of pro-democracy media and the end of large protests.
Success criterion 3

Hong Kong and the Malala Fund: The Malala Fund and comparison

Malala Yousafzai campaigned for girls’ education in Pakistan’s Swat Valley and was shot by the Taliban in 2012. She survived, co-founded the Malala Fund with her father in 2013, and in 2014 became the youngest Nobel Peace Prize winner. The Fund invests in local education activists in countries where girls miss out on school and campaigns globally for girls’ right to 12 years of free, safe, quality education.

Success criterion 3

Hong Kong and the Malala Fund: Hong Kong background and the barriers to girls’ education

Hong Kong: a British colony that was handed back to China on 1 July 1997. As a Special Administrative Region under “one country, two systems”, it was promised a high degree of autonomy, with its own legal system, currency, free press and the right to protest, until 2047. Many people were dissatisfied because Beijing kept control over who could stand as Hong Kong’s leader. In 2014 protesters, many of them students and young people, used umbrellas to protect themselves from pepper spray and tear gas, giving the Umbrella Movement its name.

Girls’ education: UNESCO estimates that around 120–130 million girls of primary and secondary school age are out of school. Educating girls raises incomes, reduces child marriage and child mortality, improves health and strengthens economies. The main barriers are poverty, child marriage, conflict and insecurity, distance to school, a lack of safe sanitation, and social norms that value boys’ education more. The Malala Fund works with local education activists (Education Champions) in countries such as Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nigeria, Ethiopia and Brazil, funds their projects, and advocates to governments; it spreads its message through social media, its online publication and Malala’s high public profile.

Key terms

Groups and organisations outside government and business.
Anti-globalisation movement
Resistance to aspects of globalisation such as TNC power.
Local sourcing
Buying food and goods produced nearby.
Vigilantism

Geopolitical constraint

Protectionism
Trade barriers that protect domestic industries.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods.
Resource nationalism
Government control over natural resources.
International-mindedness
Openness to and engagement with global perspectives and issues.
Internet freedom
The ability to access and share information online without censorship.
National Security Law
A 2020 law imposed on Hong Kong by Beijing that criminalised dissent.

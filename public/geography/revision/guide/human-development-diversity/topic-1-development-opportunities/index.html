1. Development opportunities

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 5: Human Development and Diversity · Topics · Topic 1 of 3

Success criterion 1

Measuring development: Human development as a multidimensional process

Human development is about widening people’s choices and freedoms so they can live long, healthy, educated and fulfilling lives. It is multidimensional: income matters, but so do health, education, gender equality, political freedom and the environment. A single measure such as GDP per capita is therefore not enough, so geographers use composite indicators that combine several measures.

Success criterion 1

Measuring development: The SDGs, HDI and GII

MeasureWhat it includesEvaluation
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)17 goals and 169 targets agreed by all UN members for 2015–2030, from ending poverty and hunger to gender equality, clean energy and climate actionVery broad and include the environment, but progress is hard to compare in one number and data are missing for many targets
Human Development Index (HDI)UNDP, since 1990: life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and income (GNI per capita, PPP), scored from 0 to 1Simple and widely available, but leaves out inequality, gender, freedom and the environment; national averages hide differences within countries
Gender Inequality Index (GII)UNDP, since 2010: reproductive health (maternal mortality, adolescent births), empowerment (seats in parliament, secondary education) and labour-force participation; 0 = equalityFocuses directly on gender gaps, but some data are unreliable and it cannot capture issues such as violence or unpaid work
Success criterion 1

Measuring development: Validity and reliability

A valid indicator actually measures what it claims to measure (for example, is years of schooling a good measure of education if the quality of teaching varies?). A reliable indicator gives consistent, accurate results, which depends on good data collection. Composite indices are only as good as their data and the choices made about which variables to include and how to weight them. They work best alongside other evidence, such as inequality-adjusted HDI, poverty measures and local surveys.

Success criterion 1

Measuring development: Rostow, the SDGs’ five Ps and economic versus social indicators

Rostow’s model (1960) described five stages of economic growth: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity and the age of high mass consumption. It was widely used but is now criticised for assuming all countries follow the Western path, focusing only on economic growth, and ignoring colonial history, global trade relationships and the environment.

The 17 SDGs can be grouped into the five Ps: People (end poverty and hunger, dignity and equality), Planet (protect the environment and act on climate change), Prosperity (fulfilling lives and progress in harmony with nature), Peace (peaceful, just and inclusive societies) and Partnership (global cooperation to deliver the goals).

TypeWhat it showsExamples
Economic indicatorsMeasure wealth and industrialisationGNI or GNP per capita (PPP), GDP growth
Social indicatorsShow how wealth is used to improve quality of lifeDoctors per 100,000 people, calories per person per day, adult literacy
Strengths of GNI per capita (PPP)Limitations
Easy to compare between countries and over time; PPP adjusts for the cost of living; widely availableAn average that hides inequality between rich and poor and between regions; ignores the informal economy, unpaid work, health, education, freedom and the environment

Reliability of sources: a national census or UN statistical database is usually high reliability; a small household survey, interviews in one community or an old government estimate are lower; an informal social media survey is low.

Success criterion 1

Measuring development: Same HDI, different story: New Zealand and the USA

The UNDP says the HDI captures only part of human development and does not reflect inequality, poverty, security or empowerment. Two countries with the same HDI can have very different experiences:

The USA’s HDI is lifted by its much higher income, while New Zealand’s is lifted by longer life expectancy and expected schooling. Once inequality and gender are considered (IHDI and GII), New Zealand performs better, so saying the two countries are “equally developed” has limited validity.

Success criterion 2

Affirmative action: Affirmative action and women in Nepal

Affirmative action means deliberate policies that improve opportunities for groups that have faced discrimination, such as quotas, reserved seats, targeted funding or anti-discrimination laws.

The development gap is the difference in wealth and wellbeing between groups or places. Marginalised groups (women, indigenous peoples, ethnic, religious and sexual minorities) are pushed to the edge of society; empowerment means increasing their control over their own lives and decisions.

Nepal: one of the poorest countries in Asia, where around 80% of the land is unsuitable for farming and patriarchal traditions and caste discrimination limit women’s opportunities. Women face high maternal mortality, early marriage (about 35% of women aged 20–24 were married before 18), lower literacy, unpaid farm work and limited access to land and credit, and girls are trafficked across the region. After the end of the civil war (2006) and the abolition of the monarchy, Nepal’s 2015 constitution requires that at least one-third of seats in the federal parliament go to women, and that the president and vice president are of different sexes or communities. In local government, one of each mayor and deputy mayor pair must usually be a woman. Bidhya Devi Bhandari became Nepal’s first female president in 2015, and women held about 33% of parliamentary seats in 2024. Schemes also promote girls’ education and women’s property rights, and NGOs such as Volunteers Initiative Nepal run women’s empowerment, microfinance and literacy programmes, though patriarchal attitudes, child marriage and rural poverty remain barriers.

Rwanda is a striking contrast: its 2003 constitution reserves at least 30% of parliamentary seats for women, and women now hold over 60% of seats in the lower house, the highest share in the world.

Success criterion 2

Affirmative action: LGBTQ+ communities

Affirmative action for LGBTQ+ people includes anti-discrimination laws in employment and housing, legal recognition of gender identity, and equal marriage. Nepal’s Supreme Court recognised a third gender in 2007, and the first same-sex marriage was registered in 2023. Thailand’s marriage equality law took effect in January 2025, the first in Southeast Asia. Progress is very uneven: same-sex relationships remain illegal in dozens of countries.

Success criterion 2

Affirmative action: The Dani of Papua (Irian Jaya)

The Dani are an indigenous people of the Baliem Valley in the highlands of Papua, the Indonesian half of New Guinea (formerly called Irian Jaya). They had little contact with outsiders until the late 1930s.

InfluenceEffects
Christian missionaries (from the 1950s)Schools, health clinics, literacy and religious teaching; also pressure to abandon traditional beliefs, dress and practices
Indonesian government (Papua under Indonesian control since the 1960s)Roads, schools, health care and integration into the national economy; campaigns to change traditional dress; transmigration brought settlers from other islands, creating competition for land and jobs
TourismThe annual Baliem Valley Festival brings income but can turn culture into a performance

Evaluation: these changes improved access to education and health care, but they were largely imposed from outside, weakened traditional culture and have been linked to inequality and tension in Papua.

Essay plan (12 marks: “Using examples, analyse how affirmative action can be used to close the development gap”): define affirmative action and the development gap; paragraph 1, education (girls in Nepal, mission schools for the Dani); paragraph 2, political representation and employment (Nepal and Rwanda’s quotas, LGBTQ+ anti-discrimination laws); paragraph 3, government action for indigenous groups (the Dani); conclude on when affirmative action is most effective and its limits.

Success criterion 3

Microfinance and Fairtrade: Social entrepreneurship and microfinance

Social entrepreneurship uses business methods to solve social problems: success is measured by social impact as well as (or instead of) profit. Microfinance provides small loans, savings and insurance to people, often women, who cannot get credit from ordinary banks.

ExampleHow it works
Grameen Bank (Bangladesh)Founded by Muhammad Yunus, a bank since 1983; small loans mostly to rural women, often through borrower groups that support each other; Yunus and the bank won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize
KivaA non-profit online platform (2005) where people anywhere can lend as little as US$25 to entrepreneurs through local partners
KickStart (Kenya)Designs and sells low-cost human-powered irrigation pumps (MoneyMaker pumps) so small farmers can grow more and sell surplus crops
Success criterion 3

Microfinance and Fairtrade: Fairtrade

Fair trade is a social movement that aims for producers in developing countries to receive a fair price and work in safe, decent conditions. Fairtrade certifies products (such as coffee, cocoa, bananas and tea) produced to agreed social and environmental standards. Producers receive a minimum price that protects them when world prices fall, plus a Fairtrade Premium that cooperatives spend on community projects such as schools, clinics and clean water. It also promotes democratic cooperatives and bans forced and child labour.

The ten principles of fair trade (World Fair Trade Organization) are: opportunities for disadvantaged producers; transparency and accountability; fair trading practices; payment of a fair price; no child or forced labour; non-discrimination, gender equity and freedom of association; good working conditions; capacity building; promoting fair trade; and respect for the environment.

Success criterion 3

Microfinance and Fairtrade: Evaluating social entrepreneurship

ApproachStrengthsLimitations
MicrofinanceEmpowers women, builds businesses and savings, reaches people banks ignoreSome lenders charge high interest; over-borrowing and debt; may not lift the poorest out of poverty
FairtradeStable prices, community investment, better working conditionsOnly a small share of world trade; certification costs; benefits may not reach hired labourers

Essay plan (12 marks: “Using examples, evaluate how social entrepreneurship can reduce inequalities in development”): argue that it can reduce inequality, but its impact is limited by scale and wider structures. Paragraph 1: microfinance (Grameen Bank or Opportunity International); paragraph 2: M-Pesa and financial inclusion in Kenya; paragraph 3: Fairtrade producer networks; paragraph 4: overall evaluation. In each, give named evidence, explain the chain from intervention to development outcome, add a limitation, and finish with a mini-judgement.

Success criterion 3

Microfinance and Fairtrade: M-Pesa and the networks behind microfinance

M-Pesa (“M” for mobile, pesa is Swahili for money) was launched in Kenya in 2007 by Safaricom, part-owned by Vodafone, and later spread to Tanzania and other countries. Users store money on their phone, send it by PIN-secured text message and pay for goods; they deposit and withdraw cash through a network of local agents and shops, so no bank branch is needed. By 2012 around 17 million accounts were registered in Kenya. It has given millions of people access to formal financial services, made it cheaper and safer to send remittances, and reduced crime in cash-based communities; one major study estimated it helped lift about 2% of Kenyan households out of poverty, especially households headed by women. Online services such as WorldRemit similarly cut the cost of sending money home from abroad.

Microfinance is not just money passing from lender to borrower: it depends on a network of community groups, NGOs, banks and government agencies, which train local leaders in credit management, encourage community participation, and help formal institutions understand low-income borrowers. Resources flow through the network, both material (money, equipment) and non-material (information, training, support). Other examples include Opportunity International.

Success criterion 4

TNCs and CSR: Corporate social responsibility

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is a company’s voluntary commitment to act responsibly towards workers, communities and the environment, beyond what the law requires. As supply chains link places worldwide, the actions of TNCs affect people far away, so global interdependence increases the need for responsible and sustainable business.

Success criterion 4

TNCs and CSR: IKEA and sustainability

  • Its IWAY supplier code of conduct sets standards for working conditions, child labour and the environment, checked by audits.
  • Most of its wood comes from Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) certified or recycled sources.
  • It has invested heavily in wind and solar power and has targets to cut emissions across its value chain.
  • It sells more sustainable products and offers buy-back and resale schemes.
  • Criticism: its business model depends on high volumes of cheap furniture, and it has faced investigations into illegally logged wood in its supply chain.
Success criterion 4

TNCs and CSR: Global agreements

AgreementWhat it doesExample
International Framework Agreements (IFAs)Agreements between a TNC and a global union federation, setting labour standards (fair wages, safety, the right to join a union) across the whole company and its suppliersIKEA with the Building and Wood Workers’ International; H&M with IndustriALL

Bangladesh Accord (2013)A legally binding safety agreement between brands and unions after the Rana Plaza factory collapse, which killed over 1,100 workersSigned by around 200 brands

Evaluation: voluntary CSR can be used for public relations (“greenwashing”), while negotiated or binding agreements such as IFAs and the Accord give stronger, more enforceable protection.

Success criterion 4

TNCs and CSR: Why TNCs act responsibly, and who controls CSR

TermMeaning
ESGEnvironmental, social and governance criteria used by investors to judge a company’s responsibility
StakeholderAnyone affected by or able to influence a company: workers, suppliers, consumers, investors, governments, communities, NGOs
SustainabilityMeeting present needs without compromising future generations

Motivations for CSR include profit (efficiency and new markets), reputation, regulation, ethics, investor pressure (ESG funds) and consumer pressure. Change driven by regulation and investor pressure tends to last longer than public relations campaigns: a fossil-fuel company advertising an environmental project may be greenwashing if its core business is unchanged.

IKEA and the SDGs: its renewable energy investments link to SDG 7 (clean energy) and SDG 13 (climate action); sustainable timber and FSC sourcing to SDG 15 (life on land); buy-back, repair and recycling to SDG 12 (responsible consumption); and its IWAY supplier code and IFA to SDG 8 (decent work). Questions remain about transport emissions, encouraging mass consumption, monitoring a huge supply chain, and whether targets are met in practice.

Exam focus (12 marks: “Analyse the extent to which corporate social responsibility contributes to human development”): use the chain corporate action → implementation → effect on people → human-development outcome → limitation. Paragraph 1: environmental CSR; paragraph 2: social CSR; paragraph 3: IFAs and global agreements; then a synthesis judging the overall extent and the conditions for success.

Key terms

Human development
Widening people’s choices and freedoms to live long, healthy, educated lives.
Composite indicator
A measure that combines several indicators into one score.
HDI
The Human Development Index: health, education and income.
GII
The Gender Inequality Index: reproductive health, empowerment and the labour market.
Validity
Whether an indicator measures what it claims to measure.
Affirmative action
Policies that deliberately improve opportunities for disadvantaged groups.
Indigenous people
The original inhabitants of a place, with distinct cultures.
Quota
A fixed share of places reserved for a particular group.
Transmigration
An Indonesian programme moving people from crowded islands to less populated ones.
Social entrepreneurship
Using business methods to achieve social goals.
Microfinance
Small loans and financial services for people without access to banks.
Fairtrade
Certification guaranteeing producers a minimum price and a social premium.
Fairtrade Premium
Extra money paid to cooperatives for community projects.
CSR
Corporate social responsibility: voluntary responsible business behaviour.
IFA
International Framework Agreement between a TNC and a global union.
Global interdependence
The reliance of places and companies on each other worldwide.
Greenwashing
Exaggerating environmental credentials for public relations.

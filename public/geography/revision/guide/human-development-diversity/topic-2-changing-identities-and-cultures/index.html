2. Changing identities and cultures

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 5: Human Development and Diversity · Topics · Topic 2 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the spectrum of cultural diversity and how global interactions and diasporas change cultures and identities.

Syllabus Statement

Culture, ethnicity and identity terminology; diffusion, cultural imperialism, glocalisation and hybridity; landscape change in Dubai; the Chinese diaspora and Chinatown in London.

Success criterion 1

Culture and identity: A global culture?

Global flows of media, brands, migration and tourism spread a global culture: English as a common language, Hollywood and streaming, fast food, global sports and social media. At the same time, local cultures persist and even strengthen, through local languages, religion, food and festivals, and through hybrid forms that mix global and local. The result is a spectrum of diversity, from strongly homogenised places (global city centres, airports) to places where local culture remains dominant.

Success criterion 1

Culture and identity: Is diversity widening or narrowing?

  • Narrowing: thousands of languages are endangered, global brands replace local businesses, and city landscapes look increasingly alike.
  • Widening: migration makes cities more multicultural (London, Toronto, Singapore), hybrid cultures appear, and the internet helps minority cultures reach global audiences.
  • The answer depends on scale: a single city may become more diverse even as the world as a whole becomes more alike.
Success criterion 2

Glocalisation and Dubai: Diffusion and cultural imperialism

Cultural traits diffuse through trade, migration, tourism, media and the internet. When the flow is mainly one-way, from powerful countries (especially the USA) to others, critics call it cultural imperialism. However, flows also run the other way: Japanese anime, Korean K-pop and dramas, Bollywood and Latin American music are now global.

Success criterion 2

Glocalisation and Dubai: Glocalisation and hybridity

Glocalisation is adapting a global brand to local tastes. Examples: McDonald’s sells the McAloo Tikki burger and no beef in India, and the McArabia in the Gulf; KFC has become a Christmas tradition in Japan. Glocalisation creates cultural hybridity, but it can also be seen as a subtler form of cultural imperialism: the global brand still expands and dominates, simply in local clothing.

Success criterion 2

Glocalisation and Dubai: Changing cultural landscapes: Dubai

Dubai grew from a small trading port to a global city after oil was discovered in the 1960s, then diversified into trade, finance, aviation and tourism. Its built environment shows homogenisation of the landscape: glass skyscrapers such as the Burj Khalifa (828 m, the world’s tallest building since 2010), giant malls filled with global brands, artificial islands such as Palm Jumeirah and international hotel chains. Expatriates make up around 90% of the population, creating a highly multicultural society.

Yet local culture survives and is promoted: the restored Al Fahidi historic district, wind towers, souks, mosques, Emirati dress and Arabic influences in modern architecture, a form of hybridity.

Success criterion 3

Diasporas: the Chinese diaspora: What is a diaspora?

A diaspora is a population that has spread from its original homeland to other parts of the world, while keeping connections with its culture and with the homeland. Diasporas maintain their culture abroad through language, religion, food, festivals, family networks, community organisations, businesses and media, and influence both their host and home countries through remittances, investment, ideas and politics.

Success criterion 3

Diasporas: the Chinese diaspora: The global Chinese diaspora

Tens of millions of people of Chinese descent live outside China. The largest communities are in Southeast Asia (Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia), followed by North America and Europe. Migration began centuries ago through trade, grew in the 1800s with labour migration to the Americas and Southeast Asia, and continues today through students and skilled workers. In Thailand, Chinese-Thai families have integrated deeply while keeping traditions such as Chinese New Year and Yaowarat (Bangkok’s Chinatown).

Success criterion 3

Diasporas: the Chinese diaspora: Case study: Chinatown, London

  • History: the first Chinese community grew up around the docks of Limehouse in East London in the 1800s, linked to sailors. After the Second World War it moved to Gerrard Street in Soho, where Chinese restaurants grew in the 1950s–70s, helped by migration from Hong Kong.
  • Today: restaurants, supermarkets, bakeries, Chinese gates (paifang), street signs in English and Chinese, and community associations. Chinese New Year celebrations in the West End and Trafalgar Square draw huge crowds.
  • Local scale: it gives Soho a distinctive cultural landscape and attracts tourists.
  • Global scale: it links London to a worldwide Chinese network of trade, family and culture. Similar Chinatowns exist in San Francisco (the oldest in North America), New York and Sydney.
  • Changes: rising rents and redevelopment are changing the area, and many Chinese residents now live elsewhere in London, so it is becoming more of a commercial and symbolic centre.

Key terms

Culture
The shared beliefs, values and way of life of a group.
Cultural hybridisation
The mixing of cultures to create new forms.
Cultural homogenisation
Cultures becoming more alike.
Cultural imperialism
The dominance or imposition of one culture over others.
Detraditionalisation
Traditions losing their influence on people’s lives.
Diffusion
The spread of cultural traits from place to place.
Glocalisation
Adapting global products to local tastes.
Cultural hybridity
A blend of global and local cultural elements.
Cultural landscape
The visible imprint of culture on a place, including buildings.
Diaspora
A dispersed population that keeps links with its homeland and culture.
Chinatown
An urban district with a concentration of Chinese businesses and culture.
Remittances
Money sent home by migrants.
Assimilation
When migrants adopt the culture of the host society.

3. Water scarcity and water quality

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option A: Freshwater · Topics · Topic 3 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand the causes of water scarcity, the environmental consequences of agriculture, pressures on lakes and aquifers, and conflict over shared water.

Syllabus Statement

Physical and economic water scarcity; irrigation, salinization and eutrophication; the Aral Sea and the Ogallala Aquifer; internationally shared water and the Nile basin.

Success criterion 1

Water scarcity: Physical and economic water scarcity

  • Physical water scarcity: there is not enough water to meet demand, often in arid areas (for example the Middle East, North Africa and the south-western USA).
  • Economic water scarcity: water is available, but people cannot access it because of a lack of money, infrastructure or good governance (for example much of sub-Saharan Africa).
IWMI categoryMeaning

Physical water scarcityMore than 75% of river flows withdrawn for agriculture, industry and homes
Economic water scarcityLess than 25% withdrawn, but malnutrition exists and there is a lack of investment or skills to use the water
Success criterion 1

Water scarcity: Causes of drought and scarcity

  • Shift of the ITCZ: the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone brings seasonal rain. If it fails to move as far north as usual, the Sahel misses its rainy season and drought follows.
  • El Niño (ENSO): changes in Pacific ocean currents shift rainfall, bringing drought to Australia, Indonesia and southern Africa while causing floods in Peru.
  • Global warming: higher evaporation, less snowpack, shrinking glaciers and more extreme droughts.
  • Population growth and development: more people, more irrigation and richer diets increase demand.
Success criterion 1

Water scarcity: Human changes to the water system

Humans have changed the global water system by building dams, draining wetlands, irrigating farmland and extracting groundwater. Globally about 70% of freshwater withdrawal goes to agriculture, about 19–20% to industry and about 11% to domestic use. Water scarcity links to SDG 6 (clean water and sanitation), as well as SDG 2 (zero hunger) and SDG 13 (climate action).

Success criterion 2

Agriculture and water: Salinization and eutrophication

Salinization is the build-up of salts in soil. In hot, dry climates, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salts behind; over-irrigation also raises the water table, drawing salty groundwater towards the surface by capillary action. Salty soils reduce crop yields and can make land useless (for example parts of Pakistan, Australia and the Aral Sea basin).

Eutrophication: fertilisers (nitrates and phosphates) run off fields into rivers and lakes → rapid algal blooms → algae die and are decomposed by bacteria → bacteria use up the dissolved oxygen → fish and other organisms die, creating “dead zones” such as in the Gulf of Mexico.

Success criterion 2

Agriculture and water: Groundwater and bioaccumulation

  • Groundwater: over-abstraction lowers water tables and dries up wells; nitrates and pesticides can seep into aquifers and contaminate drinking water.
  • Bioaccumulation: persistent agrochemicals (such as some pesticides) build up in the tissues of organisms faster than they are broken down. They become more concentrated at each level of the food chain (biomagnification), harming top predators such as fish-eating birds and humans.
Success criterion 3

Lakes and aquifers: How aquifers work

An aquifer is a layer of permeable rock that stores and transmits groundwater. The water table is the top of the zone of saturation, where all the pore spaces are full of water.

  • An unconfined aquifer is open to the surface and recharged directly by rain soaking down.
  • A confined aquifer is trapped between impermeable layers and recharged only where it reaches the surface, sometimes far away. Water may rise under pressure in an artesian well.

Pressures include over-abstraction (pumping faster than recharge), saltwater intrusion (sea water moving into coastal aquifers when fresh water is pumped out) and land subsidence (the ground sinking as sediments compact, as in Bangkok, Mexico City and Jakarta).

Success criterion 3

Lakes and aquifers: The Aral Sea, Central Asia

The Aral Sea was once the world’s fourth-largest lake. From the 1960s, Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton and rice in Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan.

ImpactDetail
EnvironmentalThe sea has lost around 90% of its area and split into separate lakes; salinity rose sharply; most native fish species disappeared; dust storms carry salt and pesticide residues from the dry seabed; the local climate became more extreme
EconomicThe fishing industry collapsed: ports such as Muynak are now many kilometres from the water
SocialHigh rates of respiratory illness, anaemia and some cancers linked to dust and polluted water; out-migration

Response: the Kok-Aral Dam (2005) in Kazakhstan raised the level of the North Aral Sea, which has partly recovered and supports fishing again; the larger South Aral Sea continues to shrink.

Success criterion 3

Lakes and aquifers: The Ogallala Aquifer, USA

The Ogallala is one of the world’s largest aquifers, lying beneath eight states of the Great Plains. It supplies about a third of the groundwater used for irrigation in the USA, much of it through centre-pivot irrigation of maize, wheat and cotton.

  • Recharge is extremely slow in this semi-arid region, so the water is essentially “fossil water”.
  • Water tables have fallen by more than 30 m in parts of Kansas and Texas; some wells have run dry and farmers have returned to dryland farming.
  • Management: local water districts set limits and meter wells, farmers switch to drip irrigation and less thirsty crops, and some areas aim to cut withdrawals to extend the aquifer’s life.
Success criterion 4

Shared water: the Nile: Why shared water causes conflict

Over 260 river basins are shared by two or more countries. Conflict becomes more likely because of population growth, economic and industrial development, expanding irrigation, global warming reducing supply, pollution, groundwater depletion, unclear or unfair sharing agreements and wider political tension. Upstream countries can control the flow reaching downstream countries.

Success criterion 4

Shared water: the Nile: The Nile basin

The Nile flows about 6,650 km through 11 countries. The White Nile rises in the Lake Victoria region and the Blue Nile rises at Lake Tana in Ethiopia; they meet at Khartoum. The Blue Nile supplies most of the water reaching Egypt (around 80–85% in the flood season). Egypt depends on the Nile for about 97% of its fresh water.

Success criterion 4

Shared water: the Nile: The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD)

Ethiopia began building the GERD on the Blue Nile in 2011. It is Africa’s largest hydroelectric dam, with a reservoir of about 74 billion m³, and began filling in 2020.

CountryPosition
EthiopiaNeeds electricity for development (many people lack power) and to export to neighbours; argues it has the right to use water rising in its territory
EgyptFears reduced flow, especially while the reservoir fills and during droughts; sees the Nile as an issue of national security
SudanCould benefit from regulated flow and cheaper electricity, but worries about the safety of its own dams and lack of data sharing

Talks have repeatedly stalled over how fast to fill the reservoir and how to share water in droughts. The dispute links to SDG 6 (water), SDG 7 (affordable energy) and SDG 16 (peace and strong institutions).

Key terms

Physical water scarcity
Not enough water to meet demand.
Economic water scarcity
Water exists but people cannot afford or access it.
ITCZ
The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, a belt of low pressure and rain near the equator.
El Niño
A periodic warming of the eastern Pacific that shifts global rainfall patterns.
Water stress
When demand for water is high relative to supply.
Salinization
The build-up of salts in soil, often caused by irrigation.
Eutrophication
Over-enrichment of water with nutrients, leading to algal blooms and oxygen loss.
Dead zone
An area of water with too little oxygen to support most life.
Bioaccumulation
The build-up of a substance in an organism over time.
Drip irrigation
Delivering water through pipes directly to plant roots.
Aquifer
A layer of permeable rock that stores and transmits groundwater.
Water table
The upper surface of the zone of saturation.
Over-abstraction
Removing water faster than it is replaced.
Saltwater intrusion
Sea water moving into a coastal aquifer.
Fossil water
Groundwater stored long ago that is not being recharged today.
Riparian state
A country through which a river flows.
Upstream
Towards the source of a river.
Downstream
Towards the mouth of a river.
Hydro-politics
Political issues and conflict over water resources.
GERD
The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile.

4. Water management futures

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option A: Freshwater · Topics · Topic 4 of 4

Local and community water management (Ladakh, Jordan); dams and the Three Gorges Dam; integrated drainage basin management and the Mississippi; wetland management and coastal Louisiana.

Success criterion 1

Local water management: Top-down and bottom-up approaches

ApproachExamplesStrengthsWeaknesses
Large-scale (top-down)Planned by governments or large agencies: mega-dams, inter-basin transfers, desalination plantsSupply large amounts of water to cities and farmsVery expensive; may displace people; local people have little say
Small-scale (bottom-up)Planned and run with local communities: hand pumps, wells, rainwater harvestingCheap, appropriate technology, local ownership and maintenanceSupply less water; depend on local skills and funding

Involving local communities improves management because people understand local needs, look after systems they own and are more likely to pay small fees to keep them working.

Success criterion 1

Local water management: Small-scale strategies

  • Hand pumps and boreholes bring safe groundwater to villages.
  • Rainwater harvesting collects roof runoff in tanks; greywater from washing is reused for gardens and toilets.
  • Gravity-fed supply pipes water downhill from springs without pumps.
  • Water point mapping records where taps and wells are and whether they work, so repairs can be targeted.
Success criterion 1

Local water management: Case studies: Ladakh and Jordan

Ice Stupa Project, Ladakh (India): in this cold desert, streams flow in winter when they are not needed but are low in spring when crops are planted. Since 2014, engineer Sonam Wangchuk and local villages have piped winter stream water to spray into the freezing air, building cone-shaped artificial glaciers (“ice stupas”). Their shape means they melt slowly into spring, supplying irrigation water when it is most needed.

Water Wise Women, Jordan: Jordan is one of the most water-scarce countries in the world. The initiative trains women as plumbers to fix leaks and install water-saving devices in homes. It saves water, reduces costs for families and creates jobs for women in a culture where many families prefer a woman to work inside the home.

Success criterion 2

Dams: Three Gorges: Multipurpose dam schemes

Large dams are built for several purposes at once: hydroelectric power, flood control, irrigation, water supply, navigation and recreation. Their costs and benefits can be classified as social, economic or environmental, and as upstream (reservoir) or downstream impacts.

LocationCostsBenefits
UpstreamFlooding of land, homes and heritage sites; people relocated; siltation of the reservoir; increased evaporation; shoreline erosion and landslides; reservoir-induced earthquakesWater storage, recreation, fishing, navigation on deeper water
DownstreamLess sediment, so channel and coastal erosion increase and deltas shrink; fewer nutrients for floodplain farming; changes to fish migrationFlood control, reliable water supply and irrigation, hydroelectricity
Success criterion 2

Dams: Three Gorges: The Three Gorges Dam, China

The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze is the world’s largest power station. The dam is about 2.3 km long and 181 m high, and its reservoir stretches over 600 km upstream.

Detail
BenefitsAbout 22.5 GW of capacity, producing clean electricity equal to many coal power stations; flood control for millions of people downstream; ship locks let large vessels reach Chongqing
Social costsAbout 1.3 million people relocated; towns, farmland and archaeological sites flooded
Environmental costsSediment trapped in the reservoir, reducing sediment reaching the delta near Shanghai; landslides and bank collapse along the reservoir; pollution builds up in slow-moving water; habitats lost (the Yangtze river dolphin is now considered extinct)
EconomicCost of about US$30 billion or more; boosted industry and trade inland
Success criterion 2

Dams: Three Gorges: Evaluating the scheme

The dam has met its energy and navigation goals and reduced flood peaks, but it could not prevent major floods in 2020, and its social and environmental costs have been very high. Many geographers argue that smaller schemes, or combining dams with floodplain management, could deliver benefits with fewer costs.

Activity: upstream or downstream?

Sort each impact of a large dam.

  • Villages flooded by the reservoir
  • Delta shrinks because of less sediment
  • Silt builds up behind the dam
  • Fewer floods on the plain
  • Landslides along the reservoir banks
  • Less fertile silt on farmland

Success criterion 3

IDBM: the Mississippi: What is IDBM?

Integrated drainage basin management (IDBM) plans the whole basin as one connected system, bringing together all stakeholders (governments at every level, farmers, industry, cities, environmental groups and local people) and all water uses: supply, flood control, navigation, farming, ecosystems and recreation.

ApproachWhat it involvesResult
Older single-purpose approachOne goal (e.g. navigation or flood control) managed by one agency, often in one section of the riverSolving one problem can create others downstream
IDBMMany goals balanced across the whole basin, with shared data and decisionsConsiders upstream–downstream links and long-term sustainability
Success criterion 3

IDBM: the Mississippi: Costs and benefits of IDBM

  • Benefits: reduces conflict between users; tackles the causes of problems (such as farm runoff) rather than only their symptoms; protects ecosystems; supports sustainable development and climate adaptation.
  • Costs and challenges: very complex to coordinate; slow decision-making; needs long-term funding; depends on cooperation across political boundaries; benefits can be hard to measure.
Success criterion 3

IDBM: the Mississippi: America’s Watershed Initiative (Mississippi basin)

The Mississippi basin drains 31 US states (about 40% of the lower 48 states). It supports farming, river shipping, cities and wildlife, but faces flooding, nutrient pollution (causing the dead zone in the Gulf of Mexico), habitat loss and ageing infrastructure.

America’s Watershed Initiative is a collaboration of businesses, farmers, navigation interests, conservation groups and governments across the basin. It publishes report cards grading the basin on goals such as ecosystems, flood control, transport, water supply, the economy and recreation. Its report cards have given the basin low overall grades, highlighting the need for more investment and cooperation.

Evaluation: it has improved communication and shared data across a huge basin, but it has no legal power, depends on voluntary cooperation, and progress on problems like nutrient runoff has been slow.

Success criterion 4

Wetlands: Louisiana: Wetlands and their value

A wetland is land that is saturated or covered by shallow water for at least part of the year. Types include marine (coastal reefs and shores), coastal (estuaries, salt marshes, mangroves), inland (marshes, swamps, peatlands, floodplains) and artificial (reservoirs, rice paddies, fishponds).

ValueHow wetlands provide it
Water qualityPlants and soils filter sediment, nutrients and pollutants
Water supplyRecharge groundwater and store water for dry seasons
Flood protectionAct like sponges, storing flood water and reducing peak discharge
Erosion controlVegetation stabilises banks and coastlines and absorbs storm surges
HabitatBreeding grounds for fish, birds and other wildlife
Recreation, culture and commerceTourism, fishing, timber, reeds and peat
Success criterion 4

Wetlands: Louisiana: Pressures and the Ramsar Convention

Wetlands are drained for farming and building, polluted, starved of sediment by dams and levees, and threatened by sea-level rise. Large areas of the world’s wetlands have been lost since 1900.

The Ramsar Convention (1971) is an international treaty for the conservation and wise use of wetlands. Its three pillars are: work towards the wise use of all wetlands; designate Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar sites) and manage them well; and cooperate internationally on shared wetlands and species.

Success criterion 4

Wetlands: Louisiana: Louisiana’s disappearing coastal wetlands

Coastal Louisiana has lost about 5,000 km² of land since the 1930s, an area roughly the size of a football pitch every 100 minutes at its fastest. Causes include levees on the Mississippi that stop sediment reaching the wetlands, canals dug for oil and gas that let salt water in, natural subsidence, sea-level rise and hurricanes such as Katrina (2005).

Coastal Master Plan strategyWhat it does
Sediment diversionsGaps in levees let sediment-rich Mississippi water rebuild marshes, as the river once did naturally
Marsh creationDredged sediment is pumped to build new marshland
Shoreline protection and barrier islandsRebuilding barrier islands and ridges to absorb storm surges
Structural protectionLevees and flood walls around communities

Evaluation: the 50-year, US$50 billion Coastal Master Plan (first adopted in 2007 and updated regularly) is one of the most ambitious wetland plans in the world, but it faces huge costs, opposition from fishing communities affected by fresh water from diversions, delays to major projects, and the challenge of rising sea levels.

Key terms

Top-down
Planning led by governments or large organisations.
Bottom-up
Planning led by and with local communities.
Rainwater harvesting
Collecting and storing rainwater for later use.
Greywater
Waste water from washing that can be reused.
Ice stupa
An artificial cone-shaped glacier that stores winter water as ice.
Multipurpose scheme
A water project designed for several uses, such as power, flood control and irrigation.
Reservoir
An artificial lake created behind a dam.
Siltation
The build-up of sediment, for example in a reservoir.
Hydroelectric power
Electricity generated by moving water turning turbines.
IDBM
Integrated drainage basin management: managing the whole basin as one system.
Stakeholder
A person or group with an interest in a decision.
Report card
A public grading of how well a basin is meeting its goals.
Nutrient pollution
Excess nitrates and phosphates entering water.
Wetland
Land saturated or covered by shallow water for part of the year.
Ramsar Convention
The international treaty for wetland conservation and wise use.
Wise use
Using wetlands sustainably while keeping their ecological character.
Sediment diversion
Letting sediment-rich river water flow into wetlands to rebuild land.

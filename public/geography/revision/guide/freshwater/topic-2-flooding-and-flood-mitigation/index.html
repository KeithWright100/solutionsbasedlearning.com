2. Flooding and flood mitigation

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option A: Freshwater · Topics · Topic 2 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand what controls flood hydrographs and flood risk, and how floods can be managed.

Syllabus Statement

Hydrograph characteristics and controls; natural and human factors affecting flood risk; hard and soft flood mitigation; case studies of the Chao Phraya basin (Thailand) and Boscastle (UK).

Success criterion 1

Hydrographs: Reading a flood hydrograph

A flood (storm) hydrograph shows how a river’s discharge changes after a storm, usually with rainfall shown as bars.

FeatureMeaning
Base flowThe normal, steady flow supplied mainly by groundwater
Rising limbDischarge increasing as storm water reaches the channel
Peak dischargeThe highest discharge reached
Lag timeThe time between peak rainfall and peak discharge
Falling (recession) limbDischarge decreasing as storm water drains away
Storm flowWater reaching the river quickly by overland flow and throughflow
Success criterion 1

Hydrographs: Flashy and subdued hydrographs

A flashy hydrograph has a short lag time, steep rising limb and high peak, so flooding is more likely. A subdued (flat) hydrograph has a long lag time and low peak.

ControlFlashy hydrographSubdued hydrograph
Basin size, shape and reliefSmall, circular, steepLarge, elongated, gentle
Type of precipitationIntense storm or rapid snowmeltLight, steady rain or snow that stays frozen
TemperatureFrozen or baked ground (no infiltration)Moderate temperatures, high evaporation
Land useUrban or deforestedForested
GeologyImpermeable rock (clay, granite)Permeable rock (chalk, limestone)
Soil typeThin, saturated or clay soilsDeep, sandy soils
Drainage densityHigh (many streams per km²)Low
Tides and storm surgesHigh tides or surges block outflow at the mouthNormal tides
Success criterion 1

Hydrographs: River regimes

A river regime is the pattern of discharge over a year. It reflects climate (seasonal rainfall, snowmelt, evaporation), geology and vegetation. For example, a monsoon river such as the Chao Phraya peaks in September–October, while a glacier-fed river peaks in summer meltwater. Knowing the regime helps managers forecast floods and plan reservoir releases.

Success criterion 2

Flood risk: Key terms

TermMeaning
FloodingWhen a river overtops its banks (bankfull discharge is exceeded) and covers land not normally under water
FloodplainThe flat land beside a river that floods naturally
Recurrence intervalHow often, on average, a flood of a given size is expected (e.g. a “1 in 100 year” flood has a 1% chance in any year)
Flood magnitudeThe size of a flood, measured by peak discharge or the area flooded
Flood frequencyHow often floods of a given size occur

Large floods have a long recurrence interval (low frequency, high magnitude); small floods happen often.

Success criterion 2

Flood risk: Natural factors

  • Volume and intensity of rainfall: intense convectional storms overwhelm infiltration.
  • Duration: days of rain saturate the soil so later rain all becomes overland flow.
  • Timing: rain on frozen ground or during snowmelt, or when a high tide or storm surge blocks the river mouth.
  • Basin characteristics: steep slopes, impermeable rock, thin soils and high drainage density.
Success criterion 2

Flood risk: Human factors

FactorEffect on flood risk
UrbanisationConcrete and tarmac are impermeable; drains carry water rapidly to rivers. Urban hydrographs have shorter lag times and higher peaks than rural ones.
DeforestationLess interception and transpiration; soil erosion silts up channels, reducing their capacity.
Channel modificationStraightening and concrete channels speed water downstream, moving the flood problem to places further down the river; building on the floodplain removes natural storage.

Flood risk = the chance of a flood × its impact. Risk is highest where people and property are concentrated on floodplains and deltas, as in Bangkok, Dhaka or New Orleans.

Success criterion 3

Flood mitigation: Hard engineering (structural) approaches

MethodHow it worksDisadvantages
Dams and reservoirsStore flood water and release it slowly; also supply water and hydroelectric powerVery expensive; trap sediment; flood land and displace people
Artificial levees and channel wallsRaise the banks so the channel holds more waterCan fail catastrophically; move flooding downstream
Channel straightening and dredgingDeepens and speeds up the channel so water leaves fasterNeeds repeating; damages habitats; speeds flooding downstream
Diversion (relief) channels and floodwaysTake excess water around a townExpensive; need space
Culverts, revetments and channel enlargementIncrease capacity and protect banks from erosionUnnatural; can block with debris

Afforestation (planting trees in the upper basin) is a cheaper, natural approach: trees intercept rain, increase infiltration and transpiration, and lengthen lag times, although it takes years to work.

Success criterion 3

Flood mitigation: Soft engineering and planning approaches

  • Land-use zoning: restricting building on the most flood-prone land, keeping floodways and parks that can flood safely.
  • Flood forecasting and warning systems: rainfall radar, river gauges and computer models give people time to move belongings and evacuate.
  • Loss-sharing adjustments: insurance and disaster aid spread the cost of a flood, so households and businesses can recover.
  • Wetland and floodplain restoration: letting rivers reconnect to their floodplains to store water naturally.
Success criterion 3

Flood mitigation: Evaluating approaches

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
Hard engineeringProtects high-value areas immediately and visiblyExpensive, can fail, can create a false sense of security and encourage more building on floodplains
Soft engineering and planningCheaper, more sustainable, works with natural processesDoes not stop floods; needs public cooperation; zoning can limit development

Most modern schemes combine both: structures to protect cities, with zoning, warnings and insurance to reduce the impacts when floods exceed the design limits.

Success criterion 4

Flood case studies: Chao Phraya basin and Bangkok, Thailand (2011)

The Chao Phraya basin covers about a third of Thailand. Bangkok lies on the low, flat delta only 1–2 m above sea level, and parts of the city are sinking because of groundwater abstraction, so it is very prone to flooding.

Chao Phraya, 2011
CausesAn unusually wet monsoon and several tropical storms from July 2011; large reservoirs were kept full for irrigation and then had to release water late; urban growth and industrial estates had been built on former floodplains and wetlands
ImpactsOver 800 deaths; about 13 million people affected; flooding lasted from July into January; major industrial estates north of Bangkok were flooded, disrupting global car and electronics supply chains; the World Bank estimated damage at about US$45 billion
ResponsesFlood walls and dykes along the river, pumping stations and drainage tunnels in Bangkok, “monkey cheek” (kaem ling) retention areas that store flood water, and later plans for floodways and better reservoir management

Evaluation: central Bangkok was largely protected, but water was diverted onto surrounding farmland and suburbs, causing conflict. Large structural plans after 2011 were only partly carried out; subsidence and urban growth mean risk remains high.

Success criterion 4

Flood case studies: Boscastle, Cornwall, UK (2004)

Boscastle is a small village at the confluence of the Valency and Jordan rivers in a small, steep, impermeable catchment, so it is prone to flash flooding.

Boscastle, 2004
CausesOn 16 August 2004 about 200 mm of rain fell in a few hours on saturated ground; steep slopes and impermeable slate sent water rapidly into the narrow valley; debris and cars blocked a low bridge, causing a surge
ImpactsNo deaths, but around 150 people were rescued by helicopter; dozens of homes and businesses were damaged and around 100 cars were washed away
ResponseA £4.6 million flood defence scheme (completed in 2008): the river channel was widened and deepened, the car park was lowered and moved, a wider bridge replaced the old one, culverts were improved and trees near the river were managed to reduce debris

Evaluation: the scheme is designed for a 1-in-75-year flood and has coped with later heavy rainfall, while keeping the village’s character, but it cannot stop an even larger event.

Key terms

Lag time
The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
Peak discharge
The highest discharge reached after a storm.
Base flow
The normal flow of a river, fed mainly by groundwater.
Drainage density
The total length of streams per unit area of a basin.
River regime
The annual pattern of a river’s discharge.
Bankfull discharge
The maximum discharge a channel can hold before flooding.
Recurrence interval
The average time between floods of a given size.
Flood magnitude
The size of a flood.
Flood frequency
How often floods of a given size occur.
Impermeable surface
A surface water cannot soak into, such as concrete.
Hard engineering
Building structures to control rivers, such as dams and levees.
Soft engineering
Working with natural processes, such as afforestation or wetland restoration.
Land-use zoning
Controlling what can be built in areas of different flood risk.
Floodway
An area kept free of buildings to carry flood water safely.
Loss sharing
Spreading the cost of a flood through insurance and aid.
Flash flood
A sudden flood that rises within hours of heavy rain.
Subsidence
The sinking of land, for example when groundwater is pumped out.
Retention area
Land used to store flood water temporarily.
Confluence
The point where two rivers meet.

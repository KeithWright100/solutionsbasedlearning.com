1. Drainage basin hydrology and geomorphology

Success criterion 1

The drainage basin system: The drainage basin as an open system

A drainage basin is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Its edge is the watershed, a ridge of high land that separates one basin from the next. The basin is an open system: both energy and matter (water and sediment) cross its boundary. A closed system lets energy cross the boundary but not matter, which is how the whole global hydrological cycle behaves.

Part of the systemExamples
InputsPrecipitation (rain, snow, hail); also solar energy and sediment entering the basin
StoresInterception (on vegetation), surface/depression storage, soil moisture, groundwater, channel storage, snow and ice
Flows (transfers)Throughfall, stemflow, infiltration, percolation, overland flow, throughflow, groundwater flow (base flow), channel flow
OutputsEvaporation, transpiration (together evapotranspiration) and river discharge into the sea or a lake

Infiltration is water soaking into the soil surface; percolation is its slower movement down into the rock; throughflow moves sideways through the soil; overland flow runs over the surface when the ground is saturated or impermeable.

Success criterion 1

The drainage basin system: Feedback loops and dynamic equilibrium

A feedback loop happens when a change to one part of the system triggers a response that affects the system further.

TypeEffectDrainage basin example
Negative feedbackCounteracts the change and restores balanceHeavy rain raises discharge and erosion widens the channel, so it can carry the higher flow and flooding becomes less likely
Positive feedbackAmplifies the original change, pushing the system further from balanceDeforestation reduces interception and transpiration, so more overland flow causes soil erosion, which reduces infiltration and increases overland flow even more

Most basins are in dynamic equilibrium: inputs and outputs balance over time, even though the system is always adjusting (for example, a channel slowly changing its shape after a big flood).

Success criterion 1

The drainage basin system: How humans alter the system

  • Dams and reservoirs create a huge new store, reduce downstream discharge and trap sediment, so channels downstream may erode (a new feedback loop).
  • Water abstraction from rivers and aquifers removes water from stores faster than it is replaced, lowering water tables and river flows.
  • Deforestation removes interception and transpiration, speeding up overland flow and raising flood risk.
  • Urbanisation replaces permeable soil with concrete and drains, cutting infiltration and creating fast overland flow.

Success criterion 2

Discharge: Measuring discharge

Discharge is the volume of water passing a point in a river per second, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second, m³/s).

Discharge (Q) = cross-sectional area (A) × velocity (V)

Cross-sectional area is the average depth × channel width. Worked example: a channel 12 m wide, 1.5 m deep on average, flowing at 0.8 m/s has Q = (12 × 1.5) × 0.8 = 14.4 cumecs.

Success criterion 2

Discharge: Factors affecting discharge

FactorEffect on discharge
PrecipitationIntense or prolonged rain, or snowmelt, quickly raises discharge
Basin size and shapeLarge basins collect more water; circular basins deliver water to the channel at the same time, giving a higher peak than elongated basins
Rock type and permeabilityImpermeable rock (clay, granite) causes rapid overland flow; permeable rock (chalk, sandstone) stores water and releases it slowly as base flow
Land useForests intercept and use water; urban surfaces and bare fields increase runoff
ReliefSteep slopes move water to the channel faster than gentle slopes
Success criterion 2

Discharge: Downstream change: the Bradshaw and Schumm models

The Bradshaw model shows how a river changes from source to mouth. Discharge, width, depth, velocity, cross-sectional area and load quantity increase downstream, while gradient, particle size, load size and channel roughness decrease. Velocity increases because a deeper, smoother channel loses less energy to friction.

Schumm’s model links channel pattern to the type of load and channel stability:

Channel patternLoad and stability
StraightMainly suspended load; low gradient; relatively stable (rare in nature over long distances)
MeanderingMixed or suspended load; moderate gradient; fairly stable, migrates slowly across the floodplain
BraidedLarge bedload of coarse sediment; steep gradient or variable discharge; unstable, with many channels dividing around bars

Erosion, transport and deposition: Erosion and transportation

Erosion processHow it works
Hydraulic actionThe force of moving water hits the bed and banks and compresses air in cracks, breaking rock apart
Abrasion (corrasion)Load carried by the river scrapes and grinds the bed and banks
AttritionRocks in the load knock into each other, becoming smaller and more rounded
Solution (corrosion)Weak acids in the water dissolve soluble rock such as limestone
Transport processHow it works
TractionLarge boulders roll along the bed
SaltationPebbles and sand bounce along the bed
SuspensionFine silt and clay are carried within the flow (makes rivers look brown)
SolutionDissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water
Success criterion 3

Erosion, transport and deposition: Deposition, competence and capacity

Deposition happens when a river loses energy: when velocity falls after a flood, on the inside of a meander, where a river enters a lake or the sea, or when load suddenly increases. The largest particles are deposited first.

  • Competence is the largest particle size a river can carry at a given velocity.
  • Capacity is the total load a river can carry.

Both rise rapidly with velocity, which is why most erosion and transport happen during floods.

Success criterion 3

Erosion, transport and deposition: The Hjulström curve

The Hjulström curve plots water velocity against particle size and shows three zones: erosion, transport and deposition.

  • The critical erosion velocity curve shows the speed needed to pick up a particle. Sand (about 0.1–1 mm) is the easiest to erode.
  • Clay and silt need a higher velocity to erode than sand because their tiny particles are cohesive (stick together) and lie in a smooth bed.
  • Once picked up, clay and silt stay in suspension even at very low velocities, because the settling velocity curve is very low for small particles.
  • Large boulders need very high velocities to move and are deposited as soon as the flow slows.
Success criterion 4

River landforms: Erosional landforms (upper course)

LandformHow it forms
V-shaped valley and interlocking spursVertical erosion deepens the channel while weathering and mass movement wear back the valley sides. The river winds around hard spurs of rock that interlock.
Waterfall and gorgeWhere hard rock lies over softer rock, the soft rock is eroded faster, undercutting the hard cap. The overhang collapses and the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
PotholesPebbles caught in eddies drill circular holes into the bed by abrasion.
Success criterion 4

River landforms: Meanders, river cliffs and point bars (middle course)

In a meander the fastest flow (the thalweg) swings to the outside of the bend. Helicoidal flow spirals across the channel, eroding the outer bank and carrying sediment to the inner bank.

  • Outside bend: fast, deep water erodes by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a steep river cliff.
  • Inside bend: slow, shallow water deposits sediment, forming a gently sloping point bar.
  • Over time the neck of the meander narrows; a flood can cut through it, and deposition seals off the old bend as an oxbow lake.
Success criterion 4

River landforms: Depositional landforms (lower course)

LandformHow it forms
FloodplainFlat land either side of the river built up by deposition of silt during floods and by meander migration
LeveesWhen a river floods, velocity drops sharply at the channel edge, so the coarsest sediment is deposited first, building natural raised banks
DeltaWhere a river enters the sea or a lake, velocity falls and sediment is deposited faster than tides and currents can remove it, building new land (for example the Nile and Mississippi deltas)

Key terms

Drainage basin
The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
Watershed
The boundary of a drainage basin, usually a ridge of high land.
Open system
A system where energy and matter can cross the boundary.
Feedback loop
A change in one part of a system that triggers a response affecting the system further.
Dynamic equilibrium
A balance between inputs and outputs that is maintained while the system keeps adjusting.
Throughflow
The sideways movement of water through the soil.
Discharge
The volume of water passing a point per second, measured in cumecs.
Cumecs
Cubic metres per second, the unit for discharge.
Bradshaw model
A model showing how channel characteristics change downstream.
Hydraulic radius
Cross-sectional area divided by wetted perimeter; a measure of channel efficiency.
Braided channel
A channel divided into many threads by bars of sediment.
Abrasion
Erosion by load scraping the bed and banks.
Saltation
Particles bouncing along the river bed.
Competence
The largest particle a river can carry at a given velocity.
Capacity
The total load a river can carry.
Hjulström curve
A graph of velocity against particle size showing erosion, transport and deposition.
Critical erosion velocity
The minimum velocity needed to pick up a particle.
Interlocking spurs
Ridges of hard rock that the river winds around in the upper course.
Gorge
A steep-sided valley left as a waterfall retreats upstream.
Thalweg
The line of fastest flow in a river channel.
Point bar
A deposit of sediment on the inside of a meander bend.
Levee
A natural raised bank built by deposition during floods.
Oxbow lake
A crescent-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off.

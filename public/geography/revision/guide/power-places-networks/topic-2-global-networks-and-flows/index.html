2. Global networks and flows

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 4: Power, Places and Networks · Topics · Topic 2 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the main global flows and how TNCs network places and markets.

Syllabus Statement

Trade in materials, goods and services; aid, loans, debt relief and remittances; illegal flows; FDI and outsourcing, including the Shenzhen SEZ; contrasting TNCs (IKEA and Tata).

Success criterion 1

Global networks and flows: Trade flows

Type of tradeMeaningExamples
MaterialsRaw materials taken from the earth, oceans or atmosphereOil, diamonds, salt, timber, agricultural produce
Manufactured goodsProducts that have been refined, processed, assembled or packagedSteel, plastics, electronic components, chemicals, vehicles
ServicesCommercial services provided by companies, countries or organisationsBanking and investment, telecommunications, tourism, construction, health, education, defence
  • Growth and slowdown: world trade grew strongly in the 2000s, dipped in the 2008–09 financial crisis and fell in value in 2015 because of China’s slowdown, falling commodity prices and a strong US dollar. It was disrupted again by COVID-19 in 2020.
  • Manufactured goods are dominated by East Asia, Europe and North America. China became the leading exporter and the USA the leading importer.
  • Services are led by high-income countries (USA, UK, EU) and India (IT services). Emerging economies’ share is growing, and trade between emerging economies has risen quickly.
  • Many African countries still depend on exporting raw materials, and sub-Saharan Africa handles only a small share of world ocean trade.
  • US–China trade: after China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, US imports from China rose rapidly, creating a very large US trade deficit (peaking in 2018). Trade has fallen since the US imposed tariffs.
Success criterion 1

Global networks and flows: Aid, loans, debt relief and remittances

FlowFeatures and examples
Official development assistance (ODA)Aid from governments, as grants or cheap loans. In 1970 a target was set for rich countries to give 0.7% of their national income; only a few (such as Norway, Sweden and Luxembourg) meet it. For some countries ODA is the largest inflow of money: in Rwanda it has made up most foreign capital inflows
Loans and Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs)Many developing countries borrowed heavily in the 1970s and early 1980s. Later IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions: cut public spending, privatise and remove trade barriers to attract investment. High interest rates, oil price rises, falling commodity prices and slow industrial growth created a debt crisis: sub-Saharan Africa’s debt rose from US$3 billion in 1962 to US$142 billion twenty years later
Debt reliefCancelling debts so countries can spend on development, for example the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative (1996) and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005)
RemittancesMoney sent home by migrants: about US$574 billion in 2016, more than three times official aid and more stable than private investment. Nepal received US$6.6 billion in 2016, about 31% of its GDP. The largest single flow is from the USA to Mexico; mobile phones and the internet make sending money cheaper and faster
Success criterion 1

Global networks and flows: Illegal flows

Illegal flowPatterns
Trafficked peopleMillions of people are trafficked for forced labour or sexual exploitation, often from poorer regions to wealthier ones, with Europe, North America and the Gulf states among major destinations
Counterfeit goodsFake clothing, electronics, medicines and car parts, much of it shipped from East Asia, estimated at around 2–3% of world trade
NarcoticsCocaine from the Andes (Colombia, Peru, Bolivia) to North America and Europe; Afghanistan was long the source of most of the world’s opium and heroin, until a Taliban ban in 2022 made Myanmar the largest producer; synthetic drugs such as fentanyl

Illegal flows use the same transport, shipping and financial networks as legal trade, which makes them hard to detect, and they are often linked to corruption, conflict and organised crime.

Success criterion 2

FDI, outsourcing and Shenzhen: FDI and outsourcing

TermMeaningExample
Foreign direct investment (FDI)A company invests in another country by building or buying factories, offices or businesses thereToyota building car plants in Thailand; Tata buying Jaguar Land Rover
OutsourcingA company pays another firm to do part of its workApple paying Foxconn to assemble iPhones
OffshoringMoving part of production or services to another country, either in-house or outsourcedUK banks running call centres in India

TNCs invest abroad to cut costs (cheaper labour and land), access new markets, avoid tariffs and use local resources. In doing so they network places and markets: research in one country, components from several others, assembly in another and sales worldwide.

Success criterion 2

FDI, outsourcing and Shenzhen: Special Economic Zones: Shenzhen

A Special Economic Zone (SEZ) is an area with lower taxes, fewer regulations and good infrastructure, designed to attract foreign investment and boost exports. In 1980, under Deng Xiaoping’s reforms, Shenzhen in Guangdong Province became China’s first SEZ.

  • Why there? Next to Hong Kong (capital, expertise and a port), on the coast, far from Beijing so reforms could be tested, with a large supply of cheap migrant labour from rural China.
  • Growth: from a small market town to a megacity of over 17 million people.
  • Today: a global centre of electronics and technology, home to Huawei, Tencent and BYD, with major Foxconn factories, and part of the Pearl River Delta Greater Bay Area.

Success criterion 3

TNCs: IKEA and Tata: TNCs: key terms, benefits and costs

A transnational corporation (TNC) operates in many countries. Headquarters, research and development (R&D) and decision-making are usually in high-income countries, while assembly and production are often in lower-income and newly industrialised countries. A global strategy is a company’s plan for maximising revenue and gaining a competitive advantage through global networks. A supply chain covers sourcing raw materials, manufacturing, processing, transport and all the logistics needed to reach customers.

Advantages for host countriesDisadvantages for host countries
Generates employment; improves education and technical skills over timeExploitation of local workers and resources
Investment, aid and provision of capital equipmentA large share of profits leaks out of the country to overseas shareholders
Develops resources, manufacturing and energyFew skilled jobs for local people; mechanisation can reduce demand for labour
Links the host country to global marketsIncreased imports (notably oil) can lead to foreign debt; products may be too expensive for local people
Success criterion 3

TNCs: IKEA and Tata: IKEA

IKEA was founded in 1943 by Ingvar Kamprad in Älmhult, Sweden. It sells low-cost, flat-pack furniture that customers collect and assemble themselves, keeping transport and labour costs down.

  • Strategy: huge sales volumes of a standardised range, a strong Swedish brand identity, and large stores at the edges of cities, now supported by online sales and smaller city-centre stores.
  • Global reach: over 450 stores in more than 60 markets, run through a franchise system.
  • Supply chain: around 1,600 suppliers in about 50 countries, with China and Poland among the largest; wood is sourced worldwide, raising sustainability questions.
  • Cultural adaptation: smaller items and stores near metro lines in China; local menus in India (no beef or pork meatballs); larger beds and glassware in the USA.
Success criterion 3

TNCs: IKEA and Tata: Tata

Tata is an Indian conglomerate founded in 1868 by Jamsetji Tata. The holding company, Tata Sons, is mostly owned by charitable trusts. Tata operates in over 100 countries in steel, cars, IT, tea, hotels and more.

Acquisition or businessBranding strategy
Tetley tea (2000)Kept the well-known British brand; now part of Tata Consumer Products
Corus steel (2007)Rebranded as Tata Steel Europe, with steelworks such as Port Talbot (UK) and IJmuiden (Netherlands)
Jaguar Land Rover (2008)Bought from Ford and kept its British luxury brands; plants in the UK, Slovakia and China
Tata Consultancy ServicesOne of the world’s largest IT services companies, serving clients worldwide from India
Success criterion 3

TNCs: IKEA and Tata: Comparing their strategies

IKEATata
OriginSweden, a high-income countryIndia, an emerging economy
GrowthOrganic: building its own stores worldwideMostly through acquisitions of foreign firms
BrandingOne global brand, adapted lightly to local culturesPragmatic: keeps strong foreign brands (JLR, Tetley), uses Tata where the brand helps
InterconnectionsGlobal sourcing of wood, textiles and manufacturing, distribution centres, stores in many marketsLinks Indian capital and IT expertise with factories and brands in Europe and elsewhere

Exam tip (12 marks: “Using examples, explain the strategies used by TNCs to increase their global markets”): use both TNCs and the four concepts: power (size, brands and control of supply chains), place (where they produce and sell, and why), processes (organic growth, acquisitions, outsourcing, glocalisation, online sales) and possibilities (new markets and future strategies).

Key terms

Global network
A set of connected places linked by flows.
Remittance
Money sent home by a migrant worker.
ODA
Official development assistance: aid given by governments.
Structural Adjustment Programme
A loan with conditions such as cutting public spending and opening trade.
Debt relief
The cancellation or reduction of a country’s debts.
FDI
Foreign direct investment: a company investing in productive assets in another country.
Outsourcing
Paying another firm to carry out part of a company’s work.
Offshoring
Moving part of a company’s activities to another country.
Special Economic Zone
An area with special tax and trade rules to attract investment.
TNC
A transnational corporation operating in more than one country.
Conglomerate
A company that owns businesses in many different industries.
Glocalization
Adapting a global product to local tastes and cultures.
Acquisition
Buying another company.

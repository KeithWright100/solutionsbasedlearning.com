3. Human and physical influences on global interactions

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 4: Power, Places and Networks · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the political, technological and physical factors that speed up or slow down global interactions.

Syllabus Statement

MGOs, free trade and migration controls (the EU); the shrinking world, friction of distance and time-space convergence; transport and communication developments; resources and geographic isolation.

Success criterion 1

MGOs, free trade and migration: MGOs and trading blocs

Multi-governmental organizations (MGOs) operate across many states. Some are international (the UN, the IMF and the World Bank, which lends for development projects such as roads, dams, schools and health systems); others are regional (the EU, ASEAN, the USMCA, which replaced NAFTA in 2020). Most aim to increase trade and interaction between members.

Success criterion 1

MGOs, free trade and migration: The EU and the costs and benefits of trading blocs

The European Union began as the six-member European Coal and Steel Community (1951) and European Economic Community (1957), set up to rebuild Europe and make war between members impossible. It now has 27 members (the UK left in 2020). Its single market (1993) has the “four freedoms”: free movement of goods (no tariffs or customs checks inside the EU), capital (investment flows freely, and 20 members share the euro), services and people (EU citizens can live and work in any member state, for example many Polish workers moved to the UK after 2004). Shared policies include the Common Agricultural Policy, competition and environmental laws, and regional funds for poorer areas such as Bulgaria and Romania.

Success criterion 1

MGOs, free trade and migration: Free trade zones and migration controls

A free trade zone (FTZ) is a designated area, usually a port, airport or transport hub of 10–300 hectares, where goods can be imported, stored, processed and re-exported without tariffs or complex customs procedures, for example the Aqaba Special Economic Zone in Jordan. An export processing zone (EPZ) is a type of FTZ aimed at manufacturing for export, a term now mainly used in China, where such zones must export at least 70% of output. Companies in Dubai’s free zones benefit from 100% foreign ownership, tax exemptions, long property leases, confidentiality and freedom to run several activities.

Economic migrants move for work; migration varies by distance, time (temporary, seasonal or permanent), legality and skill. Governments control it through points-based systems (Australia, Canada, the UK since 2021), visa caps (US H-1B lottery), sponsorship systems (the kafala system in the Gulf) or free movement (the EU).

The USA has sought to reduce immigration, citing jobs and wages, security and pressure on services: border wall construction on the Mexican border, more enforcement and deportations, travel bans, lower refugee caps and tighter asylum rules.

Success criterion 2

Shrinking world and data flows: Friction of distance and time-space convergence

The friction of distance is the reduced likelihood of people using a service or interacting the further away they are, because overcoming distance takes time and money. It is closely linked to transport and accessibility. Time-space convergence is when travel time between places falls, so distance matters less: for example, an Airbus A380 could fly from Paris to Adelaide in about 20 hours by 2013. The world appears to be “shrinking”.

Calculating time-space convergence: rate = (earlier journey time − later journey time) ÷ number of years. For example, London to Edinburgh took about two weeks by stagecoach in the 1650s (around 20,000 minutes) and about 4 hours (240 minutes) by train in 2000: (20,000 − 240) ÷ 350 years ≈ 56 minutes per year.

JourneyThenLaterNow
Across the AtlanticSeveral weeks by sailing shipAbout 10 days by steamshipAbout 7 hours by jet; a video call is instant

Success criterion 2

Shrinking world and data flows: Forces driving the shrinking world

  • Transport technology: steam power, the jet engine, containerisation and high-speed rail.
  • Communication technology: the telegraph, telephone, satellites, fibre-optic cables, the internet and smartphones.
  • Falling costs: budget airlines, cheap calls and free messaging apps.
  • But the world has not shrunk equally: places on major routes and networks have “converged”, while remote and poorly connected places remain relatively isolated.
Success criterion 2

Shrinking world and data flows: Global data flows

Global data flows have grown enormously, driven by streaming, cloud computing, social media and online business. Around 99% of data travelling between continents goes through submarine fibre-optic cables, with the busiest routes linking North America, Europe and East Asia. Data centres cluster in places with cheap energy, cool climates and good connections (for example Ireland, Scandinavia and the US state of Virginia). Africa and parts of Asia have fewer connections, although new cables are reducing the gap.

Between 2005 and 2014, cross-border bandwidth grew many times over, with the fastest growth on routes linking Asia to Europe and North America. Technology helps lower-income countries take part through mobile phones and mobile money (such as M-Pesa in Kenya), online freelancing, e-commerce platforms and outsourced services.

Time-space convergence is uneven: places on major transport and communication networks (global cities, hubs) have converged fastest, while remote, poorer or landlocked places with weak infrastructure have converged slowly. International tourist arrivals grew from about 25 million in 1950 to around 680 million in 2000, a clear effect of faster, cheaper travel.

Success criterion 3

Transport developments: Sea trade then and now

In the 18th century, ocean voyages carried colonial trade: spices, tea, sugar, cotton and tobacco, and enslaved people across the Atlantic, on routes linking Europe with the Americas, Africa and Asia. Today’s busiest shipping routes still link Europe, Asia and the Americas, but the centre of gravity has shifted to East Asia, which now has most of the world’s busiest container ports (such as Shanghai, Singapore and Ningbo), and trade is dominated by manufactured goods rather than raw materials.

Containerisation, introduced by Malcom McLean in 1956, replaced slow, labour-intensive loading of loose cargo with standard steel boxes moved by crane between ships, trains and lorries. Loading costs and port times fell dramatically, ships grew from a few hundred to over 20,000 containers, and global supply chains became possible.

Exam practice (12 marks): “Using examples, explain the relationship between transport and/or communication innovation and reduced friction of distance.” Define the key terms, then explain several innovations (jet aircraft, containerisation, high-speed rail, the internet) with named examples and evidence of falling time or cost.

Success criterion 4

Internet and the digital divide: The internet and its growth

The internet is a global network of interconnected computer networks that exchange data. It grew from US military and university networks in the 1960s–80s, and the World Wide Web (1989–91) made it easy to use. Users grew from a few million in the early 1990s to around 5.5 billion (about two-thirds of the world’s population) by 2024, driven by cheaper smartphones, mobile broadband and fibre networks.

Success criterion 4

Internet and the digital divide: Social media

Social media has grown to around 5 billion users. Facebook has about 3 billion users, and YouTube, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok and WeChat each have over a billion. Growth has been driven by smartphones, cheap data, young populations and the network effect (a platform becomes more useful the more people use it). Social media speeds up the spread of ideas, news, culture and brands, but also misinformation, and some governments restrict it (for example China’s “Great Firewall”).

Success criterion 4

Internet and the digital divide: Patterns of adoption and the digital divide

The digital divide is the gap between people and places with good access to digital technology and those without.

ScalePattern
Between countriesOver 90% of people use the internet in high-income countries, compared with around a quarter in low-income countries
Within countriesUrban areas are better connected than rural areas; richer, younger and more educated people are more likely to be online
GenderIn low-income countries, women are less likely than men to use the internet

Reasons: cost of devices and data, lack of electricity and infrastructure, low literacy and digital skills, and government censorship. Mobile networks and satellite internet (such as Starlink) are helping some places leapfrog fixed-line infrastructure.

Success criterion 4

Internet and the digital divide: Infrastructure, Web 2.0 and digital black holes

Communication infrastructure is the physical backbone of broadcasting, telecommunications and the internet: network nodes (servers, routers, data centres, phone masts) linked by transmission media (fibre-optic and submarine cables, satellites and wireless networks), allowing data to flow between them in digital form.

  • Web 1.0 (read-only web): pages simply presented information, with options to download or buy.
  • Web 2.0 (read/write web): from the late 1990s and 2000s users could create and share content, leading to social media, blogs and video sharing.
  • Growth: internet use grew exponentially from the late 1990s; by 2017 about 3.7 billion people (around half the world’s population) were online, and internet users grew by 82% between 2012 and 2017.
  • Over-the-top (OTT) services: apps such as WhatsApp, Viber, iMessage and Skype now carry most messaging and much international calling, cutting telecom companies’ revenue from SMS, international calls and roaming.
  • Internet of Things: everyday objects (fridges, cars, watches, home devices) with sensors that connect to the internet.
  • Digital black holes: places largely cut off from the internet, such as parts of sub-Saharan Africa, remote rural areas and closed states such as North Korea, which miss out on the economic, educational and social benefits of being online.
Success criterion 5

Resources and isolation: Natural resources

Natural resources are materials and features of the environment that people use. Biotic resources come from living or once-living things (forests, fish, crops, and fossil fuels such as coal and oil, formed from ancient organisms); abiotic resources come from non-living things (water, air, minerals, metals such as gold and iron ore). Their uneven distribution creates interactions: places with resources export them, and places without them import them. Mineral deposits reflect geology: gold, copper and diamonds are often linked to past or present tectonic activity, for example gold in South Africa’s Witwatersrand, Australia and the Andes, copper in Chile and Peru, diamonds in Botswana and Russia, and iron ore in Australia and Brazil. National borders were drawn before many deposits were found, so who owns a resource is often an accident of geography.

  • Oil: the largest proven reserves are in Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Canada and Iraq; the Middle East holds around half. Oil wealth has made the Gulf states globally connected hubs (for example Dubai and Doha).
  • Water: very unevenly distributed; scarcity affects agriculture, industry and international relations (for example on the Nile).
  • Resources can also cause conflict or the “resource curse”, where dependence on one export slows wider development.

Geographic isolation limits interaction because distance, relief or a lack of access raises the cost of trade and travel.

ScaleExamples

Success criterion 5

Resources and isolation: Overcoming physical barriers

Technology and investment can reduce isolation: air links and tourism (the Maldives), undersea cables and satellite internet, mountain roads and tunnels, and pipelines. However, these are expensive, so the physical environment still shapes who is connected. Physical factors usually interact with political and economic factors: Switzerland is landlocked and mountainous but highly globalized because of its wealth, stability and investment in transport.

Success criterion 5

Resources and isolation: Oil: chokepoints, flows and the resource curse

  • Where: most oil and gas reserves are in the Middle East, Russia, North America (including Canadian oil sands and US shale) and Venezuela; oil and gas form in sedimentary basins where organic material was buried and trapped in porous reservoir rocks.
  • Supply and demand are affected by reserves and technology (such as fracking), OPEC decisions, conflict and sanctions, economic growth, prices, and climate policy and the switch to renewables.
  • Transit routes and chokepoints: most international oil moves by tanker, with the rest by pipeline. A chokepoint is a narrow channel on a major sea route, such as the Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Malacca, the Suez Canal, Bab el-Mandeb and the Panama Canal; some limit ship size (the “Panamax” and “Suezmax” limits). They are critical to energy security because so much oil passes through them.
  • Flows created by oil: tanker and pipeline flows, money (oil revenues and investment), migrant labour (millions of migrant workers in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf, who send home tens of billions of dollars in remittances), and technology.
  • The resource curse: countries rich in one resource often grow more slowly, because of price volatility, neglect of other industries (“Dutch disease”), corruption and conflict over resource wealth (for example Nigeria and Venezuela), whereas well-managed resource wealth can support development (Norway, Botswana).
Success criterion 5

Resources and isolation: Isolation, the “tyranny of distance” and Australia

Geographic isolation is often a result of extreme physical conditions that reduce population density and the potential for economic activity; extreme environments increase the friction of distance. Physical barriers have become more permeable with transport developments, and isolation can even attract some tourists. Where resources are valuable enough, interactions develop despite physical difficulty: oil in the North Sea and Alaska made permanent populations and infrastructure viable in harsh, remote places (Aberdeen became the UK’s oil capital).

Australia and the “tyranny of distance”: historian Geoffrey Blainey used this phrase for Australia’s remoteness from the world’s main markets. Being far from the world’s “economic mass” raises trading costs, reduces international trade and limits economies of scale; the USA has around 15 times Australia’s population. Gravity trade models suggest Australia’s trade is actually strong once distance is allowed for. OECD research suggested that a 10 percentage point rise in trade exposure could raise output per person by about 4%, and the IMF found isolation had significantly held back growth in New Zealand.

Exam practice (16 marks): “Political factors are the most important influence on global interactions. Discuss this statement.” Weigh political factors (MGOs, trade policy, migration controls, sanctions) against physical factors (resources, isolation) and technological factors (transport and communications), with examples, and reach a supported conclusion.

Key terms

MGO
A multi-governmental organization of cooperating states.
Free trade area
A group of countries that remove tariffs and quotas between them.
Customs union
A free trade area with a common external tariff.
Common market
A customs union with free movement of labour and capital.
Free trade zone
A designated area where goods can be imported and re-exported without tariffs.
Trade deflection
Routing imports through the member with the lowest tariff to avoid higher tariffs.
Friction of distance
The way distance reduces interaction because of time, cost and effort.
Time-space convergence
The reduction in travel or communication time between places.
Shrinking world
The idea that places feel closer as technology reduces travel and communication time.
Submarine cable
A fibre-optic cable on the seabed carrying data between continents.
Containerisation
Moving goods in standard-sized steel containers that transfer easily between ships, trains and lorries.
TEU
Twenty-foot equivalent unit: the standard measure of container capacity.
High-speed rail
Rail services running at over 250 km/h.
Wide-body aircraft
A large passenger plane with two aisles, such as the Boeing 747.
Internet
A global network of interconnected computer networks.
Digital divide
The gap between those with and without good access to digital technology.
Network effect
When a service becomes more valuable as more people use it.
Leapfrogging
Skipping an older technology and adopting a newer one directly.
Natural resource
A material or feature of the environment that people use.
Geographic isolation
Separation from other places that limits interaction.
Landlocked country
A country with no coastline.
Resource curse
When dependence on natural resources slows wider development.

Success criterion 1

Globalization indices: Globalization and global interactions

Globalization is the growing interdependence of countries worldwide through an increasing volume and variety of cross-border transactions in goods and services, international capital flows, and the wider diffusion of technology (a widely used definition, based on the IMF’s). Global interactions are the connections and exchanges that make this happen, from trade and investment to tourism, migration, the internet and international politics. Globalization is uneven: some places are highly connected hubs, while others are partly or almost entirely “switched off”.

IdeaMeaningView of globalization
Global villageMarshall McLuhan (1960s): communications technology makes the world feel like one small, connected communityPositive
Golden Arches theoryThomas Friedman: no two countries with McDonald’s had fought a war with each other, suggesting trade links reduce conflict (since shown to have exceptions)Positive
GlocalizationGlobal products adapted to local cultures and tastesMixed
GrobalizationGeorge Ritzer: the drive of powerful TNCs and states to impose their products and influence everywhere, making places more alikeCritical
Global pillageGlobalization as exploitation of poorer countries’ resources and labour by rich countries and TNCsCritical
Race to the bottomJeremy Brecher and Tim Costello: countries compete for investment by cutting wages, taxes and environmental standardsCritical
Success criterion 1

Globalization indices: The KOF Globalisation Index

The KOF index is produced by the KOF Swiss Economic Institute (ETH Zurich). It defines globalization as creating networks of connections between actors at multi-continental distances, linked through a variety of flows, which produce complex relations of mutual interdependence. It has been published every year since 2002, with values calculated back to 1970, and scores countries out of 100 (higher = more globalized).

The version studied in class uses 23 variables in three weighted dimensions:

Dimension (weighting)Examples of variables
Economic (37%)Actual flows (50%): trade, FDI flows and stocks, portfolio investment and income payments to foreign nationals as % of GDP. Restrictions (50%): hidden import barriers, mean tariff rate, taxes on international trade, capital account restrictions
Social (39%)Personal contact (33%): telephone traffic, transfers, international tourism, foreign population, international letters. Information flows (36%): internet users, televisions, trade in newspapers. Cultural proximity (31%): McDonald’s and IKEA stores per person, trade in books
Political (25%)Embassies in the country, membership of international organisations, participation in UN Security Council missions, international treaties

In 2018 the index was revised: it now uses over 40 variables and splits each dimension into de facto globalization (actual flows, such as how much a country really trades) and de jure globalization (the policies that make flows possible, such as low tariffs). Small, open European economies such as Switzerland, the Netherlands and Belgium score highest; isolated or conflict-affected states such as North Korea, Somalia and Afghanistan score lowest. Since 1970 the biggest increases have been in Eastern Europe (after the fall of communism in 1989–91), China (after economic reforms from 1978), India (after liberalisation in 1991) and the Gulf states.

Exam tip (12-mark Paper 3 question, “Using examples, explain how global interactions can be measured”): define globalization and global interactions; introduce your chosen index; explain how it is measured and its three dimensions; give examples of countries with high and low scores; finish with a brief conclusion summarising the key points. No evaluation is needed for this command term.

Success criterion 2

Superpowers: USA and China: Definitions and characteristics

TermMeaning
Global superpowerA nation, or group of nations, able to project dominating power and influence anywhere in the world, sometimes in more than one place at the same time
NationA large group of people united by common descent, history, culture or language, living in a particular country or territory
HegemonyCultural or political leadership and influence by one nation over others
ImperialismA policy of extending a country’s power and influence through diplomacy or military force
Type of influenceCharacteristics
EconomicA large economy and share of world trade, a globally used currency, powerful TNCs, investment and aid overseas, control of key resources
PoliticalPermanent seat on the UN Security Council, leadership of alliances and global institutions, diplomatic reach
MilitaryLarge defence spending, nuclear weapons, overseas bases, a navy and air force able to operate worldwide
CulturalGlobal spread of language, media, brands, values and education (“soft power”)

Hard power uses force or payment (military strength, sanctions, aid); soft power attracts and persuades (culture, brands, values, education). Natural resources, technology and population also underpin superpower status.

Success criterion 2

Superpowers: USA and China: How superpowers have changed over time

  • Colonial empires (1500s–1900s): Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, France and especially Britain used naval power and colonisation to control trade and territory across the world; by 1920 the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world’s land.
  • 1914–1945: the two world wars badly damaged European economies; the European powers declined while the USA and the USSR grew industrially and militarily. After 1945 the USSR extended its control over Eastern Europe.
  • The Cold War (late 1940s–1991): a bipolar world. The capitalist, free-market USA led NATO and the Western bloc; the communist, centrally planned USSR led the Warsaw Pact. Both spent vast sums on a conventional and nuclear arms race and competed for allies worldwide.
  • 1991: economic stress, failed foreign interventions (such as Afghanistan) and political upheaval in Eastern Europe led to the break-up of the USSR, replaced by the Commonwealth of Independent States led by Russia. The USA became the sole superpower (a unipolar world).
  • Today: a more multipolar world, with China as the main emerging superpower and the EU, India and Russia (which is reasserting military and political influence) as other potential superpowers.

Superpowers: USA and China: Comparing the USA and China

InfluenceUSAChina
EconomicWorld’s largest economy by nominal GDP; the US dollar is the main reserve and trading currency; Wall Street; home to the largest tech TNCs (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon)Second-largest economy by nominal GDP and largest by purchasing power; the world’s biggest manufacturer and trader in goods; Belt and Road Initiative (since 2013) funds infrastructure in well over 100 countries
Military and geopoliticalBy far the largest military budget (around 40% of global spending); hundreds of overseas bases; leads NATO; permanent UN Security Council memberThe largest active armed forces; rapidly growing navy; a base in Djibouti and a growing presence in the South China Sea; permanent UN Security Council member
Natural resourcesThe world’s largest producer of oil and natural gas since the shale boom; vast farmland, coal and mineralsLarge coal reserves and dominant in rare earths (most global mining and processing), but imports much of its oil, iron ore and food (such as soybeans)
TechnologyLeads in software, AI, semiconductor design, aerospace and space exploration (NASA, SpaceX); Silicon ValleyLeads in 5G (Huawei), electric vehicles (BYD), batteries, solar panels and high-speed rail; fast-growing AI and space programmes

Evaluation: the USA remains the only all-round superpower. China is an emerging superpower: its economic power rivals the USA, but its military reach and soft power are more limited. Other emerging powers include India (fast-growing economy and population), the EU (economic and regulatory power) and Russia (military and energy power, but a weaker economy).

Success criterion 3

Global groups and lenders: OPEC and global lenders

OPEC (founded 1960, based in Vienna) includes Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Iran, Kuwait, the UAE, Venezuela and Nigeria, which hold most of the world’s proven oil reserves. By agreeing production quotas it influences the oil price and so the energy policies of importing countries, shown in the 1973 oil embargo when prices quadrupled. Since 2016 OPEC+ has added Russia and other producers. Its influence is limited by US shale oil, the shift to renewables and disagreements between members (Qatar left in 2019 and Angola in 2024).

InstitutionOriginsRole and examplesEvaluation
IMF (International Monetary Fund)Set up at Bretton Woods in 1944; almost every country is a memberLends to countries in financial crisis, such as Greece (from 2010), Argentina (2018, its largest ever loan) and Sri Lanka (2023)Loans come with conditions (austerity, privatisation) that can hurt the poorest; voting depends on contributions, giving the USA an effective veto
NDB (New Development Bank)Set up by the BRICS countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) in 2014; based in ShanghaiFunds infrastructure and renewable energy in emerging economiesFounders have equal votes; an alternative to Western-led lenders, but much smaller than the IMF or World Bank
Success criterion 3

Global groups and lenders: Power at different scales and a changing map

Wealthy and powerful places exist at every scale, and the global map of power is complex and changing.

ScaleExamples
GlobalSuperpowers (USA, China) and global groups (G7, G20, UN Security Council)
RegionalBlocs such as the EU and ASEAN; regional powers such as Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Nigeria and India
NationalCapital cities and core regions, such as Bangkok within Thailand or south-east England within the UK
LocalGlobal cities and financial districts, such as the City of London, Manhattan, Tokyo and Singapore, which can be more connected to each other than to their own countries’ rural areas
  • The map is changing: the G7’s share of world GDP has fallen as emerging economies have grown; BRICS expanded in 2024 to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the UAE (and Indonesia in 2025).
  • Membership changes: Russia’s suspension from the G8, the African Union joining the G20, and countries leaving OPEC.
  • Complexity: power can be economic, political, military or cultural, and a country can be powerful in one way but not others.

Key terms

Globalization
The growing interdependence of countries through cross-border flows of goods, services, capital and technology.
KOF index
An annual index scoring countries’ economic, social and political globalization out of 100.
De facto globalization
Actual flows and activities, such as trade or migration.
De jure globalization
Policies and conditions that make flows possible, such as low tariffs.
Race to the bottom
Countries cutting wages, taxes and standards to attract investment.
Global village
McLuhan’s idea that technology makes the world feel like one community.
Superpower
A nation able to project dominating power and influence anywhere in the world.
Hegemony
Cultural or political leadership and influence by one nation over others.
Imperialism
Extending a country’s power through diplomacy or military force.
Hard power
Influence through military force or economic pressure.
Soft power
Influence through attraction: culture, values and brands.
Multipolar world
A world with several centres of power.
G7
A group of seven leading high-income democracies.
G20
A forum of 19 major economies plus the EU and African Union.
OPEC
An organisation of oil-exporting countries that coordinates production.
IMF
The International Monetary Fund, a global lender to countries in crisis.
NDB
The New Development Bank set up by the BRICS countries.
BRICS
A group of emerging economies: Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa and newer members.

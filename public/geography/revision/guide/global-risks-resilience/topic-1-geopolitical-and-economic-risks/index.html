1. Geopolitical and economic risks

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 6: Global Risks and Resilience · Topics · Topic 1 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the threats that global interactions create for individuals, businesses and states.

Syllabus Statement

Hacking, identity theft and surveillance; political, economic and physical risks to the oil supply chain; profit repatriation, tax avoidance (Apple and Ireland), drones and 3D printing; globalization and nationalism (Brexit, North Korea and Yemen).

Laws such as the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, 2018) try to protect personal data.

Success criterion 1

Cyber threats and supply chains: Risks to global supply chains: oil

A global supply chain links every stage from raw material to consumer across many countries. The oil supply chain runs from upstream (exploration and extraction), through midstream (pipelines, tankers and storage) to downstream (refining and distribution of fuels and petrochemicals). Much of the world’s oil passes through a few narrow chokepoints, such as the Strait of Hormuz (around a fifth of world oil consumption), the Strait of Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb and the Suez Canal.

Success criterion 1

Cyber threats and supply chains: Hackers, whistleblowers, oil and OPEC

Hacking is gaining unauthorised access to computers or networks. A black hat hacker does this for personal gain or to cause harm, while a white hat hacker tests systems to improve security. Cybercrime is increasing because more of life, money and data are online, attacks are cheap and anonymous, and criminals can operate from countries where they are unlikely to be prosecuted. Responses to identity theft include two-factor authentication, data-protection laws, encryption and fraud monitoring by banks.

Governments can use surveillance data to fight crime and terrorism, track disease, monitor opponents and control protest. Whistleblowers expose wrongdoing inside organisations. WikiLeaks, founded by Julian Assange in 2006, published leaked documents, including US military and diplomatic files passed to it by Chelsea Manning in 2010.

Why oil matters: oil provides around a third of the world’s primary energy and fuels nearly all transport, as well as plastics, fertilisers and chemicals. Oil exports make up most government revenue in countries such as Saudi Arabia, Iraq and Nigeria, so price changes affect national incomes. OPEC, and the wider OPEC+ group including Russia, influences prices by setting production quotas: cutting output raises prices, while increasing output lowers them.

Success criterion 2

Tax avoidance and new technology: Sovereignty, profit repatriation and tax avoidance

Sovereignty is a state’s authority to govern its own territory and affairs without outside interference. Globalisation creates new threats to it.

  • Profit repatriation: TNCs send profits made abroad back to their home country, or to low-tax locations, instead of reinvesting them where they were earned.
  • Tax avoidance is legally reducing tax by using loopholes, for example by moving profits to subsidiaries in low-tax countries. Tax evasion (hiding income) is illegal. Wealthy individuals also use offshore accounts, as revealed by the Panama Papers leak (2016).
  • The result is lost tax revenue and less control over the national economy, especially for lower-income countries.
Success criterion 2

Tax avoidance and new technology: Case study: Apple and Ireland

Ireland’s low corporate tax rate (12.5%) and special arrangements attracted many US technology TNCs. In 2016 the European Commission ruled that Ireland had given Apple illegal state aid through tax rulings that let it pay an extremely low effective tax rate on profits from sales across Europe, and ordered Ireland to recover about €13 billion. Ireland and Apple appealed; a lower EU court sided with them in 2020, but the EU’s highest court upheld the Commission’s decision in 2024.

In response to tax avoidance, around 140 countries agreed in 2021 to an OECD plan for a global minimum corporate tax rate of 15%.

Success criterion 2

Tax avoidance and new technology: Corporate tax, tax havens and essay planning

Corporate tax rates vary widely across the EU, from about 9% in Hungary and 12.5% in Ireland to around 25% or more in France, Germany and Malta, so countries compete to attract TNCs. A tax haven is a country or territory where companies and individuals pay little tax, often with secrecy over who owns what, such as the Cayman Islands, Bermuda and the British Virgin Islands. The G20 and OECD are pressuring tax havens to share information, and some governments offer tax amnesties to bring undeclared money back.

How Apple paid little tax: Apple placed the rights to its intellectual property in Irish subsidiaries, so profits from sales outside the Americas were recorded in Ireland; under arrangements known as the “Double Irish”, some of these companies were not tax resident anywhere, and US tax was deferred because the profits were kept offshore. Ireland closed the arrangement for new companies in 2015.

Essay plan (12 marks: “Examine how the political and economic sovereignty of states can be reduced as a result of new technologies”): define political and economic sovereignty and new technologies, then examine drones (surveillance, attacks, smuggling, airspace control) and 3D printing (intellectual property rights, patents, trademarks, untraceable weapons, lost customs revenue). Use key terms such as destabilising, intelligence gathering and copyright, and conclude by judging how far states can respond through regulation.

Success criterion 3

Globalization and nationalism: Nationalism and tribalization

Nationalism is strong loyalty to one’s own nation, often putting national interests above international cooperation. Tribalization is the splitting of identity into smaller groups based on ethnicity, religion, region or political tribe. A correlation has been observed between increasing globalization and a nationalist backlash: people who feel they have lost out from job losses, migration, cultural change or loss of control may support leaders and movements that promise to “take back control”.

Success criterion 3

Globalization and nationalism: A nationalist backlash against globalization: Brexit (UK)

Brexit is a clear example of the anti-globalization backlash described above. It is not isolationism in the North Korean sense: the UK remains a highly globalized, open economy. Instead it shows a rejection of one form of global integration, membership of the European Union, driven by nationalist feelings about sovereignty and identity.

  • The vote: in the June 2016 referendum, 51.9% voted to leave the EU; the UK left in January 2020 and the transition period ended in December 2020. The Leave campaign’s slogan was “Take back control”.
  • Anti-globalization drivers: concern about sovereignty (EU laws and courts), immigration through EU free movement, and economic change. Many deindustrialised “left behind” towns in the North and Midlands of England and in Wales voted Leave, while globally connected London, Scotland and Northern Ireland voted Remain, showing that people who felt they had lost out from globalization were more likely to reject it.
  • Consequences: the end of free movement and a new points-based immigration system (2021), new trade barriers and paperwork with the EU, and political tensions over Northern Ireland.
  • Nuance: after Brexit the UK promoted “Global Britain”, signed new trade deals and joined the CPTPP Pacific trade bloc (2024), and net migration actually rose to record levels. Brexit therefore reflects nationalism and a demand for national control over global flows, rather than a complete withdrawal from globalization.
Success criterion 3

Globalization and nationalism: Isolationism: North Korea

North Korea is the classic example of an isolationist state, and it scores at or near the bottom of globalization indices.

  • Ideology: the state ideology of Juche (self-reliance), developed under Kim Il-sung, rejects dependence on other countries.
  • Economic isolation: very little foreign trade or investment; China accounts for the vast majority of its trade; UN sanctions since its first nuclear test in 2006 restrict exports and imports further.
  • Social and informational isolation: ordinary citizens cannot access the global internet (only a closed national intranet), foreign media is banned, and travel abroad is tightly controlled. Its borders were sealed from 2020 to 2023 during COVID-19.
  • Political isolation: few diplomatic relationships; the main partners are China and, increasingly, Russia.
  • Consequences: one of the poorest countries in Asia, with a famine in the 1990s that killed hundreds of thousands; a sharp contrast with highly globalized South Korea.

North Korea’s isolation comes mainly from ideology and regime security, not from a backlash against globalization: it has never been strongly connected to global flows in the first place.

Success criterion 3

Globalization and nationalism: Global rivalries in a local conflict: Yemen

Yemen shows how a local conflict becomes entangled with regional and global rivalries. Internal divisions (north and south, tribal and religious differences) led to the Houthi movement seizing the capital, Sana’a, in 2014. In 2015 a Saudi-led coalition, backed by Western arms and intelligence, intervened to support the government, while Iran supported the Houthis. The war caused one of the world’s worst humanitarian crises. From late 2023, Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping, linked to the Gaza war, disrupted global trade and drew US and UK strikes.

Success criterion 3

Globalization and nationalism: America First and France

By 2014–15 rising globalisation was matched by rising nationalism, linked to the 2008 financial crisis, migration to Europe and a search for stability in shared culture, history and language. The new nationalism takes many forms: protectionist trade barriers, policies favouring domestic workers, anti-immigration measures, resource nationalism and changes in foreign policy.

Trump told the UN in 2018 that the USA would choose “independence and cooperation over global governance”. In France, the National Front (National Rally since 2018) has grown by opposing the EU, immigration and globalisation (see Unit 5, local resistance).

Exam focus (12 marks: “Using examples, analyse the links between globalisation and the rise of nationalism in some countries”): define globalisation and nationalism, then use three examples, such as America First, Brexit and the National Front in France. For each, link a specific effect of globalisation (job losses from offshoring, migration, loss of sovereignty to international bodies) to the nationalist response. Conclude that the link varies from place to place and over time.

2. Environmental risks

Success criterion 1

The Southeast Asian haze: Causes of the haze

Trans-boundary pollution starts in one country but crosses borders to affect others. The Southeast Asian haze is smoke from fires used to clear land, mainly in Sumatra and Kalimantan (Indonesia), for oil palm and pulpwood plantations and small farms. Draining and burning peatland is especially damaging: peat fires smoulder underground for weeks and release huge amounts of smoke and carbon. Fires are worst in dry years, especially during El Niño conditions, and the south-west monsoon winds (June to October) carry the smoke to Malaysia and Singapore.

Major haze episodes occurred in 1997, 2013, 2015 and 2019.

The Southeast Asian haze: Impacts

ImpactExamples
HealthRespiratory and heart illness; in 2013 Singapore’s pollution index reached record hazardous levels; studies estimated over 100,000 premature deaths across the region from the 2015 haze
EconomicThe World Bank estimated the 2015 fires cost Indonesia about US$16 billion; flights cancelled, schools closed, tourism and outdoor work disrupted
EnvironmentalLoss of rainforest and peatland, habitat loss for orangutans and other species; in 2015 Indonesia’s daily emissions at times exceeded those of the entire US economy
PoliticalDiplomatic tension between Indonesia, Singapore and Malaysia
Success criterion 1

The Southeast Asian haze: Responses and why it is hard to solve

  • ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (2002): a regional framework for monitoring, prevention and cooperation. Indonesia was the last member to ratify it, in 2014.
  • Singapore’s Transboundary Haze Pollution Act (2014) allows it to fine companies, even foreign ones, that cause haze affecting Singapore.
  • Indonesia created a Peatland Restoration Agency (2016) and a moratorium on new permits in primary forest and peatland.
  • Difficulties: the fires are on sovereign Indonesian territory; land ownership and company links are unclear; enforcement is weak and corruption exists; small farmers depend on cheap burning; and global demand for palm oil and paper drives land clearance.
Success criterion 1

The Southeast Asian haze: Other examples and essay focus

Trans-boundary pollution originates in one country but damages the environment of another, crossing borders by air or water, sometimes over thousands of kilometres. Other examples: acid rain from UK and German power stations damaging Scandinavian lakes and forests; radioactive fallout from Chernobyl (1986) across Europe; the 1986 Sandoz chemical spill in Switzerland polluting the Rhine downstream; and dust and smog from China reaching South Korea and Japan.

Exam focus (12 marks: “Examine the causes, effects and responses to a trans-boundary pollution event”): use the 2015 Southeast Asian haze. Give detailed causes (land clearance, peat, El Niño, monsoon winds), effects at different scales and on different groups, and evaluate responses (ASEAN agreement, Singapore’s law, peatland restoration), explaining why they have only partly worked.

Pollution is concentrated along the busiest shipping lanes and around major ports (such as the Strait of Malacca, the English Channel and the approaches to Shanghai and Singapore), where traffic is heaviest. Shipping also emits sulphur and nitrogen oxides and around 3% of global greenhouse gases.

Success criterion 2

Shipping and carbon footprints: Carbon footprints of food

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions caused directly and indirectly by a person, product or activity. Global food flows add transport emissions (“food miles”), especially for air-freighted fresh produce such as green beans or berries. However, how food is produced often matters more than distance: beef and lamb have very high footprints wherever they are produced, and greenhouse-grown tomatoes in a cold country can have a higher footprint than imported ones grown outdoors.

Success criterion 2

Shipping and carbon footprints: Personal carbon footprints

Personal footprints depend on travel, diet, housing and consumption. Flying is especially carbon-intensive: a single long-haul return flight can produce more emissions than many people in low-income countries produce in a year. Footprints are very unequal: the average person in the USA or Australia has a far larger footprint than someone in sub-Saharan Africa, and the richest 10% of people globally cause around half of consumption emissions.

Success criterion 2

Shipping and carbon footprints: Ocean pollution, regulation and the footprint of food

About 80% of marine pollution comes from the land, but shipping adds oil spills, discarded fishing gear and plastics, and pollution is concentrated around container ports and busy lanes. Shipping is hard to regulate because ships operate on the high seas, outside any one country’s laws, and many are registered under flags of convenience (such as Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands) with weaker rules and enforcement. The International Maritime Organization sets global rules, such as the 2020 cap cutting the sulphur content of fuel to 0.5%, but enforcement depends on each country.

The carbon footprint of food is the greenhouse gas emissions (in kg of carbon dioxide equivalent) from producing, transporting and consuming food, including food that is wasted. Agriculture contributes through methane from livestock and rice paddies, nitrous oxide from fertilisers, and deforestation; food systems cause around a quarter to a third of global emissions. It is a better measure than food miles because production usually causes far more emissions than transport. A personal carbon footprint is the sum of all the emissions caused by a person’s activities, usually over a year.

Thinking further: who should clean up localised ocean pollution: local communities, governments or shipping companies? Consider the polluter-pays principle, the global nature of shipping, and the limited power of local action.

Success criterion 3

Industry, dairy and palm oil: Pollution from relocated manufacturing

Since the 1970s, manufacturing has shifted from high-income countries to emerging economies, especially China and the rest of Asia (the global shift). Weaker environmental laws and enforcement have sometimes made these places pollution havens.

PollutionExamples
AirSmog from coal-fired factories and power stations in Chinese industrial cities
WaterDyes and chemicals from textile factories in rivers in Bangladesh and China
Industrial and toxic wasteThe 1984 Bhopal gas leak in India killed thousands; e-waste processing in Guiyu (China) and Agbogbloshie (Ghana) contaminated soil and water

As countries grow richer, they often tighten regulations (China’s “war on pollution” from 2014), and some polluting industry moves on again, for example to Vietnam or Bangladesh.

Success criterion 3

Industry, dairy and palm oil: Agribusiness and agro-industrialisation: dairy

Agro-industrialisation is the shift to large-scale, intensive, mechanised and often vertically integrated farming run by agribusiness. In dairy farming this means larger herds, indoor or feedlot systems, imported animal feed and heavy fertiliser use. In New Zealand, rapid dairy expansion has increased nitrate pollution and eutrophication of rivers and lakes and raised demand for irrigation water, while very large “mega-dairies” elsewhere concentrate manure and methane emissions.

Success criterion 3

Industry, dairy and palm oil: Palm oil

Palm oil comes from the fruit of the oil palm and is used in around half of packaged supermarket products, as well as cosmetics and biofuels. Indonesia and Malaysia produce about 85% of the world’s supply. Expansion has caused large-scale deforestation and peatland drainage, habitat loss for orangutans, tigers and elephants, carbon emissions, haze and land conflicts with local communities.

Responses and risks: the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO, 2004) certifies more sustainable production, and the EU has passed a law to keep deforestation-linked products off its market. But oil palm produces far more oil per hectare than alternatives such as soy or rapeseed, so replacing it could need more land; demand may also shift to markets with weaker standards.

Success criterion 3

Industry, dairy and palm oil: Why industry shifted, and comparing types of pollution

Reasons for the global shift: lower labour and land costs; access to large and growing markets; government incentives such as tax breaks and special economic zones; cheaper container shipping and better communications that allow production to be split across countries; trade liberalisation; and, in some cases, weaker environmental regulation and enforcement.

Palm oil boycotts: oil palm produces far more oil per hectare than alternatives such as soy, rapeseed or coconut, so replacing it could need more land and increase deforestation. Many conservation groups therefore argue for certified sustainable palm oil rather than a boycott.

Key terms

Trans-boundary pollution
Pollution that crosses international borders.
Peatland
Waterlogged land made of partly decayed plant material, rich in carbon.
El Niño
A climate pattern that brings drought to Southeast Asia.
Haze
Smoke and particulate pollution that reduces visibility and air quality.
Ballast water
Water carried in ships’ tanks for stability.
Grey water
Wastewater from sinks, showers and laundry.
Black water
Sewage from toilets.
Carbon footprint
Total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or activity.
Invasive species
A non-native species that spreads and harms ecosystems.
Global shift
The relocation of manufacturing from high-income to lower-cost countries.
Pollution haven
A place attracting polluting industry because of weak regulation.
Agro-industrialisation
The industrialisation of farming into large-scale, intensive systems.
Eutrophication
Nutrient enrichment of water causing algal blooms and oxygen loss.
Palm oil
Vegetable oil from the fruit of the oil palm.

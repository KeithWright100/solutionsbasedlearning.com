3. Local and global resilience

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 6: Global Risks and Resilience · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand and evaluate how civil society, businesses and governments build resilience to global risks.

Syllabus Statement

International civil society organisations (Greenpeace and Amnesty International); re-shoring, crowdsourcing and crowdfunding, cybersecurity and e-passports.

Success criterion 1

Greenpeace and Amnesty International: Civil society organisations

Civil society organisations are non-governmental, non-profit groups that act independently of governments and businesses, such as NGOs, charities and campaign groups. International organisations raise awareness of global risks, lobby governments and companies, carry out research and take direct action. They cannot make laws, so their power comes from public pressure, media attention and expertise.

Success criterion 1

Greenpeace and Amnesty International: Greenpeace (environmental)

  • Founded in 1971 by activists protesting against US nuclear tests off Alaska; now active in over 55 countries and funded by individual supporters rather than governments or companies.
  • Methods: peaceful direct action (such as boarding ships and occupying oil rigs), investigations, media campaigns and lobbying.
  • Successes: its campaigns contributed to the moratorium on commercial whaling (1986) and the ban on mining in Antarctica (1991 Madrid Protocol), and pressured companies such as Asia Pulp and Paper to adopt no-deforestation policies.
  • Challenges: its ship Rainbow Warrior was bombed by French agents in 1985; 30 activists were arrested in Russia after an Arctic oil protest in 2013; critics say some actions are confrontational.
Success criterion 1

Greenpeace and Amnesty International: Amnesty International (social) and evaluation

  • Founded in 1961 by British lawyer Peter Benenson to campaign for “prisoners of conscience”; awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1977; millions of supporters worldwide.
  • Methods: detailed research reports on human rights abuses, letter-writing campaigns (such as “Write for Rights”), lobbying and legal advocacy.
  • Successes: releases of prisoners of conscience, campaigns against torture and the death penalty, and a major role in the UN Arms Trade Treaty (in force 2014).
  • Challenges: some governments restrict it; it closed its Hong Kong offices in 2021 and halted work in India in 2020 after its bank accounts were frozen.

Success criterion 1

Greenpeace and Amnesty International: Campaign examples

Greenpeace: its 2014 “Everything is NOT awesome” video pressured LEGO to end a co-branding deal with Shell over Arctic oil drilling; Shell later abandoned Arctic drilling (2015). It has also campaigned against tuna fishing methods that catch sharks, turtles and dolphins, and against Mattel over packaging made from rainforest timber.

Amnesty International has investigated civilian deaths from the 2017 assault on Raqqa in Syria, campaigned for human rights lawyers and journalists imprisoned in Turkey, and highlighted confessions obtained by torture in Somalia.

Challenge question: if you had 1,000 baht to give to one of these organisations, which would you choose and why? Justify your choice using evidence of each organisation’s aims, methods and successes.

Success criterion 2

Building resilience: Re-shoring by TNCs

Re-shoring is moving production back to a company’s home country; near-shoring moves it to a nearby country (for example US firms moving production from China to Mexico); friend-shoring moves it to political allies. After COVID-19 lockdowns, shipping disruption and US–China tensions exposed the risks of long supply chains, governments encouraged re-shoring, for example the US CHIPS and Science Act (2022) subsidises semiconductor factories in the USA. Re-shoring shortens supply chains and reduces exposure to distant risks, but raises costs and prices.

Success criterion 2

Building resilience: Evaluating resilience strategies

  • Top-down strategies (cybersecurity, e-passports, re-shoring incentives) are well-funded and can protect whole countries, but are expensive and can raise privacy concerns.
  • Bottom-up strategies (crowdsourcing, crowdfunding) are fast, flexible and local, but depend on internet access and may be unreliable or unequal.
  • No single strategy removes risk: resilience is strongest when governments, businesses and civil society work together.
Success criterion 2

Building resilience: Essay plan

Essay plan (12 marks: “Examine the role of TNCs, governments and new technologies in building resilience to global risk”): paragraph 1: TNCs (re-shoring, near-shoring and diversified supply chains); paragraph 2: governments (incentives such as the CHIPS Act, cybersecurity as in Estonia, e-passports); paragraph 3: new technologies (crowdsourcing such as Ushahidi, crowdfunding). For each, explain the risk it reduces, give evidence, and add a limitation such as cost, privacy or unequal access. Conclude on which actor is most important, and whether they work best together.

Key terms

Civil society organisation
A non-governmental, non-profit group independent of government and business.
NGO
A non-governmental organisation.
Direct action
Protest that directly confronts an activity, such as occupying a site.
Prisoner of conscience
A person jailed for their beliefs or peaceful expression.
Resilience
The ability to withstand, adapt to and recover from shocks.
Re-shoring
Moving production back to a company’s home country.
Crowdsourcing
Gathering information or help from large numbers of people online.
Cybersecurity
Protecting computer systems and data from attack.
E-passport
A passport containing a chip with biometric data.

2. Changing populations and places

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 1: Changing Population · Topics · Topic 2 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand how populations change over time, the consequences of megacity growth, and the causes and consequences of forced migration and displacement.

Syllabus Statement

Population change and demographic transition; comparing Thailand and the Gambia; the consequences of megacity growth (Mumbai); forced migration and internal displacement (the Rohingya, Burma; Haiti).

Demographic transition: Demographic indicators

IndicatorDefinition
Crude birth rate (CBR)Live births per 1,000 people per year
Crude death rate (CDR)Deaths per 1,000 people per year
Natural increaseCBR minus CDR (ignores migration); natural decrease if negative
Total fertility rate (TFR)Average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current rates; about 2.1 is replacement level
Life expectancyAverage number of years a newborn can expect to live at current mortality rates
Success criterion 1

Demographic transition: Population pyramids

A population pyramid shows a population by age and sex. Cohorts: young dependents (0–14) at the base, the working-age population (15–64), and elderly dependents (65+) at the top.

  • Wide base, narrow top: high birth rate, youthful, fast-growing population.
  • Straighter sides: falling births and deaths, slower growth.
  • Narrow base, wide top: low fertility and an ageing population.
  • Bulges or notches can show baby booms, wars, famines or migration.
Success criterion 1

Demographic transition: The Demographic Transition Model

StageBirth rateDeath ratePopulation
1HighHighStable or slow growth
2HighFalling rapidly (healthcare, sanitation, food supply)Rapid growth
3Falling (contraception, education, urbanisation)Falling, lowerGrowth slows
4LowLowStable or slow growth
5Very lowLow but rising (ageing)Natural decrease

Fertility falls because of education and employment for women, contraception, urbanisation and lower infant mortality.

Strengths: simple, shows the link between development and population, useful for comparison. Limitations: based on Western Europe’s history, ignores migration, cannot predict timing, and some countries move faster or skip stages because of policies or disease such as HIV/AIDS.

  • Births and deaths both low
  • Wide-based pyramid
  • Natural decrease as deaths exceed births
  • Rapid population growth
  • Narrow base and wide top

Success criterion 2

Thailand and the Gambia: Two countries, two stages

ThailandThe Gambia
DTM stageStage 4, approaching 5Stage 2 (moving into 3)
FertilityWell below replacement levelHigh, several children per woman
GrowthVery slow; approaching declineRapid
Life expectancyHigherLower
PyramidNarrow base, bulging middle and topWide base, narrow top

Thailand’s fertility fell rapidly from the 1970s with family planning, education and economic growth. The Gambia’s death rate has fallen while birth rates remain high.

Success criterion 2

Thailand and the Gambia: Dependency

The dependency ratio compares dependents (0–14 and 65+) with the working-age population. Both countries have dependency pressures, but of different kinds:

  • Thailand: a rising elderly dependency ratio as the large working-age cohort ages, with fewer young people to replace it.
  • The Gambia: a high youth dependency ratio, with many children relying on a smaller working population.
Success criterion 2

Thailand and the Gambia: Challenges and responses

ChallengesPossible responses
Pensions, healthcare and elderly care costs rise; shrinking workforce; risk of slower growthRaise retirement age, encourage savings and pensions, attract migrant workers, support families, invest in automation
Pressure on schools, health services and jobs; risk of unemploymentInvest in education, maternal health and family planning, create jobs to capture a demographic dividend

Rows: Thailand (top), the Gambia (bottom).

Success criterion 3

Megacity growth: How megacities grow

Urbanisation is an increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas. Urban growth is an increase in the total number of people living in urban areas; it can happen without urbanisation if rural populations grow as fast.

Megacities grow through rural-to-urban migration (people seeking jobs, education and services) and natural increase, as migrants are often young adults who then have children in the city.

Success criterion 3

Megacity growth: Global pattern

A megacity has 10 million or more people. Their number has risen rapidly since 1950, and most are now in Asia (for example Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Mumbai, Dhaka), with others in Latin America (São Paulo, Mexico City) and Africa (Lagos, Cairo). Growth is fastest in low- and middle-income countries.

Success criterion 3

Megacity growth: Consequences: Mumbai

Mumbai, on India’s west coast, is the country’s financial and commercial capital, with a metropolitan population of over 20 million.

TypeConsequences
EconomicA major share of India’s finance, trade and film industry; huge job opportunities, but much work is in the informal sector with low pay and no security
SocialHousing shortages: a large share of people live in informal settlements such as Dharavi; overcrowding; pressure on schools, health care and transport; inequality between rich and poor
EnvironmentalAir and water pollution, waste disposal problems, traffic congestion, flooding during the monsoon made worse by building over drainage and mangroves

Success criterion 4

Forced migration: Rohingya: Why the Rohingya were displaced

The Rohingya are a mainly Muslim ethnic minority from Rakhine State in western Burma (Myanmar), a mainly Buddhist country. They have long faced discrimination and were effectively denied citizenship, leaving most stateless.

Waves of violence, especially the military crackdown of 2017, forced hundreds of thousands to flee, mostly across the border into Bangladesh. These are political push factors: persecution based on ethnicity and religion.

Success criterion 5

Displacement in Haiti: The 2010 earthquake

On 12 January 2010 a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck close to Port-au-Prince, Haiti’s densely populated capital. Haiti was the poorest country in the Americas, with poorly built housing, weak building regulations and limited government capacity.

Widespread destruction of homes and infrastructure caused a huge death toll and left well over a million people homeless, forcing mass internal displacement into emergency camps.

Success criterion 5

Displacement in Haiti: Short- and long-term consequences

Short-termLong-term
Immediate need for shelter, water, food and medical care; over a million in hundreds of campsCamps lasted for years; overcrowding and poor sanitation
Hospitals, schools and government buildings destroyedA cholera outbreak from late 2010 spread rapidly
Aid arrived but coordination was difficultSlow rebuilding of public services; continued poverty and unemployment
Success criterion 5

Displacement in Haiti: Why recovery was difficult

  • Limited government capacity and resources, worsened by the loss of government buildings and staff.
  • Unclear land ownership slowed rebuilding.
  • Dependence on international aid, which was hard to coordinate.
  • Widespread poverty and political instability.
  • Hurricane Matthew (2016) caused further destruction, especially in the south-west, compounding vulnerability before recovery was complete.

Key terms

Crude birth rate
Live births per 1,000 people per year.
Total fertility rate
The average number of children per woman in her lifetime.
Replacement level
A TFR of about 2.1, at which a population replaces itself.
Natural increase
Birth rate minus death rate.
Population pyramid
A graph showing population structure by age and sex.
DTM
The Demographic Transition Model.
Dependency ratio
The ratio of dependents (young and old) to the working-age population.
Ageing population
A population with a growing proportion of older people.
Youthful population
A population with a large proportion of young people.
Elderly dependents
People aged 65 and over.
Young dependents
People aged 0–14.
Urbanisation
An increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas.
Urban growth
An increase in the total number of people living in urban areas.
Megacity
A city with 10 million or more people.
Informal settlement
Housing built without planning permission, often lacking services.
Informal sector
Unregistered, untaxed work with no job security.
Forced migration
Migration people are compelled to make by persecution, conflict or disaster.
Refugee
A person who has crossed a border fleeing persecution.
IDP
An internally displaced person who remains within their own country.
Asylum seeker
A person whose claim for protection has not yet been decided.
Stateless
Not recognised as a citizen by any country.
Internal displacement
Being forced from home but remaining within one’s own country.
Vulnerability
How likely people are to be harmed by a hazard.
Cholera
A water-borne disease that spreads where sanitation is poor.
Hurricane Matthew
The 2016 hurricane that caused further damage in Haiti.
Capacity
A government’s or community’s ability to respond and recover.

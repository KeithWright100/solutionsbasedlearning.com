1. Population and economic development patterns

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 1: Changing Population · Topics · Topic 1 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand how population and economic development are distributed at global and national scales, and why the patterns are uneven.

Syllabus Statement

Physical and human factors affecting population distribution at the global scale; global patterns and classifications of economic development; population distribution and development at a national scale; case study of two countries with uneven distributions (Egypt and Thailand).

Success criterion 1

Global distribution: Distribution and density

Population distribution is the pattern of where people live across an area: clustered, even or scattered. Population density is a measurement: the number of people per unit area, usually people per km² (total population ÷ area).

TermWhat it isExample
DistributionDescribes a spatial pattern“Most Egyptians live along the Nile”
DensityMeasures people per km²Bangladesh has over 1,000 people per km²

Mapping population: a choropleth map shades areas by value range (good for density by country or region, but hides variation within each area). A dot map uses dots that each represent a fixed number of people (for example 1 dot = 10,000 people), showing clustering more precisely.

Success criterion 1

Global distribution: Global patterns

  • Densely populated: East Asia (eastern China, Japan), South Asia (the Ganges valley and delta), Western Europe, the north-east USA, and fertile river valleys and coastal plains such as the Nile and Java.
  • Sparsely populated: hot deserts (Sahara, Arabian), cold regions (northern Canada, Siberia, Antarctica), high mountains (Himalayas, Andes) and dense rainforest interiors (Amazon).
  • Around half of the world’s population lives in a small share of its land area; a large proportion lives within about 100 km of a coast.
Success criterion 1

Global distribution: Physical and human factors

FactorEffect on distribution
ClimateModerate temperatures and reliable rainfall attract settlement; extreme heat, cold or aridity deter it
ReliefFlat, low-lying land is easier to farm and build on than steep mountains
Water supplyRivers and groundwater support farming and cities
Soils and resourcesFertile soils and minerals attract people, though harsh conditions can outweigh resources (for example Siberia)
EmploymentJobs in industry and services pull people to cities
Infrastructure and servicesTransport, schools, hospitals and utilities encourage settlement
Government policyPlanned settlement, investment or restrictions can redistribute people

A useful check: a physical factor exists without people (climate, relief); a human factor is created by people (jobs, transport, policy).

Success criterion 2

Patterns of development: Classifying development

Countries are commonly grouped by income using GNI per capita (World Bank): low-income (LIC), middle-income (MIC), split into lower and upper, and high-income (HIC). Other measures include the Human Development Index (HDI), which combines life expectancy, education and income.

  • HICs are concentrated in North America, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea and Australia.
  • MICs include much of Latin America, China, South-East Asia and parts of North Africa.
  • LICs are mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, plus countries such as Afghanistan.

Classifications are simplifications: they hide inequality within countries and change over time as economies grow.

Success criterion 2

Patterns of development: Uneven development and inequality

Inequality is the uneven distribution of wealth, resources or opportunities between people or places. Causes of uneven development combine:

  • historical: colonialism shaped economies around exporting raw materials;
  • economic: dependence on primary products, debt, unequal trade;
  • political: conflict, corruption or weak governance;
  • physical: landlocked location, hazards, disease, climate;
  • social: access to education and healthcare.
Success criterion 2

Patterns of development: The core-periphery model

The core is the economically dominant region, with investment, jobs and decision-making. The periphery is less developed and often dependent on the core, supplying raw materials, labour and migrants.

Investment and skilled people flow towards the core, so without deliberate policy the gap tends to persist or widen. The model works at different scales: global (HICs and LICs), national (Bangkok and rural north-east Thailand) and urban (central business district and outer informal settlements).

Success criterion 3

National scale: Thailand: Core and periphery in Thailand

Thailand shows a strong national core-periphery pattern. Bangkok and the surrounding central region form the core: the capital is Thailand’s primate city, many times larger than any other city, concentrating government, industry, finance, ports and tourism.

Peripheral regions such as the north-east (Isan) and parts of the north and south have lower incomes, more dependence on farming, fewer services and lower investment.

Success criterion 3

National scale: Thailand: Voluntary internal migration

Internal migration is movement within a country’s borders. Voluntary migration is chosen, usually for economic opportunity, unlike forced displacement.

Push factors (origin)Pull factors (Bangkok)
Low farm incomes and seasonal work in rural areasJobs in manufacturing, construction, services and tourism
Limited schools, hospitals and servicesBetter education and healthcare
Drought and uncertain harvestsHigher wages and the chance to send remittances home

Many migrants are young adults; some move seasonally between farm and city, sending remittances back to their families.

Success criterion 3

National scale: Thailand: Concentration in Bangkok

Investment, infrastructure and jobs concentrate in Bangkok, which pulls in more people and businesses, reinforcing the core. Development therefore draws population towards the core and widens regional inequality.

A megacity is a city with 10 million or more inhabitants. The Bangkok metropolitan region is at or around this size and faces megacity pressures such as congestion, flooding and informal housing.

  • Factory and construction jobs
  • Few hospitals in rural areas
  • Universities and colleges
  • Drought damaging harvests
  • Higher wages to send home as remittances

Success criterion 4

Egypt and Thailand: Egypt: the Nile Valley and Delta

Almost all of Egypt’s population lives on a small fraction of its land: along the Nile Valley and in the Nile Delta, with Cairo at their junction. Population densities there are among the highest in the world.

  • The Nile provides water and fertile alluvial soils in an otherwise desert country.
  • The Aswan High Dam regulates the river’s flow, supporting year-round irrigation and reducing floods.
  • The Western Desert and Sinai have very low density because they lack water and arable land, apart from oases and coastal tourist resorts.
  • Internal migration is directed towards Cairo and the Delta, where jobs and services concentrate.
Success criterion 4

Egypt and Thailand: Thailand: Bangkok and the regions

Thailand’s distribution is shaped more by human and economic factors. The fertile central plain of the Chao Phraya river supports farming, but the dominant feature is the concentration of people and activity in Bangkok, the primate city. Mountainous areas of the north and west are more sparsely populated, while migration flows from the north-east towards Bangkok and the Eastern Seaboard industrial zone.

Success criterion 4

Egypt and Thailand: Comparing the two

EgyptThailand

Both show strong core-based concentration, but for different reasons: physical and human factors interact differently in each context.

Key terms

Population distribution
The pattern of where people live across an area.
Population density
The number of people per unit area, usually per km².
Choropleth map
A map that shades areas according to value ranges.
Dot map
A map where each dot represents a fixed number of people.
Physical factor
A natural feature that affects settlement, such as climate or relief.
Human factor
A feature created by people, such as jobs, transport or policy.
GNI per capita
Gross national income divided by population, used to classify development.
LIC / MIC / HIC
Low-, middle- and high-income countries.
HDI
Human Development Index, combining life expectancy, education and income.
Inequality
Uneven distribution of wealth, resources or opportunities.
Core
The economically dominant region attracting investment.
Periphery
A less developed region often dependent on the core.
Internal migration
Movement of people within the borders of one country.
Voluntary migration
Migration people choose, usually for economic reasons.
Push factor
Something that drives people away from their origin.
Pull factor
Something that attracts people to a destination.
Primate city
A city far larger and more dominant than any other in the country.
Megacity
A city with 10 million or more inhabitants.
Nile Delta
The fertile, densely populated area where the Nile meets the Mediterranean.
Aswan High Dam
The dam that regulates the Nile’s flow for irrigation and flood control.
Arable land
Land suitable for growing crops.
Uneven distribution
When population is concentrated in some areas and sparse in others.

3. Challenges and opportunities

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 1: Changing Population · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the challenges and opportunities created by changing family size, sex ratios and ageing, and how governments and societies respond.

Syllabus Statement

Trends in family size, sex ratios and ageing; population policies; gender equality and human trafficking; the demographic dividend.

Success criterion 1

Family size, sex ratios, ageing: Family size

Family size is closely linked to fertility. Globally, the total fertility rate has roughly halved since the 1960s, though it remains high in much of sub-Saharan Africa. Reasons for smaller families include education and employment for women, access to contraception, urbanisation, falling infant mortality and the rising cost of raising children.

Smaller families let parents invest more per child in education and health.

Success criterion 1

Family size, sex ratios, ageing: Sex ratios

The sex ratio is the number of males per 100 females. Above 100 means more males; below 100 more females. At birth it is naturally about 105.

  • Older populations have low ratios because women live longer.
  • In some countries, cultural son preference and sex-selective practices have produced high ratios at birth (for example China and India).
  • Labour migration can create very high ratios, for example in Gulf states with many male migrant workers.
Success criterion 1

Family size, sex ratios, ageing: Ageing

Ageing (or greying) is a rising proportion of older people, caused by falling fertility and rising life expectancy. It is most advanced in Japan, East Asia and Europe, and varies within countries (for example rural areas age faster as young people leave).

OpportunitiesChallenges
Experienced workforce and volunteers; the “grey pound/silver economy”; grandparents providing childcareRising pension and healthcare costs; shrinking workforce; fewer taxpayers; care shortages
Smaller families allow investment per childSkewed sex ratios can cause social problems such as marriage squeezes

  • A population where women live much longer

Success criterion 2

Population policies: Types of policy

A population policy is a government strategy to influence population size, structure or distribution.

TypeAimStrategies
Pro-natalistEncourage higher birth ratesChild benefits, tax breaks, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, housing support
Anti-natalistReduce birth ratesFamily planning, free contraception, education, incentives for small families, sometimes legal limits

Governments adopt them to manage ageing and a shrinking workforce (pro-natalist) or pressure on resources, jobs and services from rapid growth (anti-natalist).

Success criterion 2

Population policies: Examples

  • China’s One Child Policy (1979–2015), anti-natalist: most urban couples were limited to one child, with fines and pressure for those who broke it. Fertility fell sharply, but it contributed to a skewed sex ratio, a rapidly ageing population and the “4-2-1” family problem. It became a two-child and then a three-child policy.
  • France and Sweden, pro-natalist: generous parental leave, childcare and family allowances. Their fertility rates have stayed relatively high for Europe, though still near or below replacement level.
Success criterion 2

Population policies: Evaluating effectiveness

Judge a policy by comparing demographic evidence (birth rates, TFR, dependency ratios) with its goals, over the short and long term, and by considering wider social consequences. Other factors, such as economic growth and education, may have lowered fertility anyway, making the policy’s own effect hard to isolate.

Activity: pro- or anti-natalist?

Sort each measure.

  • Subsidised childcare
  • Free contraception
  • Long paid parental leave
  • Fines for having extra children
  • Larger family allowances for a third child
  • Incentives for small families

Success criterion 3

Gender and trafficking: Gender equality

Gender equality means equal rights, responsibilities and opportunities regardless of gender; gender inequality is unequal treatment or opportunity. Causes include cultural norms, unequal access to education and property, discrimination in pay and employment, early marriage and unpaid care work.

It is measured with the Gender Inequality Index (GII), combining reproductive health, empowerment (education and parliamentary seats) and labour market participation. Inequality is generally highest in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. SDG 5 aims to achieve gender equality. Equality matters because it improves productivity, health and development.

Success criterion 3

Gender and trafficking: Human trafficking

Human trafficking is the recruitment, transport or holding of people by force, coercion or deception for exploitation. Smuggling is usually a paid arrangement to cross a border illegally, ending on arrival; trafficking involves continued exploitation and may not cross a border at all.

Main forms: forced labour, sexual exploitation, forced marriage, domestic servitude and child exploitation.

Success criterion 3

Gender and trafficking: Vulnerability, prevention and support

  • Vulnerability rises with poverty, conflict, displacement, discrimination, gender inequality and weak legal protection.
  • Prevention: awareness campaigns, education, international cooperation and law enforcement, border checks, and tackling poverty and inequality.
  • Victim support: safe housing, legal help, medical and psychological care, and help to return or settle safely.

Activity: trafficking or smuggling?

Sort each statement.

  • A paid arrangement to cross a border illegally
  • Exploitation continues after arrival
  • Usually ends once the border is crossed

  • The migrant has agreed to the journey

Success criterion 4

Demographic dividend: What is the demographic dividend?

The demographic dividend is the potential for faster economic growth when a country has a large working-age population relative to dependents. It opens in the middle stages of demographic transition, when fertility has fallen but the large cohorts born earlier are now of working age.

  • First dividend: more workers per dependent raises output and income per person.
  • Second dividend: people save and invest more as they plan for longer lives and retirement, boosting capital.
Success criterion 4

Demographic dividend: Conditions, benefits and barriers

ConditionsBenefitsBarriers
Falling fertility; investment in education and health; job creation; good governance and openness to tradeHigher incomes and savings, lower dependency, more investment per child, empowerment of womenNot enough jobs (youth unemployment), poor education, weak governance, conflict

The window is time-limited: the working-age bulge eventually ages, so planning is essential.

Success criterion 4

Demographic dividend: Examples

South Korea and other East Asian “tiger” economies are often cited as successes: falling fertility was matched by heavy investment in education and export industries.

Thailand benefited from a large working-age population from the 1970s to the 2000s, supporting industrial growth. But its window is closing as the population ages rapidly, raising the question of whether it will “grow old before it grows rich”.

Activity: helps or hinders?

Does each factor help or hinder capturing the dividend?

  • Investment in secondary education
  • High youth unemployment
  • Falling fertility rates
  • Political instability
  • Growth of export industries creating jobs
  • Poor healthcare for workers

A government strategy to influence population size, structure or distribution.
Pro-natalist
A policy encouraging higher birth rates.
Anti-natalist
A policy aiming to reduce birth rates.

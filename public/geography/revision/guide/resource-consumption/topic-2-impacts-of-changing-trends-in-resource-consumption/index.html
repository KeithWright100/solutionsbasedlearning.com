2. Impacts of changing trends in resource consumption

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 3: Resource Consumption and Security · Topics · Topic 2 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the water-food-energy nexus, how climate change affects it, contrasting national approaches in Nepal and Israel, and the disposal and recycling of consumer items.

Syllabus Statement

The water-food-energy nexus; the implications of climate change for the nexus; Nepal and Israel case studies; disposal and recycling of consumer items.

Success criterion 1

The nexus: Water and energy

The water-food-energy nexus is the idea that water, food and energy are interdependent: action in one area affects the others.

  • Energy needs water: cooling thermal and nuclear power stations, hydroelectric power, fracking, growing biofuels.
  • Water needs energy: pumping groundwater, treating, desalinating and distributing water, and treating wastewater.
Success criterion 1

The nexus: Food, water and energy

  • Food production is the largest consumer of water (about 70% of withdrawals), mainly through irrigation.
  • Farming can harm water quality through fertiliser and pesticide run-off, causing eutrophication.
  • Energy is used throughout the food chain: machinery, fertiliser manufacture, irrigation pumps, processing, refrigeration and transport (about 30% of global energy use is linked to food).
  • Food crops such as maize and sugar cane can be diverted into biofuels, competing with food and raising prices.
Success criterion 1

The nexus: National security and nexus thinking

TypeMeaning
Water securityReliable access to enough safe water for health, livelihoods and production
Food securityReliable access to enough affordable, safe, nutritious food
Energy securityUninterrupted access to affordable energy; depends on energy pathways (pipelines, shipping routes) and geopolitics

Nexus thinking means planning water, food and energy together so that a solution in one area does not create problems in another.

Success criterion 2

Climate change and the nexus: Negative pressures

  • Desertification in the Sahel reduces usable land; people move onto marginal land and cut trees for fuelwood, further degrading soils (a positive feedback).
  • Sea level rise threatens low-lying deltas such as Bangladesh with saltwater intrusion into farmland and drinking water.
  • Stronger storms damage crops, power lines and water infrastructure.
  • Groundwater pressure: stronger El Niño droughts in northern Australia led farmers to extract more groundwater; India is the world’s largest extractor of groundwater, much of it ancient “fossil” water being depleted faster than it recharges.
Success criterion 2

Climate change and the nexus: Contrasting effects

Climate change does not affect everywhere equally. Some temperate regions have benefited from longer growing seasons: for example, record maize and soybean yields in the USA in some warm years. Large dams such as the Hoover Dam on the Colorado River show how one project links water (irrigation), food and energy (hydroelectric power), but falling river flows in drought reduce all three at once.

Success criterion 3

Nepal: small scale: Why small-scale?

Nepal is a landlocked, mountainous country in the Himalayas with a dispersed rural population, many in villages far from roads or the national grid, and limited money and technology. Large, capital-intensive projects are difficult and expensive, so it relies on small-scale, decentralised, community-based solutions.

Success criterion 3

Nepal: small scale: Energy and food solutions

ApproachHow it supports security
Micro-hydropowerSmall schemes on local streams generate electricity for a village: lighting, mills, phone charging, small businesses
Biogas digestersLivestock manure breaks down without oxygen to make methane for cooking and lighting; the slurry is used as fertiliser
Terraced, rain-fed farmingTerraces create flat land on steep slopes, reduce soil erosion and hold rainwater for rice, maize and millet
FuelwoodStill a major energy source for cooking and heating, but collecting it causes deforestation and takes time, often for women and girls

The nexus is visible at a community scale: adopting biogas reduces fuelwood collection, which protects forests, which helps hold soil and water for farming.

Success criterion 3

Nepal: small scale: Threats and comparison

Nepal’s water comes from Himalayan glacier melt and the monsoon. Climate change is shrinking glaciers, creating risks of glacial lake outburst floods, and making the monsoon less predictable, threatening rain-fed farming and hydropower.

NepalIsrael
Small-scale, decentralised, community-basedLarge-scale, centralised, national
Low-cost, low-technologyCapital-intensive, high-technology
Micro-hydro, biogas, terracesDesalination, wastewater recycling, drip irrigation, offshore gas
Shaped by mountains, poverty and remotenessShaped by aridity, wealth and technology

Success criterion 4

Israel: large scale: Natural pressures

Israel has an arid to semi-arid climate: rainfall falls mainly in winter in the north, while the south (the Negev) is desert. Natural freshwater (the Sea of Galilee and coastal and mountain aquifers) is very limited compared with demand from a growing population, farming and industry, so water security has long been a national priority.

Success criterion 4

Israel: large scale: Technology-driven solutions

StrategyHow it works
DesalinationLarge plants on the Mediterranean coast, such as Sorek, remove salt from seawater and now supply most of Israel’s domestic water
Wastewater recyclingIsrael recycles about 85–90% of its wastewater, the highest rate in the world, mostly for agricultural irrigation
Drip irrigationDeveloped in Israel: tubes deliver water directly to roots, using far less water than flood irrigation
Offshore natural gasTamar and Leviathan fields, discovered from 2009, reduced dependence on imported energy and now generate much of Israel’s electricity
Success criterion 4

Israel: large scale: The interlinked nexus

Desalination (water) needs a lot of energy, now supplied largely by domestic gas; desalinated and recycled water supports food production through irrigation. Israel’s approach is capital-intensive and large-scale: it requires major investment, national planning and advanced technology, made possible by its wealth and research capacity. It contrasts with Nepal’s small-scale approach: neither is universally “correct”, as the best strategy depends on physical geography, wealth and technology.

Success criterion 5

Waste and recycling: International flows of waste

Large amounts of waste, especially plastic and e-waste (discarded electronic goods), have been exported from HICs to LICs and MICs, where processing is cheaper and regulation weaker.

  • The world produces over 60 million tonnes of e-waste a year, and only about a fifth is formally recycled. It contains valuable metals (gold, copper) but also toxic lead, mercury and cadmium.
  • Sites such as Agbogbloshie in Accra, Ghana became known for informal burning of cables to recover copper, exposing workers to toxic fumes.
  • In 2018, China’s National Sword policy banned most imports of plastic waste, so flows shifted to South-East Asia (Malaysia, Vietnam, Indonesia), which then introduced their own restrictions.
  • The Basel Convention controls international movements of hazardous waste.
Success criterion 5

Waste and recycling: Reduce, reuse, recycle

The waste hierarchy ranks options from best to worst: reduce (avoid waste in the first place) > reuse > recycle > recover energy > landfill. Recycling saves raw materials and energy (recycled aluminium uses about 95% less energy than new aluminium), reduces landfill and cuts emissions.

Success criterion 5

Waste and recycling: Evaluating a recycling scheme

Germany’s deposit return scheme (Pfand), introduced in 2003, adds a deposit (25 cents on most single-use bottles and cans) that is refunded when the container is returned to a shop machine.

StrengthsLimitations
Return rates for single-use bottles and cans of about 98%Costs for retailers to install and run machines
Clean, separated material is easier to recycle into new bottlesOnly covers drinks containers, not most plastic waste
Reduces litter and landfillEncourages recycling more than reducing consumption

Barriers to recycling: contamination of recycling bins, low prices for recycled materials, complex products that are hard to separate, and a lack of facilities in many countries.

Key terms

Water-food-energy nexus
The interdependence of water, food and energy systems.
Water security
Reliable access to enough safe water.
Food security
Reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food.
Energy security
Reliable, affordable access to energy.
Energy pathway
The route by which energy travels from producer to consumer.
Nexus thinking
Planning water, food and energy together.
Desertification
The spread of desert-like conditions into drylands.
Fossil groundwater
Ancient groundwater that is not being recharged.
Aquifer
Rock that stores and transmits groundwater.
Maladaptation
An adaptation that increases vulnerability or causes new problems.
El Niño
A periodic warming of the Pacific Ocean that changes weather patterns.
Decentralised
Organised locally rather than by a central authority.
Micro-hydropower
A small hydroelectric scheme serving a village or community.
Biogas digester
A tank where manure breaks down to produce methane fuel.
Terracing
Cutting steps into slopes to create flat farmland.
GLOF
A glacial lake outburst flood.
Desalination
Removing salt from seawater to make fresh water.
Wastewater recycling
Treating used water so it can be reused.
Drip irrigation
Delivering water directly to plant roots through tubes.
Capital-intensive
Requiring large amounts of money and equipment.
Arid
Very dry, with little rainfall.
E-waste
Discarded electrical and electronic equipment.
Waste hierarchy
Ranking of waste options from reduce to landfill.
Deposit return scheme
A refundable charge on containers to encourage their return.
Basel Convention
An international treaty controlling movements of hazardous waste.
Contamination
Non-recyclable material mixed with recyclables.

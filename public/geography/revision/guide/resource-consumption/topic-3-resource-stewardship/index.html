3. Resource stewardship

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 3: Resource Consumption and Security · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the SDGs, different perspectives on population and resources, and the circular economy as a stewardship strategy.

Syllabus Statement

The SDGs; Malthusian, Boserupian and resource stewardship perspectives; the value of the circular economy.

Success criterion 1

The SDGs: What the SDGs are

The Sustainable Development Goals are 17 goals adopted by all UN member states in 2015, with a target date of 2030. They replaced the Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) and are universal: they apply to every country, not only LICs.

GoalFocus
SDG 1No poverty
SDG 2Zero hunger
SDG 6Clean water and sanitation
SDG 7Affordable and clean energy
SDG 12Responsible consumption and production
SDG 13Climate action
Success criterion 1

The SDGs: Measuring progress and spatial patterns

Each goal has targets and indicators, for example the percentage of people with safely managed drinking water (SDG 6) or with access to electricity (SDG 7). For SDG 7, access to electricity has risen to over 90% of the world’s population, but most of the roughly 700 million people still without it live in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in rural areas. Progress overall is off track: many targets will not be met by 2030.

Success criterion 1

The SDGs: Projects and interconnections

A project can contribute to several goals at once. For example, off-grid solar and mini-grids in rural Africa or micro-hydro in Nepal (SDG 7) can power water pumps (SDG 6), irrigation (SDG 2), clinics and schools, and reduce emissions (SDG 13).

The goals are interconnected: progress on one can support or limit another. Expanding irrigation helps SDG 2 but may harm SDG 6 if aquifers are depleted, a direct link to the water-food-energy nexus.

Activity: which SDG?

Match each project to the SDG it mainly supports.

  • Hand pumps and toilets in rural villages
  • Solar home systems for off-grid homes
  • A deposit return scheme for bottles
  • Wastewater treatment plants
  • A micro-hydropower scheme
  • Repair cafés that extend product life

Success criterion 2

Malthus and Boserup: Pessimistic views: Malthus and neo-Malthusians

Thomas Malthus (1798) argued that population grows geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) while food supply grows only arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). Population would eventually outstrip food, and be reduced by positive checks (famine, disease, war) unless controlled by preventive checks (later marriage, fewer children).

Neo-Malthusians extend this to all resources and environmental limits, for example the Club of Rome’s Limits to Growth (1972), ecological footprints, carrying capacity and climate change.

Success criterion 2

Malthus and Boserup: Optimistic view: Boserup

Ester Boserup (1965) argued the opposite: “necessity is the mother of invention”. Population pressure drives innovation and more intensive farming, so food supply rises to meet demand.

Evidence for MalthusEvidence for Boserup
Famines such as Ethiopia in the 1980s; desertification in the Sahel; global ecological overshootThe Green Revolution (high-yield seeds, fertiliser, irrigation) roughly tripled cereal production since the 1960s; drip irrigation and desalination in Israel
Criticism: ignores technology and trade; famines often caused by conflict and poverty, not absolute shortageCriticism: underestimates physical limits; innovation is unevenly distributed and can harm the environment
Success criterion 2

Malthus and Boserup: A balanced view: resource stewardship

Resource stewardship is a balanced view: resource limits are real, but careful, responsible and innovative management can meet present needs while conserving resources for future generations. It is put into practice through sustainable consumption, the circular economy and the SDGs.

Success criterion 3

The circular economy: Linear and circular economies

The traditional linear economy is “take, make, use, dispose”: resources are extracted, made into products, used and thrown away. A circular economy keeps materials and products in use for as long as possible, then recovers them to make new products, so waste is “designed out”.

Success criterion 3

The circular economy: Principles, benefits and barriers

PrinciplesBenefits
Design out waste and pollutionLess extraction of raw materials and less waste
Keep products and materials in use (repair, reuse, remanufacture, recycle)Lower energy use and emissions
Regenerate natural systems (for example returning nutrients to soil)New jobs and business models such as leasing and repair
Shift to renewable energyGreater resource security and lower costs over time

Barriers: products not designed for disassembly, cheap virgin materials, high start-up costs, consumer habits and the need for cooperation between many businesses.

Success criterion 3

The circular economy: Case study: Kalundborg, Denmark

Kalundborg Symbiosis is an industrial park where about 17 public and private companies exchange waste and by-products: surplus steam and heat from the power station heat homes and factories, gypsum from cleaning power station emissions is used to make plasterboard, and waste yeast from insulin production becomes animal feed. Partners save millions of cubic metres of water and hundreds of thousands of tonnes of CO₂ a year.

The circular economy links the whole unit: it reduces ecological footprints, eases pressure on the nexus, addresses waste flows and supports SDG 12, putting resource stewardship into action.

Activity: linear or circular?

Sort each practice.

  • Leasing lighting instead of buying bulbs
  • Sending food waste to landfill
  • Composting food waste to feed soil
  • Single-use packaging
  • Using factory waste heat to warm homes

Key terms

SDGs
The 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals, 2015–2030.
Indicator
A measurable statistic used to track progress.
Universal
Applying to all countries.
MDGs
The Millennium Development Goals, 2000–2015.
Off-grid
Not connected to the national electricity network.
Malthusian
The view that population growth will outstrip resources.
Neo-Malthusian
A modern view that population and consumption exceed environmental limits.
Boserup
The view that population pressure drives innovation.
Carrying capacity
The maximum population an environment can support.
Green Revolution
The spread of high-yield crops and modern farming from the 1960s.
Resource stewardship
Responsible management of resources for present and future needs.
Linear economy
A take-make-use-dispose model.
Circular economy
An economy that keeps materials in use and designs out waste.
Industrial symbiosis
Companies exchanging waste, energy and by-products.
Remanufacturing
Rebuilding used products to as-new condition.
Virgin materials
Newly extracted raw materials.

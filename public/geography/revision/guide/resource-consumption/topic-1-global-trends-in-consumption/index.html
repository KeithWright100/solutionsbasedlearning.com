1. Global trends in consumption

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 3: Resource Consumption and Security · Topics · Topic 1 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand global trends in poverty, the middle class and the consumption of water, land, food and energy, and how consumption is measured.

Syllabus Statement

Poverty reduction and the new global middle class; ecological footprints; patterns and trends in water, land and food, and energy.

Success criterion 1

Poverty and the middle class: Measuring poverty

TypeMeaning
Absolute povertyLacking the basic needs for survival: food, safe water, shelter, health care
Relative povertyBeing poor compared with the average living standard in a society
Income povertyHaving an income below a set poverty line
Extreme povertyLiving below the World Bank’s international poverty line (currently about $2.15 a day)

The share of the world’s population in extreme poverty has fallen dramatically since 1990, mainly because of rapid growth in East and South Asia, especially China and India. Progress has been much slower in sub-Saharan Africa, which now contains most of the world’s extremely poor people, and the COVID-19 pandemic temporarily reversed some gains.

Success criterion 1

Poverty and the middle class: The new global middle class

The new global middle class is the fast-growing group of people, mostly in emerging economies, with enough disposable income to spend on goods and services beyond basic needs (often defined as spending about $10–100 per person per day).

  • It was estimated at about 1.8 billion people in 2009, about 3.2 billion by 2020 and is projected to reach about 4.9 billion by 2030.
  • Asia is central: by 2030 it is projected to hold about 66% of the global middle class and about 59% of middle-class consumption, up from 28% and 23% in 2009, driven by China, India and South-East Asia.
Success criterion 1

Poverty and the middle class: Implications of a growing middle class

ImplicationExamples
SocialBetter education and health care, smaller families, urban lifestyles, rising expectations
EconomicMore domestic consumption, so economies rely less on exports (as in South Korea and China); new markets for global companies
EnvironmentalMore meat, cars, flights, energy and consumer goods: larger ecological footprints and higher emissions
PoliticalDemand for better governance, accountability and rights; pressure on governments to deliver services

Success criterion 2

Ecological footprints: Key ideas

TermMeaning
Ecological footprintThe area of productive land and water needed to supply the resources a population uses and absorb its wastes, especially CO₂
Global hectare (gha)A standard hectare with world-average biological productivity, so footprints can be compared
BiocapacityThe capacity of an area’s ecosystems to produce renewable resources and absorb wastes
Ecological debtorA country whose footprint is larger than its biocapacity (a deficit)
Ecological creditorA country whose biocapacity is larger than its footprint (a reserve)
Success criterion 2

Ecological footprints: Patterns

  • The world’s average footprint is about 2.6–2.8 gha per person, but global biocapacity is only about 1.6 gha per person: humanity uses the equivalent of about 1.7 Earths.
  • Footprints rise with income and consumption: the USA, Australia and Gulf states have some of the largest (around 7–8 gha per person); many LICs, such as India or much of sub-Saharan Africa, have around 1 gha.
  • Large, sparsely populated countries such as Brazil, Canada and Australia can be creditors; densely populated, high-consuming countries such as Japan, the UK and China are debtors.
  • The carbon footprint (energy use) is usually the largest component for HICs.
Success criterion 2

Ecological footprints: Calculating and evaluating

An individual footprint is estimated from food (especially meat), housing and energy, transport (car use and flights) and goods and services.

StrengthsLimitations
Standard unit allows comparison between places and over timeDepends on chosen data and conversion assumptions, so results vary
Shows overshoot clearly and is easy to communicateDoes not include some impacts, such as water pollution or biodiversity loss
Links consumption to land and carbonA national average hides inequality within countries

Success criterion 3

Water: Availability, stress and scarcity

Only about 2.5% of Earth’s water is fresh, and most of that is locked in ice or deep groundwater. Availability depends on physical factors (climate, rainfall, evaporation, rivers, geology) and use depends on human factors (population, development, diet, technology).

  • Water stress: demand is high relative to supply, often defined as less than 1,700 m³ per person per year.
  • Physical water scarcity: not enough water to meet demand, often less than 1,000 m³ per person per year (for example the Middle East and North Africa).
  • Economic water scarcity: water exists but people cannot access it because of a lack of money or infrastructure (common in sub-Saharan Africa).
  • Regions such as the Amazon Basin have abundant water relative to demand.
Success criterion 3

Water: How water is used

Globally, about 70% of freshwater withdrawal is for agriculture (mainly irrigation), about 20% for industry (including energy) and about 10% for domestic use. In HICs industry takes a larger share; in LICs agriculture can exceed 80%. Global water use has risen about sixfold over the last century, faster than population.

Success criterion 3

Water: Embedded (virtual) water

Embedded water is the water used to produce a food or manufactured product. Countries that import water-intensive goods are effectively importing water.

ProductApproximate embedded water
BeefAbout 15,000 litres per kg
RiceAbout 2,500 litres per kg
Wheat (bread)About 1,800 litres per kg
A cotton T-shirtAbout 2,500 litres
A cup of coffeeAbout 130 litres

Consumption varies because of diet (meat-rich diets need far more water), wealth, climate and irrigation methods.

Success criterion 4

Land and food: Food consumption patterns

  • Average calorie intake has risen globally, to around 2,900 kcal per person per day, but varies from over 3,400 in many HICs to around 2,200–2,500 in some LICs.
  • Around 700–800 million people are still undernourished, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, while obesity is rising in HICs and MICs.
  • LICs: diets dominated by staple cereals and roots. MICs: rapidly rising meat, dairy, sugar and processed food. HICs: high calories, meat and processed food, with some shift towards plant-based diets.
Success criterion 4

Land and food: The nutrition transition

The nutrition transition is the shift in diet that happens as incomes rise and people move to cities: from simple, plant-based staples towards more meat, dairy, oils, sugar and processed foods. It is closely linked to the growth of the middle class, especially in China, Brazil and India (where dairy rises more than meat). Global meat production has roughly quadrupled since the 1960s.

Success criterion 4

Land and food: Land use

Agriculture uses about half of the world’s habitable land, and about three-quarters of farmland is used for livestock (grazing and feed crops), even though meat and dairy supply less than a fifth of global calories. Rising meat demand therefore drives deforestation (for example soy and cattle in the Amazon), water use and emissions. Land is also lost to urban growth, soil degradation and desertification.

Success criterion 5

Energy: The energy mix

A country’s energy mix is the combination of sources that make up its energy supply. Hydrocarbons (fossil fuels: coal, oil, natural gas and shale gas) still supply about 80% of global primary energy. Renewables (hydro, wind, solar, geothermal, biomass) are naturally replenished; nuclear power is low-carbon but not renewable.

Success criterion 5

Energy: Oil production and consumption

The biggest oil producers are the USA, Saudi Arabia and Russia; the biggest consumers are the USA, China and India. Much oil is traded from the Middle East to Asia and Europe.

Demand: increases (top) / decreases (bottom)Production: increases (top) / decreases (bottom)
Population and economic growth; rising car ownership; growth of the middle classNew oil fields (for example Siberia from the 1970s) and new technology such as fracking and deep-water drilling
Efficiency gains, electric vehicles, carbon taxes and climate policyConflict, sanctions, OPEC cuts, depletion of old fields, low prices

Known reserves are often estimated to last about 30–50 years at current rates, but this changes with discoveries, technology and demand.

Success criterion 5

Energy: Nuclear, renewables and evaluation

  • Nuclear: reliable, low-carbon electricity, but high cost, radioactive waste and safety fears after Fukushima (2011); some countries (Germany) phased it out while others (China) are expanding it.
  • Renewables: solar and wind costs have fallen sharply, making them the cheapest new electricity in many places, but they are intermittent and need storage and grid investment.
  • New sources of modern energy such as off-grid solar and mini-grids are bringing electricity to rural areas of LICs for the first time.

To evaluate an energy source, consider cost, reliability, environmental impact, available resources and energy security.

Key terms

Absolute poverty
Lacking the basic needs for survival.
Relative poverty
Being poor compared with others in the same society.
Extreme poverty
Living below the international poverty line.
New global middle class
People in emerging economies with income to spend beyond basic needs.
Disposable income
Income left after taxes and essential spending.
Ecological footprint
The productive area needed to supply a population’s resources and absorb its waste.
Global hectare
A standardised hectare of world-average productivity.
Biocapacity
The ability of ecosystems to produce resources and absorb waste.
Ecological debtor
A country using more than its biocapacity.
Ecological creditor
A country with biocapacity left over.
Overshoot
When humanity’s demand exceeds what Earth can regenerate.
Water stress
When demand for water is high relative to available supply.
Physical water scarcity
Too little water to meet demand.
Economic water scarcity
Water exists but cannot be accessed due to lack of money or infrastructure.
Embedded (virtual) water
Water used to produce a good.
Water withdrawal
Water taken from rivers, lakes or aquifers.
Nutrition transition
The shift in diet towards meat, dairy and processed foods as incomes rise.
Undernourishment
Not getting enough calories to meet basic needs.
Calorie intake
The energy consumed in food per person per day.
Staple food
A food eaten regularly that provides most energy, such as rice or maize.
Habitable land
Land that is not ice or desert.
Energy mix
The combination of energy sources a country uses.
Hydrocarbons
Fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas.
Renewable energy
Energy from sources that are naturally replenished.
Energy security
Reliable access to affordable energy.
Intermittent
Not available all the time, such as wind and solar power.
OPEC
The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries.

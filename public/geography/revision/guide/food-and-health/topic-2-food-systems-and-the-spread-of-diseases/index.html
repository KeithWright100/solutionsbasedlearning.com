2. Food systems and the spread of diseases

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option F: Food and Health · Topics · Topic 2 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand food production as a system, how diseases and agricultural innovations diffuse, and the geography of vector-borne and water-borne disease.

Syllabus Statement

Energy efficiency and water footprints; types of diffusion and barriers, including bird flu; the diffusion of agricultural innovations; malaria in Kenya; cholera in the DRC.

Success criterion 1

Food systems and footprints: A systems approach to food production

Part of the systemExamples
InputsSunlight, water, seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, fuel, machinery, labour, animal feed
StoresSoil, grain stores, livestock, warehouses, cold storage
Transfers (processes)Growing, harvesting, processing, packaging, transporting, retailing, cooking
OutputsFood (energy and nutrients), waste, greenhouse gases, water pollution

A systems approach lets geographers compare different food systems by measuring the energy efficiency (food energy out compared with energy in) and the water footprint of each product.

Success criterion 1

Food systems and footprints: Energy and water footprints

The cornflakes example: a box of cornflakes needs energy at every stage: fertiliser and diesel to grow the maize, processing and toasting in a factory, packaging (cardboard and plastic), refrigerated or road transport, and the shop. The energy used in the whole system can be several times greater than the food energy in the box. Industrial food systems often use around 10 calories of fossil-fuel energy for every calorie of food eaten.

ProductWater footprint (litres per kg, global average)
Beefabout 15,000
Chocolateabout 17,000
Riceabout 2,500
Wheatabout 1,800
Vegetablesabout 300
Success criterion 1

Food systems and footprints: Comparing sustainability between places

  • Intensive commercial farming (e.g. the US Midwest) has high yields per hectare but high energy inputs from fossil fuels, fertiliser and machinery.
  • Subsistence farming (e.g. smallholders in East Africa) uses little fossil energy, so is energy efficient, but yields are low and vulnerable to drought.
  • Growing water-hungry crops such as rice, cotton or avocados in dry regions using irrigation is less sustainable than growing them where rainfall is plentiful.
  • This links to the water–food–energy nexus: producing food needs water and energy, so using more of one affects the others.

Disease diffusion: How diseases spread

TypeMeaningExample
Expansion (contagious) diffusionSpreads outwards from a source to nearby people, like ripples in a pondMeasles in a school; early COVID-19 in Wuhan
Expansion (hierarchical) diffusionSpreads down an urban hierarchy, from large cities to smaller townsInfluenza spreading between major cities by air, then to towns
Relocation diffusionCarried by people who move to a new place, leaving the original sourceCholera taken to Haiti in 2010
Network diffusionSpreads along transport or social networksSARS along air routes in 2003

Adoption or acquisition is when a person catches the disease; the rate of spread depends on how easily it is transmitted and on the barriers it meets.

Success criterion 2

Disease diffusion: Bird flu (avian influenza)

H5N1 avian influenza was first found to infect humans in Hong Kong in 1997. It spreads among wild birds and poultry, and occasionally to people in close contact with infected birds, with a high fatality rate. Since 2020 a new strain has spread worldwide in wild birds, poultry and even mammals such as dairy cattle.

Success criterion 3

Diffusion of agricultural innovations: Agricultural innovations

An agricultural innovation is a new technique, technology or idea that changes how food is produced. Examples include high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice and wheat, chemical fertilisers, drip irrigation, mechanisation, genetically modified crops, mobile phone weather and price information, and conservation farming.

Success criterion 3

Diffusion of agricultural innovations: How innovations spread

Torsten Hägerstrand showed that innovations spread like diseases: through expansion (neighbouring farmers see results and copy them), hierarchical spread (from large commercial farms and government research stations to smaller farms) and relocation (carried by migrants or extension workers). Everett Rogers divided adopters into innovators (about 2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), the early majority (34%), the late majority (34%) and laggards (16%), giving an S-shaped adoption curve over time.

Success criterion 3

Diffusion of agricultural innovations: Advantages and drawbacks

AdvantagesDrawbacks
Higher yields and incomes (e.g. the Green Revolution doubled rice and wheat yields in India)Seeds, fertiliser and machinery are expensive, so richer farmers benefit first and inequality can grow
Less labour and drudgeryMechanisation can reduce jobs for landless labourers
Better resilience (drought-tolerant seeds, irrigation)Dependence on purchased inputs and debt; loss of traditional crop varieties
Better information and market access via mobile phonesEnvironmental damage from overuse of fertilisers, pesticides and groundwater

Comparison: disease diffusion is usually unwanted and barriers are encouraged, while innovation diffusion is usually wanted, so barriers such as cost, education and poor communication need to be removed.

Success criterion 4

Malaria in Kenya: Malaria and its geography in Kenya

A vector-borne disease is spread by a living organism (the vector) that carries the pathogen between hosts. Malaria is caused by the Plasmodium parasite (mainly P. falciparum in Kenya) and spread by female Anopheles mosquitoes. It is endemic in Kenya, where most of the population is at risk.

ZoneMalaria risk and reasons
Lake Victoria region (west)Highest risk: hot, humid, standing water and rice irrigation; malaria all year round
CoastHigh risk: warm and humid with seasonal peaks after the rains
Western highlandsEpidemic-prone: cooler, but outbreaks follow warm, wet years; rising temperatures may extend the range
Arid north and eastSeasonal or low risk: few breeding sites except after rain
Central highlands and NairobiLow risk: altitude above about 1,800 m is too cool for the parasite to develop
Success criterion 4

Malaria in Kenya: Factors and impacts

Geographic factors: temperatures of about 18–32 °C, rainfall and standing water for breeding, altitude, poverty (poor housing without screens), limited access to health care, and population movement (relocation diffusion as people travel between the lake region and cities).

ImpactExamples
DemographicChildren under five and pregnant women are most at risk; a major cause of child deaths and low birth weight; high fertility as families replace lost children
Socio-economicLost workdays and school days; families spend scarce income on treatment; lower farm productivity; costs to the health system and the national economy

Success criterion 5

Cholera in the DRC: Cholera and its spread

A water-borne disease is spread through contaminated water. Cholera is caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, spread by drinking water or eating food contaminated by the faeces of an infected person. It causes severe diarrhoea and dehydration and can kill within hours if untreated, but is easily treated with oral rehydration salts (ORS).

In the DRC, cholera is endemic around the eastern Great Lakes (Lake Tanganyika and Lake Kivu), where fishing communities use lake water. From there it spreads by relocation and network diffusion along the Congo River, roads and trade routes, and with displaced people, causing repeated outbreaks; in 2017 the country recorded more than 50,000 suspected cases.

Success criterion 5

Cholera in the DRC: Geographic factors and impacts

FactorExamples
PhysicalWarm temperatures, flooding in the rainy season, dependence on lakes and rivers for water
HumanLack of safe water and sanitation (fewer than half of people have basic drinking water), conflict and displacement in the east, crowded camps, weak health system, poverty
ImpactExamples
DemographicDeaths, especially among children, the malnourished and people living with HIV; displaced people at high risk
Socio-economicLost work and income; fishing and markets disrupted; schools closed; heavy cost to a weak health system

Water footprint
The total volume of fresh water used to produce a product.
Water–food–energy nexus
The links between water, food and energy security.
Expansion diffusion
Spread outward from a source while staying strong at the source.
Relocation diffusion
Spread by people moving to a new area.
Hierarchical diffusion
Spread down the urban hierarchy from large to small places.
Barrier
A factor that slows or stops diffusion.
Agricultural innovation
A new technique, technology or idea in farming.
Adoption curve

High-yielding variety
A crop bred to produce more food per hectare.
Extension worker

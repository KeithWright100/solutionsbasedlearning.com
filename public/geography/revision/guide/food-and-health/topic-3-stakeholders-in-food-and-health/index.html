3. Stakeholders in food and health

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option F: Food and Health · Topics · Topic 3 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand the roles of organisations, TNCs and gender in food and health, the factors affecting famine, and the Yemen case study.

Syllabus Statement

The WFP, FAO, WHO, governments and NGOs; TNCs, agribusiness and food sovereignty; gender roles; FAD, FED, governance, media and aid; causes of and responses to food insecurity in Yemen.

Success criterion 1

Organisations, governments and NGOs: Governments and NGOs

  • Government aid agencies give bilateral aid. USAID was for decades the world’s largest donor of food and health aid (for example PEPFAR for HIV, and Feed the Future), but in 2025 the US government made deep cuts and moved its remaining work into the State Department, leaving big funding gaps.
  • NGOs such as Oxfam (emergency water and sanitation, campaigns on inequality and fair trade) and Save the Children (child nutrition, health and education) work at local level, often with community partners.
  • National governments run health systems, food subsidies and safety nets, such as India’s Public Distribution System.
Success criterion 1

StrengthsLimitations
Large scale and expertise; can respond quickly to emergencies; coordinate many actorsDepend on voluntary donations, which fluctuate with politics and other crises
NGOs work closely with communities and can reach remote areasAccess is limited by conflict and government restrictions

Success criterion 2

TNCs and food consumption: Agribusiness and integration

TermMeaning
AgribusinessLarge-scale, commercial, industrialised farming and food production run as a business, often by TNCs
Vertical integrationA company controls several stages of the supply chain, from seeds and farms to processing, transport and retail
Horizontal integrationA company buys or merges with competitors at the same stage, e.g. Bayer buying Monsanto in 2018

A handful of TNCs dominate global food: a few companies (such as Cargill, ADM and Bunge) control most grain trading, and brands such as Nestlé, PepsiCo, Coca-Cola and McDonald’s sell in almost every country.

Success criterion 2

TNCs and food consumption: How TNCs shape what we eat

  • Economies of scale: buying, producing and shipping in huge volumes lowers costs, so they can undercut small producers.
  • Media and advertising: TV, social media, celebrity endorsement, sponsorship of sport and marketing aimed at children promote processed, high-sugar and high-fat foods.
  • Global brands and fast food spread Western diets, contributing to the nutrition transition.
  • Supply-chain power: supermarkets set prices and standards for farmers.
Success criterion 2

TNCs and food consumption: Food sovereignty and outcomes

Food sovereignty (promoted by La Via Campesina since 1996) is the right of people to define their own food and farming systems and produce healthy, culturally appropriate food. TNCs can threaten it by controlling seeds, land and markets.

Positive outcomesNegative outcomes
ConsumersCheap, varied, convenient and safe food all year roundProcessed diets linked to obesity and diabetes; less choice of local food
Local producersContracts, access to global markets, technology and trainingLow prices, dependence on one buyer, loss of land and traditional crops
Success criterion 3

Gender, food and health: Gender and food production

Women make up a large share of the agricultural workforce in low-income countries (around 40%, and over half in parts of Africa and Asia), yet they have less access to land, credit, seeds, fertiliser, training, technology and markets. The FAO estimated that if women farmers had the same access to resources as men, yields on their farms could rise by 20–30%, which could reduce the number of hungry people in the world by 12–17%.

Success criterion 3

Gender, food and health: Gender and health

  • When women control household income, more is spent on food, health and education for children, improving child nutrition.
  • In some LICs women face barriers to health care: distance, cost, needing permission from husbands, lack of female health workers and low literacy. This contributes to high maternal mortality.
  • Women and girls may eat last and least in times of food shortage, and are more likely to suffer anaemia.
  • Men’s health: social norms of masculinity can increase risk-taking, smoking, alcohol use and dangerous jobs, and make men less likely to seek medical help; men have lower life expectancy almost everywhere.
Success criterion 3

StrategyStrengthLimitation

Success criterion 4

Famine severity: Food insecurity and famine

Food insecurity is when people lack regular access to enough safe and nutritious food. Famine is the most extreme form: widespread lack of food leading to high levels of acute malnutrition and death. Under the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), Phase 5 (famine) means at least 20% of households face extreme food shortages, at least 30% of children are acutely malnourished, and at least 2 people per 10,000 die each day.

TypeMeaningExample
Food Availability Deficit (FAD)There is not enough food in an area, e.g. after drought, floods or pestsThe 1984 Ethiopian famine followed severe drought
Food Entitlement Deficit (FED)Food is available but people cannot get it because they lack money, land, work or rights (Amartya Sen’s idea)The 1943 Bengal famine, when food prices rose beyond the reach of the poor
Success criterion 4

Famine severity: Governance, media and aid

  • Governance: Amartya Sen argued that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press. Corrupt or authoritarian governments, conflict and poor policies (e.g. China’s Great Leap Forward, 1959–61) make famines worse.
  • Media: coverage can trigger aid, as BBC reports on Ethiopia in 1984 led to Band Aid and Live Aid; without media attention, crises can be ignored (“silent emergencies”).
  • Access to aid: blockades, insecurity and restrictions slow aid. In Somalia in 2011, an estimated 260,000 people died, partly because armed groups restricted humanitarian access.
Success criterion 4

Famine severity: The 2008 global food price spike

Between 2006 and 2008 world prices of rice, wheat and maize roughly doubled. Causes included high oil prices, demand for biofuels, droughts in major exporters such as Australia, rising demand for meat and feed, export bans by countries such as India and Vietnam, and speculation. Food riots broke out in over 30 countries. This was an example of a Food Entitlement Deficit: food existed, but the poorest, who spend most of their income on food, could not afford it.

Success criterion 5

Yemen: causes: Background to the conflict

Yemen, on the southern tip of the Arabian Peninsula, was already the poorest country in the Middle East. In 2014 the Houthi movement took control of the capital, Sana’a, and in March 2015 a Saudi-led coalition intervened to support the government. The war has killed tens of thousands directly and many more through hunger and disease, and over 4 million people have been displaced.

Success criterion 5

Success criterion 5

Yemen: causes: Scale and measurement

At the peak of the crisis, over 17 million people (more than half the population) were food insecure, and over 2 million children under five were acutely malnourished, along with over a million pregnant and breastfeeding women. The IPC scale classifies areas from Phase 1 (minimal) to Phase 5 (famine). The most at-risk governorates have included Hajjah, Hodeidah and Taiz, close to front lines, with high displacement, damaged infrastructure and blocked supply routes. Human factors, above all conflict, are far more important than physical factors in causing Yemen’s crisis.

Success criterion 6

Yemen: responses: The WFP’s emergency operation

Yemen has been one of the World Food Programme’s largest emergency operations, at its peak reaching around 13 million people a month. Assistance includes:

  • General food distributions: monthly rations of wheat flour, pulses, oil, sugar and salt.
  • Cash and vouchers: where markets work, families receive cash or commodity vouchers to buy food locally, supporting traders and giving choice.
  • Targeted nutrition programmes: specialised nutritious foods to prevent and treat acute malnutrition in children under five and pregnant and breastfeeding women.
  • School meals to support nutrition and attendance.
Success criterion 6

Yemen: responses: Deciding who gets help

The WFP uses targeting to prioritise the most vulnerable households, based on IPC phases, household surveys and registration (including biometric registration). Priority groups include displaced families, households headed by women, families with malnourished children, and areas in IPC Phase 4 or above.

Success criterion 6

Yemen: responses: Difficulties

TimescaleDifficulties
Shorter termFunding shortfalls forced the WFP to cut rations and reduce the number of people reached; aid diverted in some areas (the WFP partly suspended aid in 2019 over diversion); restricted access, checkpoints and insecurity; currency collapse reducing the value of cash
Longer termThe conflict continues; the economy, infrastructure and health system need rebuilding; water scarcity; dependence on imports and aid; aid treats symptoms, not causes

Key terms

WFP
The World Food Programme, the UN’s food-assistance agency.
FAO
The UN Food and Agriculture Organization.
WHO
The World Health Organization.
NGO
A non-governmental organisation, such as Oxfam.
Bilateral aid
Aid given directly from one government to another.
Agribusiness
Large-scale, commercial food production run as a business.
Vertical integration
Control of several stages of a supply chain by one company.
Horizontal integration
Merging with competitors at the same stage of production.
Food sovereignty
The right of people to control their own food systems.
Gender gap
Differences in opportunities and outcomes between men and women.
Microfinance
Small loans to people without access to banks.
Maternal health
The health of women during pregnancy, childbirth and after birth.
Food insecurity
Lack of regular access to enough safe, nutritious food.
Famine
Widespread lack of food causing malnutrition and death.
FAD
Food Availability Deficit: not enough food in an area.
FED
Food Entitlement Deficit: people cannot access the food that exists.
IPC
The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification.
Houthis
An armed movement that took control of Sana’a in 2014.
Blockade
Restricting the movement of goods into a country or port.
Acute malnutrition
Recent, severe undernutrition (wasting).
Governorate
An administrative region in Yemen.
Cash and voucher assistance
Giving people cash or vouchers to buy food locally.
Targeting
Prioritising aid for the most vulnerable people.
Aid diversion
When aid is taken by people it was not intended for.

4. Future health and food security and sustainability

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option F: Food and Health · Topics · Topic 4 of 4

Syllabus Statement

Solutions to food insecurity and food waste; the Green Revolution, regenerative agriculture, GMOs, vertical farming and in vitro meat; prevention and treatment (Majiang, China); Ebola in West Africa.

Success criterion 1

Solutions to food insecurity: Where food insecurity is greatest

Food insecurity is greatest in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and countries affected by conflict (Yemen, Sudan, South Sudan, Gaza, Haiti), climate extremes and economic shocks. The FAO’s four pillars of food security are availability, access, utilisation and stability.

Success criterion 1

Solutions to food insecurity: Reducing food waste

Roughly a third of all food produced is lost or wasted. Food loss happens mainly in low-income countries during harvest, storage and transport (pests, poor storage, lack of refrigeration). Food waste happens mainly in high-income countries in shops and homes (over-buying, “best before” dates, cosmetic standards). SDG target 12.3 aims to halve food waste by 2030.

  • Better storage, such as hermetic grain bags and solar-powered cold rooms.
  • Clearer date labelling and selling “wonky” produce.
  • Laws such as France’s 2016 ban on supermarkets throwing away edible food, and food banks and apps that redistribute surplus.

Success criterion 2

New approaches to food production: Case study: rice production

Rice feeds over half the world’s population, but flooded paddies use huge amounts of water and produce methane. Contemporary approaches include drought-tolerant and flood-tolerant varieties (such as “scuba rice” that survives two weeks under water), alternate wetting and drying (which can cut water use by about a third and methane emissions by up to half), the System of Rice Intensification, and satellite monitoring of crops. The most appropriate approach depends on context: high-tech systems suit wealthy cities, while low-cost techniques suit smallholders.

Prevention and treatment: Curative and primary health care

ApproachMeaningEvaluation
Curative health careTreating people once they are ill, usually in hospitals with doctors and specialist equipmentEssential for serious illness, but expensive, concentrated in cities and does not stop people getting ill
Primary (preventative) health carePreventing illness through vaccination, clean water, sanitation, nutrition, health education and local clinics, often run by community health workersCheap and reaches many people, but cannot treat complex illness and needs long-term commitment

The 1978 Alma-Ata Declaration made primary health care the key to “health for all”.

Success criterion 3

Prevention and treatment: Factors shaping disease management

  • Social marginalisation: poor, rural, ethnic minority, migrant and homeless people, and those with stigmatised diseases such as HIV, are less likely to access prevention and treatment.
  • Government priorities: spending on health versus defence or debt; urban hospitals versus rural clinics; free or insurance-based care.
  • Means of infection: water-borne diseases are best prevented by sanitation; vector-borne by vector control; non-communicable diseases by lifestyle change.
  • Scientific intervention: vaccines (smallpox eradicated in 1980; polio nearly eradicated), new drugs and diagnostics, but drug resistance is a growing threat.
Success criterion 3

Prevention and treatment: Case study: Majiang, China

Majiang County is a poor, mountainous, largely rural area in Guizhou Province in south-west China, with many ethnic minority communities and limited access to hospitals. Here the Amity Foundation, a Chinese NGO, has worked in partnership with local health services on preventative primary health care: training village doctors and health workers, improving water and sanitation, and health education. The case shows how low-cost prevention can reach marginalised rural people who are far from curative services.

Success criterion 4

Ebola in West Africa: Pandemics and Ebola

A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several countries or continents. New flu pandemics happen through antigenic shift, a sudden major change when different virus strains combine (e.g. H1N1 in 2009), whereas antigenic drift is gradual small mutation that causes seasonal flu.

Ebola is a viral disease spread through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of infected people (including during funerals) or animals. It causes fever, vomiting and bleeding, has a high fatality rate (around 40–50% in this outbreak) and an incubation period of up to 21 days.

Success criterion 4

Ebola in West Africa: The 2013–16 outbreak

The first case is thought to have been a young child in Meliandou, Guinea, in December 2013. It spread for about three months before being identified in March 2014, because Ebola had never been seen in West Africa, its early symptoms resemble malaria and cholera, and surveillance was weak. It spread to Sierra Leone and Liberia through porous borders and travel, reaching crowded capital cities. In total there were about 28,600 cases and 11,300 deaths, more than all previous outbreaks combined.

West Point, Monrovia was very vulnerable: a densely populated slum on a peninsula with very few toilets, poor water supply and high poverty. In August 2014 the government quarantined the area with troops, leading to riots and loss of trust.

Success criterion 4

Ebola in West Africa: Responses and lessons

LevelStrategiesEvaluation
LocalContact tracing, community health workers, safe and dignified burials, community education and trust-buildingCommunities changing burial practices was key to ending transmission
InternationalThe WHO declared a public health emergency of international concern in August 2014; Médecins Sans Frontières, the UK, US and China sent staff and built treatment centres; a vaccine (rVSV-ZEBOV) was trialled in Guinea in 2015The response was criticised as slow; the WHO was reformed afterwards
MediaRaised awareness and funding, but also spread fear, rumours and stigmaTravel bans and fear damaged economies

Lessons for the future: strong local health systems and surveillance, early action, community trust, rapid international funding, and vaccines and treatments ready in advance. These lessons were tested again during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Key terms

Food security
When all people at all times have access to enough safe, nutritious food.
Food loss
Food lost during production, storage and transport.
Food waste
Edible food thrown away by shops and consumers.
Resilience
The ability to cope with and recover from shocks.
Green Revolution
The spread of high-yielding seeds, fertiliser and irrigation from the 1960s.
GMO
A genetically modified organism.
Vertical farming
Growing crops in stacked layers indoors.
Regenerative agriculture
Farming that restores soil health and biodiversity.
Curative health care
Treating people once they are ill.
Primary health care
Preventative, community-based health care.
Social marginalisation
When groups are pushed to the edge of society and excluded from services.
Community health worker
A locally trained person who provides basic health care.
Pandemic
An epidemic that spreads across several countries or continents.
Antigenic shift
A sudden major change in a virus that can cause a pandemic.
Antigenic drift
Small gradual changes in a virus over time.
Contact tracing
Finding and monitoring people who have been in contact with a case.
Incubation period
The time between infection and symptoms.

1. Measuring food and health

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option F: Food and Health · Topics · Topic 1 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand how food and health are measured, the global patterns they show, and how diets and diseases change as countries develop.

Syllabus Statement

Food and nutrition indicators; the nutrition transition and Popkin’s patterns; health indicators including HALE, infant and maternal mortality, sanitation and doctors; the epidemiological transition and the burden of disease.

Success criterion 1

Food and nutrition indicators: What is health?

The World Health Organization defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity”. Good health depends on many factors: enough safe and nutritious food, clean water and sanitation, housing, education, income, access to health care, a healthy environment and lifestyle choices (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol).

Success criterion 1

Food and nutrition indicators: Measuring food and nutrition

IndicatorWhat it measuresStrengths and limitations
Global Food Security IndexA composite index of food affordability, availability, quality and safety, and sustainability and adaptation for over 100 countriesShows the underlying causes of food insecurity, not just the outcome; based partly on expert judgement
Global Hunger Index (GHI)Combines undernourishment, child stunting, child wasting and under-5 mortality into a score from 0 to 100 (low, moderate, serious, alarming, extremely alarming)Focuses on outcomes and children; uses data that can be several years old
Calories per person per dayAverage daily food energy available per person; around 2,000–2,500 kcal is recommended for an adultA national average hides inequality between regions, groups and households, and says nothing about diet quality
Indicators of malnutritionStunting (low height-for-age), wasting (low weight-for-height), underweight, micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight and obesityShow the effects on people directly; malnutrition includes over-nutrition as well as under-nutrition

Worldwide, around 700 million people are undernourished, about 150 million children under five are stunted and over 45 million are wasted, while over a billion people live with obesity. This is the double burden of malnutrition: under-nutrition and over-nutrition can exist in the same country, community or even household.

Success criterion 1

Food and nutrition indicators: Global patterns, trends and distributions

A spatial distribution describes how an indicator varies between places at one point in time (usually shown on a choropleth map). A trend describes how it changes over time.

  • Distribution: hunger and undernutrition are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, with very high GHI scores in countries affected by conflict (for example Yemen, Somalia, South Sudan). Calorie supply is highest in North America and Europe (often over 3,300 kcal per person).
  • Trend: hunger fell steadily from 2000 to the mid-2010s, then stalled and rose again because of conflict, climate extremes, economic shocks and the COVID-19 pandemic. Obesity has risen in almost every country.

Activity: trend or distribution?

Decide whether each statement describes a trend or a spatial distribution.

  • Undernourishment is highest in sub-Saharan Africa
  • Global hunger fell between 2000 and 2015
  • Calorie supply is highest in North America
  • Obesity rates have risen since 1980
  • Stunting is concentrated in South Asia and Africa
  • India’s GHI score has fallen since 2000

Success criterion 2

The nutrition transition: What is the nutrition transition?

The nutrition transition is the shift in diet and physical activity that happens as societies develop: from diets high in cereals, pulses and vegetables towards diets high in fats, sugar, salt, meat and processed foods, combined with less physical activity. Barry Popkin identified five broad patterns, which can overlap within one country.

Success criterion 2

The nutrition transition: Popkin’s five patterns

PatternFeatures
1. Collecting foodHunter-gatherer diets high in carbohydrates and fibre, low in fat; very active lifestyles; lean people
2. FamineSettled farming; less varied diets based on staple cereals; periodic famine and nutritional deficiencies
3. Receding famineRising incomes; more fruit, vegetables and animal protein; famine disappears; activity begins to fall
4. Nutrition-related non-communicable diseaseDiets high in fat, sugar and processed food; sedentary jobs and lifestyles; rising obesity, diabetes and heart disease
5. Behavioural changePeople and governments respond: healthier diets, more exercise, food labelling and sugar taxes; aims to delay disease and extend healthy life
Success criterion 2

The nutrition transition: Drivers and regional variations

  • Urbanisation: city dwellers buy more processed food, eat out more and do less manual work.
  • Economic growth: higher incomes increase demand for meat, dairy, oils and sugar.
  • Technological change: mechanisation reduces physical activity; supermarkets and cold chains make processed food cheap and available.
  • Mass media: advertising and global brands spread Western diets and fast food.

Most high-income countries are in patterns 4 and 5; many middle-income countries such as China, India, Brazil and Mexico are moving rapidly from pattern 3 to 4, often within a single generation, so they face both under-nutrition in rural areas and obesity in cities.

Success criterion 3

Health indicators: Defining the indicators

IndicatorDefinition
Health-adjusted life expectancy (HALE)The average number of years a person can expect to live in full health, taking into account years lived with illness or disability
Infant mortality rate (IMR)Deaths of babies under one year old per 1,000 live births per year
Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)Deaths of women from pregnancy or childbirth-related causes per 100,000 live births
Access to sanitationThe percentage of people using at least basic (or safely managed) toilets and waste disposal
Doctors per personThe number of physicians per 1,000 people (or people per doctor)
Success criterion 3

Health indicators: Global patterns

  • HALE is highest in Japan, Singapore, South Korea and much of Europe (over 70 years) and lowest in parts of sub-Saharan Africa (around 50 years). The global average is in the low 60s.
  • Infant and maternal mortality are highest in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia; about 70% of maternal deaths occur in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Sanitation: about 3.5 billion people lack safely managed sanitation and over 400 million still practise open defecation, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
  • Doctors: many European countries have over 3 doctors per 1,000 people; many sub-Saharan African countries have fewer than 0.2.
Success criterion 3

Health indicators: Evaluating the indicators

IndicatorStrengthLimitation
HALEIncludes quality as well as length of lifeHard to measure illness and disability consistently between countries
Infant mortalityVery sensitive to living conditions, water, nutrition and health care; widely collectedBirths and deaths may go unregistered in poorer or rural areas
Maternal mortalityShows quality of health care and the status of womenRelatively rare events, so data are uncertain and often modelled
SanitationLinked directly to disease preventionMeasures access, not whether facilities are used or maintained
Doctors per personEasy to compareIgnores nurses and community health workers, and doctors are often concentrated in cities

The epidemiological transition: Key terms

TermMeaning
Communicable (infectious) diseasePassed between people or animals, e.g. malaria, cholera, TB
Non-communicable (degenerative) diseaseNot infectious; often linked to age and lifestyle, e.g. cancer, heart disease, diabetes
Disease of povertyLinked to poor living conditions: malnutrition, unsafe water, poor sanitation (e.g. cholera, diarrhoea, TB)
Disease of affluenceLinked to wealthy lifestyles: rich diets, inactivity, smoking (e.g. obesity, type 2 diabetes)
IncidenceThe number of new cases in a period of time
PrevalenceThe total number of people with a disease at one time
EndemicAlways present in a population or region, e.g. malaria in Kenya
Epidemic / pandemicA sudden rise in cases in a region / an epidemic that spreads across several countries or continents
Success criterion 4

The epidemiological transition: The epidemiological transition model

Abdel Omran’s epidemiological transition model (1971) describes how the main causes of death change as countries develop, linked to the demographic transition model:

StageFeatures
1. Age of pestilence and famineHigh death rates from infectious disease, famine and poor sanitation; life expectancy 20–40 years
2. Age of receding pandemicsBetter food, water, sanitation and medicine; infectious disease declines; life expectancy rises to 50+
3. Age of degenerative and human-made diseasesHeart disease, cancer and strokes become the main causes of death; life expectancy over 70
4. Age of delayed degenerative diseases (added later)Medical advances delay death from degenerative disease; life expectancy over 80; rising dementia
Success criterion 4

The epidemiological transition: Ageing and the burden of disease

The burden of disease measures the impact of ill health, often in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs): years lost to early death plus years lived with disability. As the global population ages, non-communicable diseases now cause about three-quarters of deaths worldwide. Many low- and middle-income countries face a double burden of disease: infectious diseases have not been eliminated, while heart disease, diabetes and cancer rise quickly, putting huge pressure on health systems.

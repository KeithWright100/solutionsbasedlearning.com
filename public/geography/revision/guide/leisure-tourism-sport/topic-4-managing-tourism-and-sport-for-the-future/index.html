4. Managing tourism and sport for the future

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option E: Leisure, Tourism and Sport · Topics · Topic 4 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand the consequences of unsustainable tourism, ecotourism, future trends and influences on sport participation.

Syllabus Statement

Carrying capacity and unsustainable tourism in Iceland; ecotourism in Rwanda; social media, security and diaspora; sports diplomacy, the Paralympics, gender and the DSD regulations.

Success criterion 1

Unsustainable tourism: Carrying capacity

TypeMeaning
Physical carrying capacityThe maximum number of people a site can physically hold (e.g. seats, beds, space on a path)
Ecological carrying capacityThe level of use beyond which the environment is damaged (e.g. erosion, wildlife disturbance)
Perceptual carrying capacityThe level of crowding beyond which visitors or locals feel the experience is spoiled; it varies between people

Tourism is often unsustainable because it involves long-distance travel (high emissions), concentrates huge numbers of people in small, often fragile places, and uses large amounts of water, energy and land.

ImpactExamples

Success criterion 1

Unsustainable tourism: Iceland and managing hotspots

In Iceland, rapid growth caused footpath erosion and damage to moss at sites such as Þingvellir and the waterfalls of the south coast, traffic accidents on narrow roads, toilet shortages, and dangerous behaviour at Reynisfjara beach. Possible management options to increase resilience include: entry fees and permits, timed tickets and visitor caps, boardwalks and hardened paths, spreading visitors to other regions and seasons, better infrastructure and education, and taxes on accommodation to fund conservation.

Success criterion 2

Ecotourism: Rwanda: Sustainable tourism and ecotourism

Sustainable tourism meets the needs of tourists and host communities today while protecting the environment and culture for the future. Ecotourism is responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of local people and involves education.

PrincipleWhat it means
EnvironmentalMinimise impact; fund and support conservation
EconomicProvide income and jobs for local people; keep money in the community
Social and culturalRespect local culture; involve communities in decisions; educate visitors
Success criterion 2

Ecotourism: Rwanda: Mountain gorilla tourism

In Volcanoes National Park, small groups of tourists trek to see habituated mountain gorillas for one hour. Permits cost US$1,500 per person, so a small number of high-paying visitors generates large revenues with limited impact. Through a revenue-sharing scheme, 10% of park revenues go to local communities for schools, health centres, water supplies and other projects, giving people a strong reason to protect the gorillas rather than poach. The wider Virunga mountain gorilla population has increased, and the species was moved from “critically endangered” to “endangered” in 2018.

Success criterion 2

Ecotourism: Rwanda: Akagera and evaluation

Akagera National Park has been managed since 2010 by the government with the conservation organisation African Parks. Lions were reintroduced in 2015 and black rhinos in 2017, so the park again has the “Big Five”. Poaching has fallen sharply and tourism revenue now covers most of the park’s running costs.

Evaluation: locally, Rwanda’s model funds conservation and community development; globally, high prices limit numbers but most visitors still fly long distances, and benefits may not reach the poorest people. Tourism is also vulnerable to regional instability and shocks such as COVID-19.

Success criterion 3

Tourism in the future: Long-term growth

International tourist arrivals grew from about 25 million in 1950 to about 1.5 billion in 2019. Growth paused after shocks such as the 2001 terrorist attacks, the 2003 SARS outbreak and the 2008–09 financial crisis, and collapsed by about 70% in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, before recovering close to 2019 levels by 2024.

Success criterion 3

Tourism in the future: Factors shaping the future

FactorLikely influence
Social media and the internetOnline booking, reviews and platforms like Airbnb make independent travel easy; Instagram and TikTok create sudden new hotspots and can fuel overtourism; virtual tours may replace some trips
International securityTerrorism, war, political instability and disease outbreaks deter visitors and change routes; travel advice and insurance affect choices
Diaspora growthMigrants and their families travel to visit friends and relatives (VFR tourism) and to explore their heritage, e.g. the Indian and Chinese diasporas
Other factorsRising incomes in Asia, ageing populations, climate change and carbon costs, new technology and space tourism
Success criterion 3

Tourism in the future: Evaluating the factors

Most forecasts expect tourism to keep growing, led by Asia’s growing middle class. Social media is likely to have the widest everyday influence on where people go, while security shocks have the most sudden and dramatic effects, though recovery is usually quick. Climate change may become the biggest long-term influence, through higher costs of flying and changes to destinations such as ski resorts and coral reefs.

Success criterion 4

Politics, culture and sport: Sport and politics

Sports diplomacy is the use of sport to build relations between countries. Examples include “ping-pong diplomacy” between the USA and China in 1971, North and South Korea marching together and fielding a joint women’s ice hockey team at the 2018 Winter Olympics, and boycotts such as the exclusion of apartheid South Africa and the US-led boycott of Moscow 1980. International agreements and organisations (the IOC, FIFA, anti-doping rules) also shape who can take part.

Success criterion 4

Politics, culture and sport: The Paralympics and inclusion

The Paralympic movement began with the 1948 Stoke Mandeville Games for injured servicemen, organised by Dr Ludwig Guttmann. The first Paralympic Games were held in Rome in 1960 with about 400 athletes; recent Games have had over 4,000 athletes from more than 160 countries, with huge TV audiences. The Paralympics have raised the profile of disabled athletes and helped change attitudes towards disability.

Gender: women first competed in the Olympics in 1900. London 2012 was the first Games where every competing country sent female athletes, and Paris 2024 was the first with equal numbers of places for men and women. Barriers remain: pay gaps, less media coverage, cultural restrictions and fewer opportunities in some countries.

Success criterion 4

Politics, culture and sport: Caster Semenya and gender regulations

South African runner Caster Semenya won Olympic 800 m gold in 2012 and 2016. She has a difference of sex development (DSD) that gives naturally high testosterone. In 2018 World Athletics introduced DSD regulations requiring athletes like her to lower their testosterone with medication to compete in some women’s events, and in 2023 these rules were extended to all female events. Semenya refused and has been unable to defend her title. In 2023 the European Court of Human Rights ruled that she had been discriminated against, but the regulations remain in place.

Arguments for the regulationsArguments against
Fairness for female athletes; a level playing fieldDiscriminates against women with natural biological differences; forces medical treatment; affects mainly women from the Global South

Key terms

Carrying capacity
The maximum use a place can sustain without unacceptable damage.
Overtourism
When visitor numbers are so high that they damage a place and the experience.
Resilience
The ability of a place to cope with pressure and recover.
Economic leakage
Tourism income that leaves the destination.
Ecotourism
Responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and benefits local people.
Sustainable tourism
Tourism that meets present needs without harming the future.
Revenue sharing
Giving part of tourism income to local communities.
Habituated
Wild animals that have become used to the presence of people.
Diaspora
People living outside their country of origin and their descendants.
VFR tourism
Travel to visit friends and relatives.
Sports diplomacy
Using sport to build relations between countries.
Paralympics
The international Games for athletes with disabilities.
Boycott
Refusing to take part as a form of protest.
DSD
Differences of sex development.

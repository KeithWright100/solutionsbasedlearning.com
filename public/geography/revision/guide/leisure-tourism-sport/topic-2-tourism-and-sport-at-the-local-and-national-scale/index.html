2. Tourism and sport at the local and national scale

Success criterion 1

Tourism hotspots: Hotspots and touristic resources

A tourism hotspot (or honeypot) is a place that attracts very large numbers of visitors. It may be seasonal (busy at certain times of year, like a ski resort in winter or a beach in summer) or diurnal (busy at certain times of day, like a city attraction or a viewpoint at sunset).

TypeMeaningExamples
Primary touristic resourcesFeatures that were not created for tourism: climate, beaches, mountains, wildlife, historic buildings, cultureThe Swiss Alps, the Great Barrier Reef, the Old City of Jerusalem
Secondary touristic resourcesFacilities built for tourists: hotels, restaurants, theme parks, airports, ski liftsDisneyland Paris, Dubai’s hotels and malls
Success criterion 1

Tourism hotspots: Why hotspots grow

  • Physical factors: attractive climate, scenery, coasts and mountains.
  • Human factors: heritage, culture, festivals, shopping, food, accessibility (airports, roads, rail), safety and political stability.
  • Marketing and branding: campaigns, celebrity and film exposure, social media and UNESCO World Heritage status raise a place’s profile.
  • Rural hotspots often depend on primary resources (national parks, Lake District); urban hotspots rely more on secondary resources and culture (Paris, Bangkok).
Success criterion 1

Tourism hotspots: Butler’s Tourism Area Life Cycle

StageFeatures
ExplorationSmall numbers of adventurous visitors; few facilities; locals in control
InvolvementLocals provide services; a tourist season begins
DevelopmentOutside investment, large hotels and marketing; rapid growth; locals lose control
ConsolidationGrowth slows; tourism dominates the economy
StagnationCarrying capacity reached; environmental and social problems; image declines
Decline or rejuvenationVisitors fall unless the resort reinvents itself (new attractions, new markets)
Success criterion 2

Spheres of influence: Threshold, range and sphere of influence

TermMeaning
Sphere of influenceThe area from which a facility draws its users
Population thresholdThe minimum number of people needed to support a facility
RangeThe maximum distance people are willing to travel to use a facility

Facilities with a high threshold (a national stadium) need a large population and so have a large range and sphere of influence. Facilities with a low threshold (a local park or gym) serve only the surrounding neighbourhood.

Success criterion 2

Spheres of influence: The leisure hierarchy

A leisure hierarchy ranks facilities by threshold and sphere of influence, from many small, local facilities at the bottom to a few large, national ones at the top.

LevelExampleSphere of influence
National / internationalNational parks, national stadiums (Wembley), major theme parksWhole country or world
Regional / cityCity stadiums, arenas, large leisure centresCity and surrounding region
Local / neighbourhoodParks, playgrounds, gyms, sports pitchesA few streets or a suburb
Success criterion 2

Spheres of influence: Leisure facilities within a town

  • Central area (CBD): theatres, cinemas, museums, bars and restaurants serving the whole town; little space for large facilities.
  • Inner city: older parks, swimming baths and community facilities; some redeveloped as new stadiums.
  • Suburbs: local parks, playing fields, gyms and golf clubs.
  • Urban–rural fringe: land-hungry facilities such as out-of-town stadiums, golf courses, retail-leisure parks and garden centres, where land is cheaper and access by car is easier.

The number and type of facilities depend on population size and wealth, land values, planning policies and transport.

Success criterion 3

The Premier League: The geography of the Premier League

The English Premier League has 20 clubs, with three relegated to the Championship and three promoted each season. Clubs are concentrated in large urban areas, especially London (often six or more clubs) and the North West (Manchester and Liverpool), with others in the Midlands and North East. Large parts of the South West, East Anglia and rural areas have few or no Premier League clubs.

Success criterion 3

The Premier League: Why stadiums are where they are

  • Population: large cities provide the threshold of supporters, sponsors and media coverage.
  • History: most clubs were founded in the late 1800s in industrial towns, often by factory workers or churches.
  • Accessibility: near railway stations, main roads and motorways.
  • Relief and land: large, flat sites; newer stadiums are often built on cheaper land at the edge of cities (for example Tottenham, Brentford and Everton’s new ground).
  • Isolated regions with small populations cannot support a top-flight club.
Success criterion 3

The Premier League: Nottingham Forest and the supporter base

Nottingham Forest’s City Ground stands on the south bank of the River Trent in West Bridgford, close to Trent Bridge cricket ground and only a few hundred metres from Notts County’s stadium across the river, one of the closest pairs of league grounds in England. The site is close to the city centre, rail station and main roads. Forest were European Cup winners in 1979 and 1980 and returned to the Premier League in 2022.

A club’s supporter base extends far beyond its city: through television, social media and global marketing, the biggest clubs (Manchester United, Liverpool) have hundreds of millions of fans worldwide, especially in Asia.

Success criterion 4

Large-scale events: Events as temporary sites of leisure

Large-scale sporting, musical, cultural and religious events, such as the Olympic Games, the football World Cup, music festivals, carnivals and the Hajj, temporarily turn a place into a major site of leisure. They attract huge numbers of visitors in a short time, and their costs and benefits can be social, economic or political.

Success criterion 4

Large-scale events: The Full Moon Party, Koh Phangan, Thailand

Every month, on or near the full moon, a beach party at Haad Rin on the island of Koh Phangan attracts anything from about 10,000 to 30,000 young tourists.

Full Moon Party
BenefitsA major source of income for bars, guesthouses, ferries and shops; jobs for local people; brings tourists who also visit other parts of Thailand
CostsLitter and plastic on the beach and in the sea; noise; drug and alcohol problems, injuries and occasional deaths; pressure on water and waste services; a party image that some locals dislike
Success criterion 5

Glastonbury Festival: Site and situation

Glastonbury Festival is held at Worthy Farm near the village of Pilton, Somerset, in south-west England. It was started by farmer Michael Eavis in 1970 and is now one of the world’s largest greenfield festivals, with around 200,000 people on site. Pilton itself has only about 1,000 residents.

  • A large area of open farmland in a shallow valley, with gentle slopes that form natural amphitheatres around the stages.
  • Good regional access by the M5 and A-roads, and rail links to Castle Cary station with shuttle buses.
  • Within reach of large populations in Bristol, London and the Midlands.
  • The festival usually takes a “fallow year” every five or six years so the land can recover.

Glastonbury Festival: Advantages and disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
EconomicTens of millions of pounds for the local and regional economy; jobs for local people; local businesses and farmers benefit; millions donated to charities such as Oxfam, Greenpeace and WaterAid, and to local projectsPrices rise; some businesses lose normal customers
SocialInternational fame for the area; community projects funded by the festivalTraffic congestion and road closures; noise; crime; pressure on local services
EnvironmentalStrong sustainability policiesLitter, tents left behind, damage to farmland, pollution risk to local streams, transport emissions
Success criterion 5

Glastonbury Festival: Sustainability and the SDGs

Glastonbury links to SDG 11 (sustainable cities and communities) and SDG 12 (responsible consumption and production). It banned the sale of single-use plastic bottles in 2019, encourages people to take their tents home, uses compost toilets and recycling crews, and promotes travel by coach and train.

Key terms

Tourism hotspot
A place that attracts very large numbers of visitors.
Primary touristic resource
A natural or cultural feature not created for tourism.
Secondary touristic resource
A facility built for tourists.
Butler’s model
The Tourism Area Life Cycle showing how a resort grows and may decline.
Diurnal
Varying through the day.
Sphere of influence
The area from which a facility draws its users.
Threshold
The minimum population needed to support a facility.
Range
The maximum distance people will travel to a facility.
Leisure hierarchy
A ranking of leisure facilities by size and sphere of influence.
Urban–rural fringe
The zone where a town meets the countryside.
Relegation
Being moved down to a lower league.
Supporter base
The people who follow a club, locally and globally.
Threshold population
The minimum number of people needed to support a facility or club.
Temporary site of leisure
A place that becomes a leisure destination only for an event.
White elephant
A costly venue that is little used after an event.
Soft power
Influence gained through culture and image rather than force.
Greenfield festival
A festival held on undeveloped farmland.
Fallow year
A year off to allow the land to recover.
Stakeholder
A person or group affected by or interested in a decision.

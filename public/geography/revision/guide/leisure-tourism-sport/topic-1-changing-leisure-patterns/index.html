1. Changing leisure patterns

IB Geography Revision Guide · Option E: Leisure, Tourism and Sport · Topics · Topic 1 of 4

Learning Intention

To understand how leisure has changed, how it links to economic development and what affects who takes part.

Syllabus Statement

Leisure, recreation, sport and tourism; the growth of leisure time; development and leisure in China and the USA; factors affecting participation, including Plog’s model.

Success criterion 1

Changing leisure patterns: Key terms

TermMeaning
LeisureFree time when people are not working or doing essential tasks, used for activities they choose
RecreationAny leisure activity done for enjoyment, from gardening to watching TV
SportA physical activity with rules, often competitive, done for enjoyment or fitness
TourismTravelling to and staying in places outside your usual environment for less than a year, for leisure, business or other purposes

Tourism includes day trips (excursionists) and overnight stays (tourists), and can be domestic (within your own country) or international.

Success criterion 1

Changing leisure patterns: The growth of leisure time

Over the last century leisure time has grown in most societies, especially in high-income countries:

  • Technology: machines in factories and homes (washing machines, dishwashers) reduced working hours and household chores.
  • Affluence: higher incomes mean people can afford holidays, equipment and memberships.
  • Accessibility: cars, cheap flights, paid holidays and the internet make leisure easier to reach and book.
  • Legislation: laws on working hours and paid leave (for example the European Union’s minimum of four weeks’ paid holiday).

Leisure time still varies: people in many European countries have more than in the USA, Japan or South Korea, and in almost every country women have less leisure time than men, because they do more unpaid care and housework.

Success criterion 2

Development and leisure: Income and leisure time

As countries develop, incomes rise and working hours usually fall, so people have both the time and the money for leisure. But there is a trade-off: every extra hour of leisure means an hour less of paid work. An indifference curve shows the combinations of income and leisure time a person would be equally happy with; people choose the point where they get the best balance they can afford.

Success criterion 2

Development and leisure: Barriers to participation

GroupBarriers
Low-income countriesLong working hours, often in farming or informal jobs; little or no paid holiday; low disposable income; poor transport; few facilities; visa restrictions on travel abroad
Low-income groups in richer countriesCost of travel, equipment, club fees and holidays; jobs with irregular hours or no paid leave; caring responsibilities; living far from facilities; poor public transport
Success criterion 2

Development and leisure: Contrasting societies: China and the USA

China: rapid economic growth created a new middle class with money for travel. Domestic tourism exploded, especially during the Golden Week holidays, and before the COVID-19 pandemic about 150 million Chinese trips abroad were made each year, making China the world’s largest source of international tourists and tourist spending.

USA: one of the richest countries, yet the only advanced economy with no legal right to paid holiday. Many workers get around 10–15 days and do not use them all, a pattern called “vacation deprivation”. High income does not automatically mean more leisure time.

Success criterion 3

Who takes part?: Affluence, gender and stage in life

FactorEffect on participation
AffluenceWealthier people take more holidays, travel further and play more expensive sports (golf, skiing, sailing); poorer people choose cheaper sports such as football or running
GenderWomen often have less free time and face cultural barriers, safety concerns and fewer opportunities in some sports; women’s participation and professional sport are growing
Stage in lifeYoung adults travel independently and play team sports; families choose child-friendly holidays; retired people have time and often money for cruises and long holidays
Success criterion 3

Who takes part?: Personality: Plog’s model

Plog’s model classifies tourists by personality:

  • Allocentrics (venturers): adventurous, seek new, unusual, remote destinations and avoid crowds.
  • Mid-centrics: the majority; prefer places that are popular but still a little different.
  • Psychocentrics (dependables): cautious, prefer familiar, safe, well-developed resorts and package holidays.

As a destination becomes popular, allocentrics move on and psychocentrics arrive, linking Plog to Butler’s model.

Success criterion 3

Who takes part?: Place of residence and passport power

People in cities have more sports facilities and airports nearby; rural residents may have more outdoor space but fewer clubs and longer journeys. Climate matters too: skiing in mountain regions, surfing on coasts.

The “power” of a passport (how many countries it allows visa-free entry to) strongly affects international tourism. Holders of Japanese, Singaporean or many European passports can enter around 190 countries visa-free; holders of Afghan or Syrian passports can enter fewer than 30.

Key terms

Leisure
Free time used for activities people choose.
Recreation
Any leisure activity done for enjoyment.
Tourism
Travel to and stay in places outside your usual environment for less than a year.
Domestic tourism
Tourism within your own country.
Excursionist
A day visitor who does not stay overnight.
Disposable income
Money left after taxes and essential spending.
Indifference curve
A graph showing combinations of income and leisure a person values equally.
Vacation deprivation
Workers not having or not using enough paid holiday.
Golden Week
A national holiday week in China when domestic travel peaks.
Allocentric
An adventurous tourist who seeks new, unusual destinations.
Psychocentric
A cautious tourist who prefers familiar, safe destinations.
Plog’s model
A classification of tourists by personality type.
Passport power
The number of countries a passport allows visa-free entry to.

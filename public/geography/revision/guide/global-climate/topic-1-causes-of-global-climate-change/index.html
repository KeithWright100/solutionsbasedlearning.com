1. Causes of global climate change

Success criterion 1

The atmospheric system: Weather, climate and the atmosphere

Weather is the day-to-day state of the atmosphere (temperature, rainfall, wind, cloud). Climate is the average weather of a place over a long period, usually 30 years or more. One cold winter is weather; a 30-year warming trend is climate.

The atmosphere is a thin layer of gases held by gravity: mainly nitrogen (about 78%) and oxygen (about 21%), with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour. It has four main layers:

LayerHeightKey features
Troposphere0 to about 12 kmTemperature falls with height; contains almost all water vapour, cloud and weather
StratosphereAbout 12 to 50 kmTemperature rises with height; contains the ozone layer, which absorbs ultraviolet radiation
MesosphereAbout 50 to 80 kmTemperature falls again; the coldest layer
ThermosphereAbove about 80 kmVery thin air; temperature rises with height

The troposphere matters most for climate because it holds most of the atmosphere’s mass, nearly all its water vapour and the greenhouse gases that trap heat near the surface.

Success criterion 1

The atmospheric system: Orbital cycles and the Sun

Milankovitch cycles are long-term changes in Earth’s orbit and tilt that alter how much solar energy reaches different latitudes and seasons. They help explain the timing of glacial and interglacial periods.

CycleWhat changesApproximate period
EccentricityThe shape of Earth’s orbit changes from nearly circular to more ellipticalAbout 100,000 years
Axial tilt (obliquity)The tilt of Earth’s axis varies between about 22.1° and 24.5°; more tilt means stronger seasonsAbout 41,000 years
PrecessionEarth’s axis wobbles like a spinning top, changing which hemisphere faces the Sun at its closest pointAbout 26,000 years

Solar variability is change in the Sun’s energy output, for example the 11-year sunspot cycle. More sunspots mean slightly more energy. The changes are small and cannot explain the warming since the mid-20th century, when solar output has been flat or slightly falling.

Success criterion 1

The atmospheric system: Volcanoes, and natural versus human causes

Large explosive eruptions inject sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it forms sulphate aerosols that reflect sunlight. This causes short-term global cooling for one to three years. After Mount Pinatubo (Philippines, 1991), global average temperature fell by about 0.5°C for around two years. Volcanoes also release CO₂, but far less than human activity.

Natural causesHuman (anthropogenic) causes
Milankovitch cycles, solar variability, volcanic eruptionsBurning fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture, industry
Act over years (volcanoes) to tens of thousands of years (orbits)Rapid change over about 200 years, accelerating since 1950
Cannot explain recent warmingThe dominant cause of warming since the mid-20th century

Success criterion 2

Energy balance and feedbacks: Insolation, albedo and the energy budget

Insolation (incoming solar radiation) arrives as short-wave radiation. Albedo is the proportion of that radiation a surface reflects: fresh snow and ice reflect up to about 80–90%, oceans and forests only around 5–15%. Earth’s average albedo is about 30%.

The global energy budget balances incoming and outgoing energy. Of every 100 units of insolation, about 30 are reflected straight back to space, about 20 are absorbed by the atmosphere and about 50 by the surface. The warmed surface emits long-wave (infrared) radiation; greenhouse gases absorb much of this and re-radiate it, some back towards the surface. When incoming and outgoing energy are equal, the global temperature is stable. Extra greenhouse gases reduce outgoing radiation, so the system gains energy and warms until a new balance is reached.

Success criterion 2

Energy balance and feedbacks: Positive and negative feedback

A feedback loop is when a change triggers effects that alter the original change.

  • Positive feedback amplifies the change, pushing the system further from its starting state.
  • Negative feedback dampens the change, helping return the system towards balance.
FeedbackHow it worksType
Ice-albedoWarming melts snow and ice, exposing darker land and ocean that absorb more insolation, causing more warmingPositive
Water vapourWarmer air holds more water vapour (about 7% more per °C), and water vapour is a greenhouse gas, causing more warmingPositive
Permafrost thawThawing permafrost releases methane and CO₂, causing more warmingPositive
Plant growth (CO₂ fertilisation)More CO₂ can increase plant growth, which absorbs more CO₂Negative
Low cloudMore evaporation can form more low cloud, which reflects insolationNegative (uncertain)
Success criterion 2

Energy balance and feedbacks: Why feedbacks matter

Feedbacks make climate prediction difficult because they can multiply the warming caused by greenhouse gases alone. Scientists worry about tipping points: thresholds, such as the loss of Arctic summer sea ice or large-scale permafrost thaw, beyond which positive feedbacks become self-sustaining and changes are hard to reverse.

Success criterion 3

Enhanced greenhouse effect: Natural and enhanced greenhouse effect

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth about 33°C warmer than it would otherwise be (an average of about 15°C instead of about −18°C). Greenhouse gases absorb outgoing long-wave radiation and re-radiate it. The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by human emissions raising greenhouse gas concentrations.

GasMain sourcesNote
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cementAbout 280 ppm before 1750; over 420 ppm today
Methane (CH₄)Livestock, rice paddies, landfill, gas leaksMuch stronger per molecule than CO₂ over 20 years
Nitrous oxide (N₂O)Nitrogen fertilisers, industryLong-lived and powerful
Halocarbons (CFCs, HFCs)Refrigeration, aerosols, industryVery powerful; some also damage ozone
Water vapourEvaporation (amplifies warming as a feedback)Not directly controlled by emissions
Success criterion 3

Enhanced greenhouse effect: Fossil fuels and deforestation

  • Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) release carbon stored for millions of years. Energy, industry and transport produce roughly three-quarters of global greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Deforestation releases carbon stored in trees and soils, and removes a carbon sink that would otherwise absorb CO₂. Land-use change, especially in the tropics, is a major source.
  • The Keeling Curve, measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii since 1958, shows CO₂ rising every year with a seasonal cycle.
Success criterion 3

Enhanced greenhouse effect: Development, globalization and trade

Emissions rise with economic development as industry, energy use, car ownership and meat consumption grow. High-income countries have the largest cumulative (historical) emissions and very high emissions per person. Today, large emerging economies, especially China and India, are among the biggest annual emitters.

Globalization and trade move emissions around the world: goods consumed in HICs are often made in emerging economies, so production-based figures can hide the consumption-based emissions of rich consumers (“carbon leakage”). Shipping and aviation emissions also grow with trade.

Key terms

Weather
The day-to-day state of the atmosphere.
Climate
The average weather of a place over 30 years or more.
Troposphere
The lowest layer of the atmosphere, where weather happens.
Milankovitch cycles
Long-term changes in Earth’s orbit and tilt that affect climate.
Solar variability
Changes in the Sun’s energy output, such as the sunspot cycle.
Sulphate aerosols
Tiny particles from eruptions that reflect sunlight and cool the planet.
Insolation
Incoming solar radiation.
Albedo
The proportion of incoming radiation reflected by a surface.
Global energy budget
The balance between incoming and outgoing energy in the Earth system.
Positive feedback
A process that amplifies an initial change.
Negative feedback
A process that dampens an initial change.
Tipping point
A threshold beyond which change becomes self-sustaining.
Greenhouse effect
The trapping of outgoing long-wave radiation by gases in the atmosphere.
Enhanced greenhouse effect
Extra warming caused by human greenhouse gas emissions.
ppm
Parts per million, a measure of gas concentration.
Carbon sink
A store that absorbs more carbon than it releases, such as a forest.
Cumulative emissions
Total emissions over time, not just today.
Carbon leakage
Emissions moving to other countries through trade or relocation of industry.

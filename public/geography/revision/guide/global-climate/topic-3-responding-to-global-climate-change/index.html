3. Responding to global climate change

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 2: Global Climate · Topics · Topic 3 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand how risk and vulnerability vary, and how governments, civil society and businesses respond to climate change.

Syllabus Statement

Risk, vulnerability and resilience; government-led adaptation and mitigation, including the Paris Agreement; civil society and corporate strategy.

Success criterion 1

Risk and vulnerability: The risk equation

Risk is the likelihood of harm from a hazard. It depends on three things:

ComponentMeaning
HazardA potentially damaging physical event, such as a flood, drought or heatwave
ExposureThe people, buildings and assets located where the hazard can strike
VulnerabilityHow likely those people and assets are to be harmed, shaped by poverty, health, housing and governance

Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability, reduced by capacity to cope. If any part is zero, there is no risk: a flood in an empty area harms no one.

Success criterion 1

Risk and vulnerability: Resilience and adaptive capacity

Resilience is the ability to absorb a shock, adapt and recover. Adaptive capacity is the ability to adjust to changing conditions, shaped by wealth, technology, education, infrastructure, institutions and social networks. Adaptive capacity is not fixed: it can be built up through investment and planning.

Success criterion 1

Risk and vulnerability: Why vulnerability varies

  • Between countries: wealth, quality of buildings, warning systems and health care.
  • Within countries: the poor, the elderly, children, women, people with disabilities, indigenous groups and outdoor workers are often more vulnerable.
  • Example: a cyclone of similar strength may kill few people in a wealthy country with warnings and shelters but many in a poor, densely populated delta. Bangladesh has cut cyclone deaths dramatically since the 1970s by building thousands of shelters and improving early warnings, showing vulnerability can be reduced.

Activity: which component?

Match each example to the right part of the risk equation.

  • A category 4 cyclone
  • Ten million people living on a delta
  • Houses made of mud and bamboo
  • A month-long heatwave
  • A city built on a floodplain
  • No early-warning system

Success criterion 2

Adaptation and mitigation: Adaptation and mitigation

StrategyAimExamples
MitigationReducing the causes of climate change: cutting emissions or increasing carbon sinksRenewable energy, carbon taxes, energy efficiency, electric transport, reforestation, carbon capture and storage
AdaptationReducing the harm from climate change that is already happening or unavoidableSea walls and flood barriers, drought-resistant crops, early-warning systems, heat action plans, managed retreat
Success criterion 2

Adaptation and mitigation: International agreements

  • UNFCCC (1992) set up annual “COP” negotiations.
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997) set binding emission cuts for developed countries only; the USA never ratified it.
  • Paris Agreement (2015): almost every country agreed to keep warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C. Each submits its own targets (Nationally Determined Contributions), reviewed every five years. Targets are not legally binding, and current pledges are not yet enough to meet the goal.

Challenges: free-riding, disagreement over who should pay, the needs of developing countries to grow, short political cycles, and fossil-fuel interests.

Success criterion 2

Adaptation and mitigation: Scales and effectiveness

ScaleExamples
LocalFlood-resistant building codes, urban tree planting, local flood defences
NationalRenewable energy targets, carbon pricing, banning new petrol cars, national adaptation plans
InternationalThe Paris Agreement, climate finance for developing countries, loss and damage funding

To evaluate a strategy, consider its cost, who benefits, how much it reduces emissions or harm, its speed, side effects, and whether it is fair to the poor.

Activity: adaptation or mitigation?

Sort each government strategy.

  • A carbon tax on fossil fuels
  • Building a sea wall
  • Subsidies for solar panels
  • Drought-resistant seed for farmers
  • Planting new forests
  • A heatwave early-warning system

Success criterion 3

Civil society and business: Civil society

Civil society is collective action outside government and business: NGOs, community groups, campaigners and individuals.

  • NGOs (for example Greenpeace, WWF) carry out research, advocacy, legal challenges and awareness campaigns, and sometimes run projects such as reforestation or disaster relief.
  • Grassroots movements start from ordinary citizens and communities. The school strikes started by Greta Thunberg in 2018 grew into the global Fridays for Future movement, raising public pressure on governments.
Success criterion 3

Civil society and business: Corporate strategy

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is when businesses voluntarily act to reduce their environmental and social impact, for example setting net-zero targets, using renewable energy, cutting emissions in supply chains or reporting their carbon footprint.

Greenwashing is when a company exaggerates or misleads people about how environmentally friendly it is, for example advertising a small green project while expanding fossil-fuel production. It is a concern because it misleads consumers and investors and delays real change.

Success criterion 3

Civil society and business: Individuals and the multi-actor response

Individual actions (eating less meat, flying less, saving energy, voting and campaigning) can reduce emissions and signal demand for change, but on their own they are small compared with the systemic changes needed in energy, transport and industry.

ActorMain tools
GovernmentsLaws, taxes, regulation, international agreements
BusinessesInvestment, technology, supply chains, CSR
Civil societyAdvocacy, pressure, awareness, community projects
IndividualsConsumer choices, voting, activism

Effective responses need all actors working together at different scales.

Activity: CSR or greenwashing?

Sort each company action.

  • Publishing independently checked emissions data
  • A “green” advert while expanding oil drilling
  • Switching factories to renewable electricity
  • Labelling a product “eco” with no evidence
  • Cutting emissions across its supply chain
  • Offsetting instead of reducing any emissions

Key terms

Risk
The likelihood of harm from a hazard.
Hazard
A potentially damaging physical event.
Exposure
People and assets located where a hazard can strike.
Vulnerability
How likely people and assets are to be harmed.
Resilience
The ability to absorb, adapt to and recover from a shock.
Adaptive capacity
The ability to adjust to changing conditions.
Mitigation
Action to reduce the causes of climate change.
Adaptation
Action to reduce the harm caused by climate change.
Paris Agreement
The 2015 agreement to limit warming to well below 2°C.
NDC
A country’s Nationally Determined Contribution to cutting emissions.
Carbon tax
A charge on fossil fuels based on their carbon content.
Climate finance
Money to help developing countries mitigate and adapt.
Civil society
Collective action outside government and business.
NGO
A non-governmental organisation.
Grassroots movement
Action that starts from ordinary citizens and communities.
CSR
Corporate social responsibility: voluntary action by businesses to reduce harm.
Greenwashing
Misleading claims about how environmentally friendly something is.
Net zero
Balancing greenhouse gas emissions with removals.

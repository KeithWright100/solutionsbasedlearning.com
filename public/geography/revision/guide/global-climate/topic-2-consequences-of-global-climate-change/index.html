2. Consequences of global climate change

IB Geography Revision Guide · Unit 2: Global Climate · Topics · Topic 2 of 3

Learning Intention

To understand the impacts of climate change on the hydrosphere, extreme weather, the biosphere, agriculture, and people and places.

Syllabus Statement

Impacts on the hydrosphere, extreme weather events, biomes, habitats and migration, agriculture (Bangladesh), and the uneven impacts on people and places.

Success criterion 1

The hydrosphere: Ocean warming and sea level rise

The oceans have absorbed over 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases. Global mean sea level has risen by roughly 20 cm since 1900, and the rate has accelerated to around 4 mm a year.

CauseHow it raises sea level
Thermal expansionWater expands as it warms, so a warmer ocean takes up more space
Melting land iceGlaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets add water to the ocean

Melting sea ice does not raise sea level much because it is already floating, but its loss lowers albedo and speeds up Arctic warming.

Success criterion 1

The hydrosphere: Ocean acidification

The oceans absorb about a quarter of human CO₂ emissions. CO₂ reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering pH. Surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1 since the industrial revolution, around a 30% increase in acidity. This makes it harder for corals, shellfish and plankton to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, threatening food webs and fisheries.

Success criterion 1

The hydrosphere: Precipitation and coastal impacts

  • A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, so the water cycle intensifies: wet areas generally get wetter and dry areas drier, with heavier downpours and longer droughts.
  • Rainfall patterns become less reliable, affecting farming and water supply.
  • Coastal impacts: more frequent flooding and storm surges, coastal erosion, saltwater intrusion into farmland and groundwater, loss of wetlands and mangroves, and threats to low-lying cities and islands.

Activity: which change?

Sort each effect by its main cause.

  • Thermal expansion of seawater
  • Coral skeletons weakening
  • Heavier monsoon downpours
  • Saltwater intrusion into delta farmland
  • Falling ocean pH
  • Longer droughts in dry regions

Success criterion 2

Extreme weather: Extreme weather: frequency and intensity

An extreme weather event is unusual in its severity for a place and time of year, for example the hottest few per cent of days on record. Frequency is how often events happen; intensity is how severe each one is. A small shift in the average climate produces a much larger change in extremes: if the whole temperature distribution shifts warmer, record-breaking hot days become far more common and very cold days rarer.

Success criterion 2

Extreme weather: Types of extreme event

EventLink to climate change
HeatwavesMore frequent, longer and hotter as average temperatures rise
DroughtsHigher evaporation and shifting rainfall make droughts longer and more severe in many regions
Tropical stormsWarmer oceans supply more energy and moisture: the proportion of the most intense storms (category 4–5) is rising, with heavier rainfall and higher storm surges on higher seas; total numbers may not increase
Heavy rainfall and floodingAbout 7% more water vapour per °C of warming produces heavier downpours and flash floods

Compound events, such as a heatwave combined with drought and wildfire, can overwhelm the ability of communities to respond.

Success criterion 2

Extreme weather: Attribution science

Attribution science uses climate models to compare the probability of an event in today’s climate with a simulated world without human emissions. It cannot say climate change “caused” a single event, but it can say how much more likely or more intense it became. Many recent heatwaves would have been extremely unlikely without human-caused warming.

Activity: frequency or intensity?

Does each statement describe a change in frequency or intensity?

  • Heatwaves now occur several times more often
  • Hurricanes produce heavier rainfall
  • Fewer very cold nights each winter
  • Droughts last longer and are more severe
  • Record-breaking hot days are more common
  • Higher storm surges during cyclones

Success criterion 3

The biosphere: Shifting biomes

A biome is a large-scale ecosystem with a distinctive climate, vegetation and animal community, such as tropical rainforest, savanna or tundra. As temperatures rise, climate zones move polewards and upslope, so biome boundaries shift: for example, shrubs and trees are spreading north into the Arctic tundra, and alpine zones are being squeezed towards mountain summits.

Success criterion 3

The biosphere: Habitats, ranges and migration

  • Habitat loss: sea ice, mountain-top and coastal wetland habitats are shrinking; species that depend on them, such as polar bears on Arctic sea ice, lose hunting grounds.
  • Range shifts: many species are moving towards the poles or to higher altitudes to stay within suitable temperatures.
  • Changing migration and timing: some birds arrive earlier in spring and insects emerge earlier. If plants, insects and birds shift at different rates, mismatches occur, for example chicks hatching after the peak in caterpillar food.
Success criterion 3

The biosphere: Coral bleaching and extinction risk

Coral bleaching happens when sea temperatures stay too high: corals expel the algae that give them food and colour, turning white. Repeated bleaching, together with acidification, can kill reefs. The Great Barrier Reef has suffered several mass bleaching events since 2016.

Species face greater extinction risk when they cannot move (island, mountain-top or reef species), have narrow temperature tolerances, reproduce slowly, or face barriers such as cities and farmland blocking their migration.

Activity: which impact?

Sort each example.

  • Fish stocks moving north in the North Sea
  • Swallows arriving earlier in spring
  • Shrinking Arctic sea ice
  • Alpine plants moving up mountain slopes
  • Insects emerging before birds’ chicks hatch
  • Bleached and dying coral reefs

Success criterion 4

Agriculture: Yields and growing seasons

Climate change has uneven effects on farming:

  • In many tropical and subtropical regions, higher temperatures, heat stress and drought reduce yields of staple crops such as wheat, maize and rice.
  • In some high-latitude regions (Canada, Russia, northern Europe) longer, warmer growing seasons may allow new crops or higher yields, at least in the short term.
  • Higher CO₂ can boost growth of some crops, but the benefit is limited by heat, water and nutrient shortages.
Success criterion 4

Agriculture: Water stress, pests and food security

  • Water stress: less reliable rainfall, longer droughts and shrinking glaciers that feed rivers reduce water for irrigation.
  • Pests and diseases spread into new areas as winters become milder, for example crop pests moving polewards.
  • Food security (reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food) is threatened as yields become less predictable and prices rise. The poorest, who spend a large share of income on food or farm for subsistence, are hit hardest.
Success criterion 4

Agriculture: Case study: Bangladesh

Bangladesh is a low-lying, densely populated delta country formed by the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers, where many people depend on farming, especially rice.

Impacts on agriculture
More intense monsoon flooding and river floods damage crops and stored food
Stronger cyclones and storm surges in the Bay of Bengal flood coastal farmland
Sea level rise causes saltwater intrusion, making soils too salty for rice in the south-west
Hotter temperatures and less reliable rainfall stress crops in the dry season
Adaptation strategies
Salt-tolerant and flood-tolerant rice varieties
Floating gardens (beds of water hyacinth) that rise with floodwater
Switching from rice to shrimp or crab farming in saline areas
Embankments, cyclone shelters and early-warning systems

  • Planting salt-tolerant rice
  • Cyclone storm surges flood farmland
  • Floating gardens on flooded land
  • Monsoon floods destroy stored grain
  • Early-warning systems and cyclone shelters

Success criterion 5

People and places: Uneven impacts and environmental migration

The impacts of climate change depend on both physical exposure (how much a place is hit by hazards) and social and economic vulnerability (how well people can cope). The countries that have contributed least to emissions are often the most vulnerable, an issue of climate justice.

Environmental migrants (sometimes called “climate refugees”) are people forced to move by environmental change such as sea level rise, drought or repeated flooding. The term “refugee” has no legal status for climate migrants under the 1951 Refugee Convention, so they have little international protection. Most move within their own country.

Success criterion 5

People and places: Health, LICs and small island states

  • Health: heat stress and deaths in heatwaves; the spread of vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue to new areas and altitudes; water-borne diseases after floods; malnutrition when harvests fail.
  • LICs are more vulnerable because more people depend on rain-fed farming, housing and infrastructure are weaker, and governments have less money for defences, health care and recovery.
  • Small island developing states (for example the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati) are very low-lying with little higher ground, so sea level rise, storm surges and saltwater intrusion threaten land, fresh water and their very existence. Bangladesh shows how a densely populated, low-lying delta faces flooding, cyclones and displacement.
Success criterion 5

People and places: Ocean transport routes

As Arctic sea ice shrinks, the Northern Sea Route (along Russia’s coast) and the Northwest Passage (through Canada’s Arctic islands) are open for longer each summer. They can shorten voyages between East Asia and Europe compared with the Suez Canal route, saving fuel and time, but they raise risks of oil spills in a fragile environment and geopolitical disputes over control.

Activity: physical exposure or vulnerability?

Sort each factor.

  • Land only one metre above sea level
  • Few savings to rebuild after a flood
  • Location on a cyclone track
  • Dependence on rain-fed farming
  • A coastline facing rising seas
  • Weak health services

Key terms

Hydrosphere
All the water on Earth, including oceans, ice, rivers and water vapour.
Thermal expansion
The increase in volume of water as it warms.
Ocean acidification
The fall in ocean pH caused by absorbing CO₂.
Saltwater intrusion
Seawater moving into freshwater aquifers or farmland.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level caused by low pressure and strong winds in a storm.
Extreme weather event
Weather that is unusually severe for a place and time of year.
Frequency
How often an event occurs.
Intensity
How severe an event is.
Attribution science
Research that estimates how climate change affected the likelihood or severity of an event.
Compound event
Several hazards occurring together or in sequence.
Biome
A large-scale ecosystem defined by climate, vegetation and animals.
Range shift
A change in the geographic area where a species lives.
Phenology
The timing of seasonal events such as flowering or migration.
Coral bleaching
Corals expelling their algae under heat stress and turning white.
Extinction risk
The likelihood that a species will die out.
Food security
Reliable access to enough safe and nutritious food.
Growing season
The part of the year when temperature and rainfall allow crops to grow.
Water stress
When demand for water exceeds the available supply.
Adaptation
Adjusting to the effects of climate change to reduce harm.
Environmental migrant
A person forced to move by environmental change.
Climate justice
The idea that those least responsible for emissions often suffer most.
Vector-borne disease
A disease spread by organisms such as mosquitoes.
SIDS
Small island developing states.
Northern Sea Route
An Arctic shipping route along Russia’s northern coast.
